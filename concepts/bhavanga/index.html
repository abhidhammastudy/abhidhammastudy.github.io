Life-continuum

bhavaṅga

The quiet stream of mind that runs whenever nothing is actively being known, as in dreamless sleep.

In brief

  • The life-continuum (bhavaṅga) is the quiet stream of mind that runs whenever nothing is actively being known, as in dreamless sleep.
  • It is a result of past kamma, the same kind of mind-moment that begins a life at rebirth and ends it at death.
  • When an object strikes a door, the life-continuum vibrates and is arrested, a process of knowing runs, and the mind sinks back into it.
  • It is not a blank, a storehouse of memories or a self, but a series of moments, each arising and passing away.
  • It shows how a life can stay continuous without any lasting subject behind it.

What it is

Bhavaṅgabhavaṅga · life-continuum the resultant citta, of the same kind as rebirth-linking, that runs between processes, literally “factor of becoming” and usually rendered life-continuum, is the resultant citta (one produced by past kamma) that keeps a life continuous whenever no active process is running, as in dreamless sleep. It is the same kind of citta as rebirth-linking (paṭisandhipaṭisandhi · rebirth-linking the first citta of a life: a resultant that links it to the last) and death (cuticuti · death consciousness the last citta of a life, of the same kind as its life-continuum), and it takes the same object, the one presented at the end of the previous life. It is not a soul or a hidden self: it arises and dissolves like any other citta. It shows how continuity is possible without an enduring subject.

In depth

How the Manual presents it

The life-continuum is one of the fourteen functions of citta (III §8). Nineteen resultant cittas can perform it: the two investigating cittas with equanimity, for a rootless rebirth; the eight great resultants; and the nine sublime resultants (III §9–10). In any one life a single type does three jobs. It is rebirth-linking at the start, life-continuum in between and death at the end, and it always takes the object of the last process of the previous life.

V §41 describes the continuity directly. From the moment after rebirth-linking, that same citta with that same object runs on unbroken like a river current whenever no process is running, until it becomes death consciousness. Then rebirth-linking, life-continuum and death keep rolling round like a cartwheel. The Guide adds that sixteen moments of life-continuum follow rebirth-linking. Then comes the first process of the new life, at the mind door: seven greed-rooted javanas without wrong view, which take the rebirth-linking citta as object and so cling to the new existence (bhava-nikanti; Guide to V §41).

Its place in the process

When an object strikes a door, three moments of life-continuum are named (IV §6). The first is past life-continuum (atīta-bhavaṅgaatīta-bhavaṅga · past life-continuum the life-continuum moment during which the object arises, before it registers at the door; counted first of the 17), during which the object is present but not yet felt. The second is vibration (bhavaṅga-calanabhavaṅga-calana · vibration of the life-continuum the life-continuum moment that is shaken when an object comes into range; the next is the arrest). The third is arrest (bhavaṅgupacchedabhavaṅgupaccheda · arrest of the life-continuum the last life-continuum moment before a process; after it the stream stops and adverting arises). Then adverting turns the mind toward the object. With a very slight object nothing happens beyond the vibration (IV §9). Every process ends by sinking back into the life-continuum.

One special case shows the rules at work. Joy and displeasure cannot follow each other directly. So when javanas of displeasure end, in someone whose life-continuum is joyful, and no registration follows, the commentarial teachers say that an equanimous investigating citta arises once as a buffer before the return. This is the "adventitious life-continuum" (IV §18).

Sources and the commentarial picture

The term appears in the Paṭṭhāna and the Milindapañha. The full theory belongs to the commentaries and the Manual. In the mango simile, the man asleep under the tree is the life-continuum (Atthasālinī). The Atthasālinī's version ends with the eating; later tradition added his falling asleep again, for the return to the life-continuum (note to IV §6).

The Aṅguttara commentary identifies the "luminous mind" of AN 1.51–52 with the life-continuum. That identification is commentarial, not the sutta's own.

Common misunderstandings

It is not a blank. It is a citta with an object, only not a fresh one, which is why nothing seems to be known. Nor is it a storehouse. The Abhidhamma keeps no memories or drives "in" the life-continuum: latent tendencies and kamma are carried by the continuum of cittas as a whole, as conditions.

It does no kamma: it is resultant, every moment of it. And it is not a self, since it arises and dissolves like every citta. See the readings The mango and the moment and Kamma, death and rebirth.

Stories that show it

Rāhula and the mirror

The Buddha's life · MN 61

The Buddha asked his young son Rāhula what a mirror is for. “For reflection, sir.” “In the same way,” said the Buddha, “reflect before, during and after every action of body, speech and mind: does it lead to harm for myself, for others, or both?”

Reflection is a discipline for the javanas, where volition becomes kamma (“The mango and the moment” and “Kamma, death and rebirth”).

The last line of every Jātaka

Jātaka · Jātaka, closing identification

Each Jātaka ends by naming who was who: “At that time, so-and-so was the king, and I myself was the ascetic.” One continuity is followed across many lives as if it were one person.

Conventional truth at work (“A chariot of parts”). Ultimately there is only a stream of cittas and matter, each moment conditioning the next. “The Bodhisatta” names the stream: in Nāgasena's words to King Milinda, neither the same person, nor a different one.

Khantivādi, the teacher of patience

Jātaka · Ja 313

A drunken king, Kalābu, asked an ascetic what he taught. “Patience,” he said. To test him, the king had his hands, feet, nose and ears cut off. The ascetic felt no anger and wished the king long life. Leaving, the king was swallowed by the earth.

“An ensemble of fifty-two” at its sharpest: non-hatred (adosaadosa · non-hatred Non-hatred is the mind's not opposing its object: friendliness and patience, not only the absence of ill will.) and hatred (dosadosa · hatred aversion, from irritation to rage; root of two cittas, always with displeasure) never share a citta. The king's fate returns in “Kamma, death and rebirth” as kamma that ripens in this very life.

How a story is read here is a study aid, not the tradition's own reading.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

In dreamless sleep the life-continuum flows unbroken. Does that make it the self that lasts?

One way to see it, after you have tried

No. It is a series of cittas, each arising and ceasing, all of one kind because one kamma produced them. What continues is a stream, as a river continues; no thing stays the same in it. It is also a result: nobody chooses it.
