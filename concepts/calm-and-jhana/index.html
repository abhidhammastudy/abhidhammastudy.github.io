Calm

samatha

Training the mind to rest on one object, from access concentration to the five jhānas, the meditative absorptions.

In brief

  • Calm (samatha) is the training of the mind to rest on one object until it becomes unified and steady.
  • The Manual lists forty meditation subjects, suited to different temperaments; some lead only to access, the stage just short of absorption, others to the full absorptions called jhāna.
  • In jhāna the mind is held by a few factors, such as zest, happiness and one-pointedness, which grow fewer and finer at each higher level.
  • Jhāna holds down the five hindrances, such as sensual desire and ill will, but it does not uproot them.
  • Its value on the way out is a mind unified enough to see clearly, so calm serves as the basis for insight, while only the path uproots.

What it is

Calm (samathasamatha · calm meditation that develops concentration, up to the jhānas) is the training of one-pointedness (ekaggatāekaggatā · one-pointedness unifies the mind on its object; when strong it is concentration (samādhi)) on a meditation subject until the five hindrances (sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, doubt) are suppressed and the mind enters absorption (appanāappanā · absorption the jhāna or path javana itself, after access; the third stage of concentration) as jhāna. Jhāna is a sublime (mahaggata) state in which the jhāna factors (initial and sustained application, zest, happiness, one-pointedness) are refined step by step. Calm suppresses the defilements but does not uproot them; it serves as the basis (pādaka) for insight.

In a picture

The toddler and the strong man

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga IV, CS §58

The difference between access concentration and full absorption (jhāna).

Lift a small child and stand it on its feet, and it falls down again and again. A strong man can get up from his seat and stand all day long.

The Visuddhimagga says concentration grows like that. In the stage called access, the factors of jhāna have arisen but are not yet strong. The mind takes the meditation sign as its object for a while, then drops back into the life-continuum, the mind's resting state, then takes up the sign again: the child standing and falling.

In absorption the factors are strong. Once the mind has cut through the life-continuum, it can go on in one unbroken run of wholesome moments for a whole night or a whole day: the strong man standing.

Where the picture stops working: The strong man fits absorption once it is mastered: in a beginner's first absorption the jhāna javana runs only once before the mind falls back into the life-continuum, and only with practice does the run grow longer (CMA IV §22 and Guide). And the man stands by his own strength, while the run of javanas is held up by no one: each javana is a condition for the next by repetition (CMA VIII §13).

Told in: Visuddhimagga IV (the earth kasiṇa), CS §58; CMA IV §14 and Guide: the absorption process; IV §22 and Guide: javana in attainments; IX §§17–18: access and absorption

The muddy lake and the clear lake

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · AN 1.45–46

Only a mind that is not clouded can see. Purification of mind is for the sake of seeing.

Suppose a pool of water is cloudy, stirred up and muddy. A man with good eyes stands on the bank, but he cannot see the shells, the gravel and pebbles, or the shoals of fish, moving or at rest, because the water is clouded. In the same way, the Buddha said, a monk whose mind is clouded cannot know his own good, or the good of others, or of both, or reach any knowledge and vision beyond the ordinary that is fit for the noble ones.

Suppose instead the pool is clear, settled and unclouded. The same man on the bank sees the shells, the gravel and pebbles, and the fish, moving or at rest. So a monk whose mind is unclouded can know his own good and others', and reach that knowledge and vision.

The commentary on the Aṅguttara says what clouds the mind: it is wrapped in the five hindrances; the clear mind is one freed from them. The knowledge and vision fit for the noble ones it takes to be the divine eye, insight knowledge, the path and fruition, and reviewing. The Vibhāvinī, the commentary on the Manual, defines purification of mind in the same terms: it is so called because it cleans the mind by freeing it of the hindrances.

Where the picture stops working: Water clears if it is left alone; a mind is cleared by effort, and by the right kind of attention. Nor does the picture say how clear is clear enough. The Manual makes purification of mind access or absorption concentration (CMA IX §29); the Guide adds that for one who takes the vehicle of pure insight it is momentary concentration, steady enough to count as access. Tying the two lakes to this purification is the site's reading, through the Vibhāvinī's definition; neither the sutta nor its commentary speaks of the purifications. And a clear lake shows what is there only to someone who looks: the seeing is the work of the purifications that follow (CMA IX §§30–34).

Told in: AN 1.45 and 1.46, the clouded and the clear pool (an1.45:1.1–1.6, an1.46:1.1–1.6); Manorathapūraṇī (commentary to the Aṅguttara) on AN 1.45–46, CS §§45–46; Vibhāvinī on CMA IX §29 (CS §50): purification of mind as freeing the mind of the hindrances; CMA IX §29 and Guide: purification of mind

In depth

How the Manual presents it

The Manual lists forty subjects (IX §2–12): ten kasiṇas, ten kinds of foulness, ten recollections, four illimitables, one perception (the repulsiveness of food), one analysis (of the four elements) and four immaterial states. It matches them to six temperaments (IX §3, §13). Foulness and the parts of the body suit the lustful. The illimitables and the four colour kasiṇas suit the hating. Breathing suits the deluded and the discursive. The first six recollections suit the faithful. Death, peace, food and the elements suit the intellectual. The rest suit every temperament.

Stages, signs and reach

Development has three stages: preliminary, access and absorption. Its objects have three signs: preliminary, learning and counterpart (IX §14–17). The counterpart sign is a concept born of meditation, free of the original object's flaws. It arises in only twenty-two subjects: the kasiṇas, foulness, the parts of the body and breathing (IX §16, §18). Ten subjects reach only access, never absorption: eight recollections, the perception of food and the analysis of elements (IX §14).

The subjects differ in how far they can take the mind (IX §15). The kasiṇas and breathing reach all five jhānas. Foulness and the body reach only the first, because they need initial application. The first three illimitables reach four, since they are joyful. The fourth illimitable, equanimity, reaches only the fifth. After the first jhāna come the five masteries: adverting, attaining, resolving, emerging and reviewing (IX §18). The immaterial attainments follow from a kasiṇa removed to leave space (IX §19). The direct knowledges come from the fifth jhāna (IX §21).

Jhāna factors and hindrances

The first jhāna has five factors: initial application, sustained application, zest, happiness and one-pointedness. In the Abhidhamma's fivefold scheme they drop away one at a time, and in the fifth jhāna equanimity replaces happiness. The suttas' fourfold scheme drops both kinds of thought at once. The commentaries pair each factor with the hindrance it holds down (Guide to I §§18–20; Vism IV). One-pointedness checks sensual desire, zest checks ill will, initial application checks sloth and torpor, happiness checks restlessness and worry, and sustained application checks doubt.

In the process, the first absorption of a beginner lasts a single javana, after three or four sense-sphere moments of preparation, access, conformity and change-of-lineage (IV §14, §22). With mastery, the jhāna javanas run on in long succession.

Calm and insight

Jhāna suppresses the hindrances; it does not uproot them. It is weighty kamma, and it leads to rebirth in the Brahmā worlds. Access and absorption together make up purification of mind (IX §29), the ground on which insight builds; for one who goes by dry insight, momentary concentration, as steady as access, counts instead (Guide to IX §29). Calm may rest on a concept, but insight must turn to the conditioned dhammas themselves. The commentaries distinguish one who goes by the vehicle of calm from the practitioner of dry insight, who reaches the path with only the concentration that insight itself brings (Guide to IX §29; Vism XVIII). Even so, the path citta is always counted at some level of jhāna, the first for the practitioner of dry insight (Guide to I §§30–31).     Stories that show it

    The monkey king's bridge

    Jātaka · Ja 407

    To save his troop from archers, a monkey king stretched himself across a river as a living bridge. The troop crossed over his back, but a jealous monkey leapt down on him, and his heart burst. The king of Benares, moved, tended him as he died.

    Compassion (karuṇākaruṇā · compassion Compassion is the wish that suffering be removed from others.) is one of the variable beautiful factors in “An ensemble of fifty-two”. It joins a wholesome citta only now and then, when the suffering of others is the object. The Jātaka's own lesson is for rulers: one who leads should seek the good of all who depend on him.

    Pukkusāti and the elements

    Disciples · MN 140

    One night in a potter's shed, the Buddha taught a stranger, Pukkusāti, to see his body as earth, water, fire, air, space and consciousness, none of it “mine”. Pukkusāti recognised his teacher, but while seeking robes for ordination he was killed by a cow. The Buddha declared him a non-returner.

    The four great elements are the core of the 28 kinds of matter in “How matter is made”. Analysing the body into elements is also one of the subjects in “Forty meditation subjects”.

    The rose-apple tree

    The Buddha's life · MN 36

    As a boy, the Bodhisatta sat in the shade of a rose-apple tree while his father was at work (at the ploughing festival, the commentaries say), and entered the first jhāna. Years later, worn out by austerities, he remembered it and asked: “Could that be the path to awakening?”

    On the tree in “The tree of purification”, concentration is a root. The commentaries say his subject was the breath, one of the eleven in “Forty meditation subjects” that can reach all five jhānas.

    Reflect and discuss

    Opening question

    Jhāna suppresses the hindrances but does not uproot them. What is it good for on the way out?

    One way to see it, after you have tried

    It gives a mind unified enough to see clearly, and a happiness that needs no sense object. It is a basis for insight. Only the knowledge of the path uproots.
