Cetasika

mental factors

The mental factors that colour each citta.

In brief

  • Mental factors (cetasika) are the qualities that arise with each moment of consciousness and give it its character.
  • Each factor arises and ceases together with its moment of consciousness, takes the same object and rests on the same base.
  • The Abhidhamma counts fifty-two: seven present in every moment, six that come only sometimes, fourteen unwholesome and twenty-five beautiful.
  • Opposed factors never meet in one moment, and a moment's ethical quality is settled above all by its roots: greed, hatred, delusion or their opposites.
  • Volition is present in every moment, but it makes kamma only in wholesome and unwholesome ones.

What it is

Mental factors are the qualities that arise with consciousness and give it its character. Each is a dhamma in its own right, a real quality with its own mark. They are joined to citta by four marks of association (sampayutta-lakkhaṇa): they arise together, cease together, share one object and one base. Fifty-two are counted: seven universals (sabbacitta-sādhāraṇasabbacittasādhāraṇa · universals the seven factors present in every citta, from contact to attention), six occasionals (pakiṇṇakapakiṇṇaka · occasionals six ethically variable factors that join some cittas but not all), fourteen unwholesome and twenty-five beautiful (sobhanasobhana · beautiful the 59 cittas other than unwholesome and rootless ones, and their 25 factors). The ethical quality of a moment is decided by its factors, above all by its roots (hetuhetu · root one of six factors that steady a citta: greed, hatred, delusion, or their opposites): greed, hatred and delusion, or their opposites.

In depth

How the Manual presents it

A mental factor is defined by four marks of association: it arises with citta, ceases with it, takes the same object and has the same base (II §1). The Manual sorts the 52 into three classes: 13 ethically variable, 14 unwholesome and 25 beautiful (II §2–9). The first class divides into 7 universals and 6 occasionals, which gives the four groups used across this site: universals, occasionals, unwholesome and beautiful.

The ethically variable factors (aññasamāna) number 13. Seven are universal: contact, feeling, perception, volition, one-pointedness, life faculty and attention. Six are occasional: initial application, sustained application, decision, energy, zest and desire-to-act.

The unwholesome factors number 14. Four are present in every unwholesome citta: delusion, shamelessness, fearlessness of wrongdoing and restlessness. Then come the greed group (greed, wrong view, conceit), the hatred group (hatred, envy, avarice, worry), sloth and torpor, and doubt.

The beautiful factors number 25. Nineteen are present in every beautiful citta: among them faith, mindfulness, shame, fear of wrongdoing, non-greed, non-hatred, neutrality of mind, and six pairs (tranquillity, lightness, malleability, wieldiness, proficiency and rectitude, each of the mental body and of consciousness). Then come the three abstinences, the two illimitables (compassion and appreciative joy) and wisdom.

Two ways of reading the list

The Manual reads the list from both sides. The method of association (II §10–17) takes each factor and asks which cittas it joins. The method of combination (II §18–29) takes each citta and asks which factors it holds. The extremes show the range. Eye-consciousness has only the seven universals, while the first sense-sphere wholesome citta can hold 38 (II §23, Table 2.3).

Eleven factors are "unfixed" (II §17): envy, avarice, worry, the three abstinences, compassion, appreciative joy, conceit, and sloth and torpor. They arise only sometimes in the cittas they belong to. The illimitables and the abstinences, when they arise, come one at a time. In path and fruition, by contrast, the three abstinences are always present together, as right speech, action and livelihood (II §15). Eight of the 52 are the factors of the path; see the concept The noble eightfold path.

The commentarial picture

The commentaries describe each factor by its characteristic, function, manifestation and proximate cause, the scheme the Guide uses for every entry. Their similes fix one property at a time. Initial application strikes the object like a stick on a bell; sustained application is the ringing that goes on (Vism IV). Volition is like a senior pupil who says his own lesson and sets the others to saying theirs. Feeling is the king who sits down to the meal; the other factors are like the cook, who gets no more than a taste while making it. The Guide gives both similes (Guide to II §2), and they go back to the Atthasālinī and Vism XIV. See the reading The commentators' picture-book.

Distinctions that matter

Feeling and perception are each a single factor, and each is an aggregate of its own. The aggregate of formations is the other fifty. Volition is in every citta, results included. It becomes kamma only in wholesome and unwholesome cittas, and these always occur as javanas (III §9).

Opposed factors never meet in one citta: greed and non-greed, or wrong view and wisdom. The thirteen variables take their colour from the company they keep. The ethical quality of a citta is settled by its roots, the six of III §5. For more, see the readings An ensemble of fifty-two and Which factors meet which cittas.

Stories that show it

Kisāgotamī and the mustard seed

Disciples · Dhammapada commentary, v. 114

Mad with grief, Kisāgotamī carried her dead child from house to house asking for medicine. The Buddha asked her for a mustard seed from a house where no one had died. She found none, laid the child down, and understood that death comes to all.

Her grief is displeasure (domanassadomanassa · displeasure painful mental feeling; only in the two hate-rooted cittas), which in “A map of the eighty-nine” occurs only in the two hate-rooted cittas: grief is aversion toward loss. Its soil is attachment (“from the dear comes sorrow”, Dhp 212), and the commentary says her heart, soft with love for her son, had to grow firm before she could let him go. The search turned a private wound into a universal fact, and the verse on death turned that fact into the path.

Khantivādi, the teacher of patience

Jātaka · Ja 313

A drunken king, Kalābu, asked an ascetic what he taught. “Patience,” he said. To test him, the king had his hands, feet, nose and ears cut off. The ascetic felt no anger and wished the king long life. Leaving, the king was swallowed by the earth.

“An ensemble of fifty-two” at its sharpest: non-hatred (adosaadosa · non-hatred Non-hatred is the mind's not opposing its object: friendliness and patience, not only the absence of ill will.) and hatred (dosadosa · hatred aversion, from irritation to rage; root of two cittas, always with displeasure) never share a citta. The king's fate returns in “Kamma, death and rebirth” as kamma that ripens in this very life.

The monkey king's bridge

Jātaka · Ja 407

To save his troop from archers, a monkey king stretched himself across a river as a living bridge. The troop crossed over his back, but a jealous monkey leapt down on him, and his heart burst. The king of Benares, moved, tended him as he died.

Compassion (karuṇākaruṇā · compassion Compassion is the wish that suffering be removed from others.) is one of the variable beautiful factors in “An ensemble of fifty-two”. It joins a wholesome citta only now and then, when the suffering of others is the object. The Jātaka's own lesson is for rulers: one who leads should seek the good of all who depend on him.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Contact, feeling, perception, volition and attention arise with every citta. What would a moment without volition be like, and why can there be none?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Every citta has an object and gathers its factors toward it. Volition (cetanā) is that gathering, so a citta without it would not hold together. Even in resultant cittas volition is present; there it makes no new kamma.
