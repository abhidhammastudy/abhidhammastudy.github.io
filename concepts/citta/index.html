Citta

consciousness

Consciousness: one moment of knowing an object. The Abhidhamma counts 89 kinds.

In brief

  • Consciousness (citta) is one moment of knowing an object, and it never arises without its mental factors.
  • Each moment arises, stands and dissolves, conditioning the next as it goes, so mind is a stream of events and not a substance.
  • The Abhidhamma counts 89 kinds of consciousness, sorted by plane, by roots, by feeling, and as wholesome, unwholesome, resultant or functional.
  • These are kinds, not 89 minds housed in a person: the moments arise one at a time.
  • Counting kinds of knowing leaves no separate knower behind the knowing, and no owner behind the stream.

What it is

Consciousness is the bare knowing of an object (ārammaṇa-vijānana); in the Dhammapada’s words, mind is the forerunner (pubbaṅgamapubbaṅgama · forerunner what goes before: the mind as forerunner of states, as in the first verse of the Dhammapada) of states. It never arises without its mental factors, and it never lasts: each citta arises, stands and dissolves in three sub-moments (uppāda, ṭhiti, bhaṅga), and conditions the next as it goes. The 89 kinds are sorted by plane (bhūmibhūmi · plane one of 31 planes of existence, each matched to a kind of mind), by kind (jātijāti · birth the arising of the aggregates in a new existence; the eleventh link, and the characteristic of matter called production: wholesome, unwholesome, resultant, functional), by roots and by feeling. Mind here is not a substance but a continuity (santatisantati · continuity Continuity: the renewed arising of matter in a series.) of such events, with no owner behind them.

In depth

How the Manual presents it

Chapter I sorts consciousness first by plane (I §3): 54 sense-sphere cittas, 15 fine-material, 12 immaterial and 8 supramundane, 89 in all.

The 54 break down into 12 unwholesome (8 rooted in greed, 2 in hatred, 2 in delusion; I §4–7), 18 rootless (7 unwholesome-resultant, 8 wholesome-resultant, 3 functional; I §8–11) and 24 sense-sphere beautiful (8 wholesome, 8 resultant, 8 functional; I §12–16). The fine-material cittas are five jhānas, each wholesome, resultant or functional. The immaterial ones are four, in the same three kinds. The supramundane are four paths and four fruitions. If each path and fruition is counted at each of the five jhānas, the 8 become 40 and the total is 121 (I §30–31).

By kind (jātijāti · birth the arising of the aggregates in a new existence; the eleventh link, and the characteristic of matter called production) the 89 are 21 wholesome, 12 unwholesome, 36 resultant and 20 functional.

What makes one citta differ from another

Each type is fixed by a handful of variables. They are its plane; its kind; its roots, none, one, two or three of the six (III §5–7); and its feeling, which is joy, equanimity, displeasure, bodily pleasure or bodily pain (III §2–4). For the greed- and hate-rooted cittas, prompting (and for greed, wrong view) count too; for the sense-sphere beautiful cittas, knowledge and prompting: whether the citta is prompted (sasaṅkhārikasasaṅkhārika · prompted arising after urging or persuasion, by oneself or by another) or spontaneous (I §§4–5, 13).

Chapter III adds what a citta does and where it stands. There are 14 functions, performed at 10 stations of the process (III §8–11), and further distinctions by door and by base (III §12–21). Some facts follow from these variables. Displeasure occurs only in the two cittas rooted in hatred. Bodily pain occurs only in one unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness. And the ten sense consciousnesses, eye-consciousness among them, carry only the seven universal factors, the barest cittas there are (II §28).

The commentarial picture

The commentators define citta three ways (Guide to I §3). As agent, it is what cognizes an object. As instrument, it is that by which the factors cognize. As activity, it is just the cognizing. The third is the most exact, because it leaves no agent standing behind the act. In the four-part definition the commentaries use for every dhamma, citta's characteristic is knowing an object, its function is to go before (pubbaṅgamapubbaṅgama · forerunner what goes before: the mind as forerunner of states, as in the first verse of the Dhammapada), it shows itself as continuity, and its proximate cause is mentality-materiality.

The Milindapañha gives the image for continuity. A lamp burns all night: the flame of the last watch is neither the flame of the first nor a different one (Milindapañha 3.2.1). The Guide to II §1 gives the image for company. Citta is like a king who never travels without his retinue, the mental factors.

Common misunderstandings

The 89 are kinds, not 89 minds housed in a person. Cittas arise one at a time, each in three sub-moments, and a single unit of matter lasts as long as seventeen of them (IV §6). "Beautiful" is not the same as "wholesome": the 24 sense-sphere beautiful cittas include resultants and the arahant's functionals. "Resultant" does not mean unimportant. Seeing, hearing, rebirth-linking and the life-continuum are all results, the places where past kamma is felt.

Stories that show it

Kisāgotamī and the mustard seed

Disciples · Dhammapada commentary, v. 114

Mad with grief, Kisāgotamī carried her dead child from house to house asking for medicine. The Buddha asked her for a mustard seed from a house where no one had died. She found none, laid the child down, and understood that death comes to all.

Her grief is displeasure (domanassadomanassa · displeasure painful mental feeling; only in the two hate-rooted cittas), which in “A map of the eighty-nine” occurs only in the two hate-rooted cittas: grief is aversion toward loss. Its soil is attachment (“from the dear comes sorrow”, Dhp 212), and the commentary says her heart, soft with love for her son, had to grow firm before she could let him go. The search turned a private wound into a universal fact, and the verse on death turned that fact into the path.

Why Moggallāna smiled

Disciples · SN 19.1

Coming down Vulture Peak, Mahā Moggallāna smiled. Asked why, he said he had seen a skeleton moving through the air, pursued by crows and vultures. The Buddha confirmed that he too had seen such beings, and named the deeds that led there.

An arahant smiles with one of five cittas, none of which makes kamma: the smile-producing consciousness found only in arahants (in “A map of the eighty-nine”), or one of the four joyful great functional cittas. What he saw belongs to the woeful planes of “Thirty-one planes”.

Dhammadinnā on feeling

Disciples · MN 44

Questioned by the layman Visākha, the nun Dhammadinnā explained that lust lies latent in pleasant feeling, aversion in painful feeling and ignorance in neutral feeling. The Buddha said he would have answered in the same way.

This joins the colours of feeling in “A map of the eighty-nine” to the wheel in “The wheel across three lives”: feeling (link 7) is where craving (link 8) takes hold.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The Abhidhamma counts 89 kinds of citta. What is gained by counting minds instead of describing a mind?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Counting puts kinds of knowing where a single knower was assumed. Each kind is fixed by its roots, feeling, plane and function, so the mind can be watched in parts. And no knower is left over apart from the knowing.
