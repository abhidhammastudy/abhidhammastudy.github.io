Cognitive process

17 mind-moments

The fixed sequence in which cittas take an object.

In brief

  • When you see a sight or hear a sound, the Abhidhamma holds, the knowing happens as a fixed series of moments of consciousness.
  • An object strikes a sense door, the life-continuum vibrates and is arrested, then adverting, seeing, receiving, investigating and determining follow in order.
  • Then come the javanas, usually seven moments that run over the object; here, except in an arahant, new kamma is made.
  • In the commentaries and the Manual, a full process with a vivid object runs seventeen moments, the lifetime of a sense object, and weaker objects give shorter processes.
  • A sense-door process knows only a bare patch of colour or a sound; recognising a thing and its name takes many later processes at the mind door.
  • Nobody sits behind the process steering it; each moment does its one job and conditions the next.

What it is

When you see a sight or hear a sound, the Abhidhamma holds, the knowing happens as a fixed series of cittas, the order of mind (citta-niyāma). An object strikes a door, the life-continuum vibrates and is arrested, and adverting, sense consciousness, receiving, investigating, determining, javana (impulsion, where kamma is made, except in an arahant) and registration follow, each conditioning the next by proximity (anantaraanantara · proximity Each citta, as it ceases, makes way for the next and determines what kind can follow, so the stream of mind has no gaps.). Nobody directs the sequence; it is a lawful succession of impersonal events. Knowing a whole thing takes many such processes, at the five sense doors and at the mind door, in turn.

In a picture

The deaf doorkeeper and the king's gift

From the commentaries · Atthasālinī, the commentary on the Dhammasaṅgaṇī, CS §498

In a cognitive process each moment does one small job and passes the object on. Only the javana moments experience its taste.

A king lies asleep in his chamber. A servant sits rubbing his feet. At the door stands a doorkeeper who is deaf, and three attendants stand in a row.

A man from the borderland comes with a gift and knocks. The deaf doorkeeper hears nothing. The servant rubbing the king's feet gives him a sign, and the doorkeeper opens the door and looks. The gift is taken by the first attendant, who passes it to the second, who passes it to the third, who hands it to the king. The king enjoys it.

The Atthasālinī, the commentary on the first book of the Abhidhamma, gives the key. The knock is the object striking the eye's sensitive matter. The servant's sign is the adverting moment, which turns the life-continuum, the mind's resting state, towards the object. The deaf doorkeeper opening and looking is eye-consciousness: it sees, and does nothing else. The three attendants are receiving, investigating and determining. The king enjoying the gift is javana: only javana experiences the taste of the object.

Where the picture stops working: A king at the end of a line of servants suggests someone in charge, a self who finally gets the gift. There is no one: each moment arises from its conditions, among them the moment before, does its work and passes away (CMA IV §6 and Guide). The picture also stops at the king; the two registration moments that can follow javana are not in it. Nor does it speak of kamma. That javana is where wholesome and unwholesome kamma is made comes from elsewhere in the Manual (Guide to CMA III §8); in an arahant the javanas are only functional and make none (CMA III §13 and Guide).

In depth

How the Manual presents it

Chapter IV begins with six sixes (IV §2): six bases, six doors, six objects, six kinds of consciousness, six processes, and a sixfold way objects are presented. The processes are grouped into five-door processes and mind-door processes.

The model case is a "very great" object at a sense door, which runs to seventeen mind-moments (IV §6). There is one moment of past life-continuum, then one of vibration and one of arrest. Then come five-door adverting, the sense consciousness (seeing, say), receiving, investigating and determining. Seven javanas follow, then two moments of registration.

The count matters, because a sense object lives exactly seventeen mind-moments. If it strikes after more moments of life-continuum have passed, the process is cut short. With a great object registration drops out (IV §7). With a slight object there are no javanas, and determining repeats two or three times (IV §8). With a very slight one there is only vibration (IV §9). These four grades give the four courses at the five doors (IV §10). At the mind door the Manual has two grades, clear and obscure (IV §12); Ledi Sayadaw adds two more, making four (Guide to IV §12). The absorption process of jhāna and path has its own form (IV §14).

The rules that govern it

The Guide to IV §6 calls this the fixed order of consciousness (citta-niyāma). Each citta arises in a lawful sequence, linked to the next by proximity and contiguity, and none is skipped or chosen. Further rules are set out in IV §17–22. Registration occurs only after sense-sphere javanas, in sense-sphere beings, with vivid objects. Its feeling must be compatible with the javana's. Javanas normally run seven times, six with a very weak object, and five near death (IV §21).

Only the javanas vary in ethical quality. Everything from adverting to determining, and registration, is resultant or functional.

How a thing gets known

A five-door process knows only a present sense datum: a patch of colour, a sound. Recognition comes later, at the mind door. Following Ledi Sayadaw, the Guide to IV §12 lays out the sequel in seven steps. First a mind-door process repeats the object just past. Then one grasps it as a whole, the way a whirled firebrand looks like a circle. Then one recognises the colour, one grasps the thing, one recognises the thing, one grasps the name and one recognises the name. Together they may run to many thousands of processes, Ledi Sayadaw says. This sequence is the account of a later commentator, not of the Manual's own text. Ledi Sayadaw's Paramatthadīpanī gives both these seven and, more briefly, four kinds of grasping that follow each sense door: of the past object, of the whole, of the thing and of the name.

The commentarial picture and misunderstandings

The mango simile (Atthasālinī; Guide to IV §6) maps the stages. A man asleep is struck by a falling mango and wakes. He looks, picks it up, squeezes it, smells it and eats it. Seeing is the looking, receiving the picking up, investigating the squeezing, determining the smelling, javana the eating. The Atthasālinī stops there. The Vibhāvinī adds the swallowing for registration and his falling asleep again for the return to the life-continuum (note to IV §6).

Nobody sits behind the process watching or steering it, the Guide insists. Each citta does its one job and hands on to the next; whatever wholeness the act of knowing has comes from the way they condition one another (Guide to IV §6). Nor does a person live through one long process. Knowing a single thing takes many processes, alternating between the doors faster than anyone can notice. The seventeen-moment process belongs to the commentaries and the Manual, not to the suttas. See the readings The mango and the moment, Other processes and Who has which cittas.

Stories that show it

Bāhiya: in the seen, only the seen

Disciples · Ud 1.10

Bāhiya stopped the Buddha on his alms round, begging to be taught. The Buddha said: “In the seen there will be only the seen, in the heard only the heard, in the sensed only the sensed, in the cognized only the cognized.” Bāhiya became an arahant there and then, and was killed by a cow soon after.

“The mango and the moment” shows where the instruction bites, and the Udāna commentary reads it the same way: let the javanas stay at the measure of eye-consciousness, which neither lusts, hates nor is deluded. Seeing itself, at moment 5, is a result and makes no kamma. Liking, disliking and “I” enter only in the javanas.

Nanda, guardian of the doors

Disciples · Ud 3.2; AN 8.9; AN 1.230

The Buddha's half-brother Nanda was ordained on his wedding day and pined for his bride. In time he became an arahant, and the Buddha named him foremost among those who guard the sense doors: whichever way Nanda looked, he looked with full awareness, so that longing and grief could not flow in.

The Buddha met Nanda's desire where it was and led it past itself: once free, Nanda wanted nothing of the promise. The doors he then learned to guard are the doors of “The mango and the moment”. Guarding them means the seeing is not followed by unwholesome javanas.

Makhādeva's first grey hair

Jātaka · Ja 9

King Makhādeva told his barber to report the first grey hair. When it came, the king held it in his hand, saw death approaching, handed the kingdom to his son and went forth.

A small object can carry a large process: the same grey hair can stir vanity or urgency (saṃvega), depending on how it is attended to (“The mango and the moment”). MN 83 adds the Buddha's own point: Makhādeva's good custom led to the Brahmā world; the custom he himself founded, the noble eightfold path, leads to Nibbāna.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

If seeing is over before you know you are seeing, what is the "I see" that follows?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Later processes at the mind door take the earlier one as their object, in seven steps. One repeats the colour just past, one grasps it as a whole, one recognises the colour, one grasps the thing, one recognises the thing, one grasps the name and one recognises the name, "mango" (Guide to IV §12, following Ledi Sayadaw). Only in these recognising processes, on Ledi Sayadaw's account, does one know "I see" this or that. The "I see" is built out of material the seeing itself did not contain.
