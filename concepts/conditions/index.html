24 conditions

Paṭṭhāna

The ways in which one dhamma supports another.

In brief

  • The Paṭṭhāna, a canonical book of the Abhidhamma, names twenty-four ways in which one thing helps another to arise or to last.
  • They include root, object, support and kamma, and the Manual gathers all twenty-four under four master conditions.
  • Mind conditions mind, mind and matter condition each other, and concepts and even Nibbāna can condition mind, each in set ways.
  • A condition need not be present: a moment that has just ceased, or kamma done long ago, can still give support.
  • Nothing arises from a single cause, from no cause or from a first cause; in their place stands a web of mutual support.
  • Each named condition marks where change can begin: what one attends to, whom one keeps company with, what one repeats.

What it is

The Paṭṭhāna analyses every relation between dhammas into twenty-four modes: root, object, predominance, proximity, conascence, mutuality, support, decisive support, kamma, nutriment and the rest. A condition (paccayapaccaya · condition a dhamma that helps another to arise or persist, in a specific way) is a dhamma that helps another, the conditioned (paccayuppannapaccayuppanna · conditioned state the dhamma that a condition helps to arise or persist), to arise or to persist. The scheme puts a web of mutual support where other systems put a creator or a first cause: nothing arises from a single cause, and nothing from no cause. A moment of seeing, for example, depends at once on the eye as its support, the visible form as its object, and the citta just before it by proximity.

In a picture

The king who wanted the sound of the lute

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · SN 35.246

What looks like one thing is many parts working together. Searched down to the ash, the lute gives up no sound to be found anywhere in it; the monk who searches his aggregates the same way is left with no "I", "mine" or "I am".

A king who had never heard a lute heard one played and was enchanted. "What is that sound?" he asked. "That is a lute, Your Majesty." "Bring it to me." They brought him the lute.

"I have no use for the lute," said the king. "Bring me that sound." They told him a lute is made of many parts: a body, a skin, a neck, the bindings, the strings, a plectrum, and the right effort of the player. Only with all of these together does it give its sound.

The king split the lute into ten pieces, then a hundred, then splinters, burned them, and let the wind or a fast river carry the ashes away. "A poor thing, it seems, this lute," he said, "and so is anything called a lute. Yet people are so careless about it, and so taken in."

In the same way, the Buddha said, a monk searches into form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness, as far as each of them reaches. When he searches like that, whatever he had of "I", "mine" or "I am" is no longer there for him.

Where the picture stops working: The player can look like the one who makes the music happen. He is one condition among the others. And the smashing is not the point: in the commentary the king breaks the lute to find the sound, while the monk only examines. The Visuddhimagga takes the lute further, for impermanence: the sound was not stored anywhere before it sounded and goes nowhere afterwards; it comes to be from the lute, its fittings and the player's effort (Vism XX, CS §723).

Told in: SN 35.246, the simile of the lute; Sāratthappakāsinī (commentary to the Saṃyutta) on SN 35.246, CS §246: the key; Visuddhimagga XX, CS §723: the lute's sound, from nowhere and to nowhere

In depth

How the Manual presents it

Chapter VIII gives two methods of conditionality (VIII §2). Dependent arising shows simply that this arises when that is present. The method of the Paṭṭhāna names the specific force by which one thing conditions another.

The Guide to VIII §1 fixes the terms: the conditioning state (paccaya-dhamma), the conditioned state (paccayuppannapaccayuppanna · conditioned state the dhamma that a condition helps to arise or persist), and the conditioning force (paccaya-satti). A condition is a dhamma that is helpful (upakāraka) to the arising or persistence of another. All conditioned things, and also Nibbāna and concepts, can condition. Only cittas, cetasikas and matter (except the four characteristics of matter) are conditioned.

The 24 conditions are listed in VIII §11: root, object, predominance, proximity, contiguity, conascence, mutuality, support, decisive support, prenascence, postnascence, repetition, kamma, result, nutriment, faculty, jhāna, path, association, dissociation, presence, absence, disappearance, non-disappearance.

Six relations, four master conditions

The Manual sorts them by what conditions what (VIII §12–18). Mind conditions mind in 6 ways: proximity, contiguity, absence, disappearance, repetition and association. Mind conditions mind-and-matter in 5: root, jhāna and path factors, kamma and result. Mind conditions matter in 1, postnascence. Matter conditions mind in 1, prenascence. Concepts and mind-and-matter condition mind in 2, object and decisive support. Mind-and-matter conditions mind-and-matter in 9: predominance, conascence, mutuality, support, nutriment, faculty, dissociation, presence and non-disappearance.

All 24 can be gathered under four master conditions (VIII §27, Table 8.4): object, decisive support, kamma and presence.

Distinctions that matter

Several conditions have sub-kinds. Predominance is by object or conascent (VIII §19). The four conascent predominants (desire, energy, citta and investigation) work one at a time, and only in javanas with two or three roots. Decisive support is threefold (VIII §17): by object; by proximity; and natural decisive support, which takes in lust, faith, pleasure, pain, a person, food, climate and lodging. Kamma is conascent, as volition with its own citta, or asynchronous, as past volition producing results (VIII §14).

A condition need not be present. Proximity works from a citta that has just ceased; asynchronous kamma may work from long ago. Postnascence runs backward: later cittas sustain a body that arose before them, as later rain sustains plants already grown (Guide to VIII §15).

The commentarial picture and why it matters

The Guide gives the familiar similes, drawn from the commentaries. Root is like the roots of a tree, which give it stability (Guide to VIII §14). Mutuality is like a tripod, each leg holding up the others (Guide to VIII §21). Support is like the earth for trees, or canvas for a painting (Guide to VIII §22).

What the scheme rules out matters as much as what it describes. Nothing arises from a single cause, from no cause, or from a first cause. Each named condition marks a place where change can begin: what one attends to, whom one keeps company with, what one repeats. See the reading The web of twenty-four conditions.

    Stories that show it

    Assaji's four lines

    Disciples · Vinaya, Mahāvagga I.23

    The wanderer Upatissa saw the monk Assaji on alms round and asked for his teacher's doctrine. Assaji gave it in one verse: “Of things that arise from a cause, the Tathāgata has told the cause, and also their cessation.” Upatissa, later Sāriputta, gained the eye of Dhamma on hearing it.

    The shortest summary of “The web of twenty-four conditions” and “The wheel across three lives”: whatever is conditioned has conditions, and they can cease. Sāriputta went on, the commentary says, to pass the Abhidhamma on to its first human disciples (“The library”).

    Rays from the Jewel House

    The Buddha's life · Atthasālinī, introduction

    In the fourth week after the awakening, the commentary says, the Buddha sat at the “jewel house” and contemplated the Abhidhamma. Through the first six books no rays appeared; when he reached the Paṭṭhāna, rays of six colours streamed from his body.

    The tradition marks the Paṭṭhāna, the book behind “The web of twenty-four conditions”, as the vastest of all. Its colours inspired the modern Buddhist flag.

    The whole of the holy life

    Disciples · SN 45.2

    Ānanda said that good friendship is half of the holy life. “Not so, Ānanda,” said the Buddha. “Good friendship is the whole of the holy life.” A monk with a good friend can be expected to develop the noble eightfold path.

    In “The noble eightfold path” the path, in the strict sense, is eight factors in one citta at the moment of the path; this story is about how they come to arise at all. A good friend is a natural decisive support from outside.

    Reflect and discuss

    Opening question

    The Paṭṭhāna names twenty-four ways one thing helps another. Why would liberation need so much detail about conditions?

    One way to see it, after you have tried

    Each named condition replaces a first cause or an independent agent with a relation that can be seen. And each one points to where change is possible: attention, association, support, repetition.
