Death and rebirth

rebirth-linking

The last citta of one life and the first of the next, with nothing passing between.

In brief

  • Death is the cutting off of the life faculty in one existence, when lifespan or kamma runs out, or when a destructive kamma cuts life short.
  • As death nears, the kamma about to ripen presents an object: the deed itself, a sign of it, or an image of the coming world.
  • The first moment of the next life, rebirth-linking (paṭisandhi), follows the moment of death at once, with no state in between.
  • Nothing passes across: the new life is conditioned by the old, as a seal leaves its mark in wax or an echo answers a voice.
  • The last thought does not choose the next life, since which kamma ripens follows an order: weighty kamma first, then kamma close to death, then habit.
  • The new being is, in the Milindapañha's words, neither the same as the old nor another.

What it is

Death (cuticuti · death consciousness the last citta of a life, of the same kind as its life-continuum) is the cutting off of the life faculty in one existence. Rebirth-linking (paṭisandhipaṭisandhi · rebirth-linking the first citta of a life: a resultant that links it to the last), the first citta of the next, follows without interval, conditioned by the kamma that ripens at death and taking the object shown to the last javanas: a kamma, a sign of kamma or a sign of destiny. Nothing passes across. The Visuddhimagga compares it to a seal and its impression, or an echo and its sound, and the Milindapañha calls the new being “neither the same nor another” (na ca so na ca añño).

In depth

How the Manual presents it

Death is the cutting off of the life faculty within one existence. It comes in four ways (V §34): the lifespan runs out; the productive kamma runs out; both run out together; or a destructive kamma cuts life short.

As death nears, the kamma that is about to ripen presents an object in one of three forms (V §35). It may be the kamma itself, recalled. It may be a sign of the kamma (kamma-nimittakamma-nimitta · kamma sign at death, an object linked with a past deed, such as offered flowers), something used or seen in doing it. Or it may be a sign of destiny (gati-nimittagati-nimitta · destiny sign at death, an image of the coming birth), an image of the coming world.

The last process runs only five weak javanas (IV §21; V §38). They have no rebirth-producing power of their own; they are the channel for the past kamma now set to produce the next birth (Guide to V §37). Death consciousness may follow the javanas at once, or after two moments of registration, or after some moments of life-continuum (Guide to V §37). Rebirth-linking arises immediately after. It takes the object of that last process, rests on the heart-base or, in the immaterial worlds, on no base, and is produced by a formation rooted in latent ignorance and craving (V §37).

Kinds of rebirth-linking

The Manual names four (V §9–17), by world. Rebirth in the woeful planes uses the unwholesome-resultant investigating citta. In the happy sense-sphere it uses the wholesome-resultant investigating citta, for those born deficient and for some lower deities, or one of the eight great resultants. The fine-material worlds have five rebirth cittas and the immaterial worlds four. That makes nineteen in all. Non-percipient beings are reborn with matter alone, a vital nonad (VI §28).

The objects differ by world (V §38–39). A sense-sphere rebirth takes a sense-sphere object, present or past. A fine-material rebirth takes a concept. An immaterial rebirth takes a concept or a sublime citta. The plane one dies in limits where one can go (V §40). A worldling dying in an immaterial world is reborn in the same or a higher one, or in the sense sphere with three roots. From the fine-material worlds there is no rootless rebirth.

The commentarial picture

Vism XVII (CS §631) gives four images of a connection without transfer: an echo, a lamp, a seal and a reflection in a mirror. Nothing moves from the voice to the echo, yet the echo depends on the voice. The Milindapañha has two lamp images: a flame that burns through the night, neither the same flame nor another, and a lamp lit from another lamp, with nothing passing across.

The Theravāda allows no intermediate existence between death and rebirth-linking. The Kathāvatthu argues against one (Kv VIII.2), and in the Manual the two cittas are simply consecutive.

Common misunderstandings

The last thought does not choose the next life. Which kamma ripens follows an order: weighty kamma first, then kamma close to death, then habitual kamma, then reserve (V §19). The death-moment process only carries it.

Nor does the body simply outlast the mind. Kamma-born matter stops arising seventeen cittas before death, so the last of it ceases with the death citta, and the corpse that remains is temperature-born matter (VI §25). And the new life does not begin with a blank. The first process after rebirth is a clinging to the new existence: greed-rooted javanas that take the rebirth-linking citta as their object (Guide to V §41). Stories that show it

The last line of every Jātaka

Jātaka · Jātaka, closing identification

Each Jātaka ends by naming who was who: “At that time, so-and-so was the king, and I myself was the ascetic.” One continuity is followed across many lives as if it were one person.

Conventional truth at work (“A chariot of parts”). Ultimately there is only a stream of cittas and matter, each moment conditioning the next. “The Bodhisatta” names the stream: in Nāgasena's words to King Milinda, neither the same person, nor a different one.

Khantivādi, the teacher of patience

Jātaka · Ja 313

A drunken king, Kalābu, asked an ascetic what he taught. “Patience,” he said. To test him, the king had his hands, feet, nose and ears cut off. The ascetic felt no anger and wished the king long life. Leaving, the king was swallowed by the earth.

“An ensemble of fifty-two” at its sharpest: non-hatred (adosaadosa · non-hatred Non-hatred is the mind's not opposing its object: friendliness and patience, not only the absence of ill will.) and hatred (dosadosa · hatred aversion, from irritation to rage; root of two cittas, always with displeasure) never share a citta. The king's fate returns in “Kamma, death and rebirth” as kamma that ripens in this very life.

Rāhula and the mirror

The Buddha's life · MN 61

The Buddha asked his young son Rāhula what a mirror is for. “For reflection, sir.” “In the same way,” said the Buddha, “reflect before, during and after every action of body, speech and mind: does it lead to harm for myself, for others, or both?”

Reflection is a discipline for the javanas, where volition becomes kamma (“The mango and the moment” and “Kamma, death and rebirth”).

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Nothing passes from the dying moment to rebirth-linking. What connects the two lives?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Condition alone. The last process's kamma and its object condition the rebirth-linking citta, as a seal leaves its mark in wax or an echo answers a voice: Visuddhimagga XVII's similes. The new citta is neither the old one nor unrelated to it.
