Dependent origination

12 links, 3 lives

The twelve links, read across three lives.

In brief

  • Dependent origination is a chain of twelve links, from ignorance to ageing and death, showing how the round of births keeps turning.
  • Its principle is simple: when this is, that is; from the arising of this, that arises.
  • The commentaries and the Manual spread the links over three lives: past causes, present results, present causes and future results.
  • The Guide adds that the three periods are a frame for the causal pattern, not a timetable: all twelve links are at work in any one life; the Vibhaṅga's Abhidhamma section reads them in one moment.
  • Ignorance and craving are its two roots; with no self passing from life to life, it avoids both eternalism and annihilationism.
  • SN 12.1 also states it in cessation form: with the cessation of ignorance, formations cease, and so on to the end of suffering.

What it is

“When this is, that is; from the arising of this, that arises” (SN 12.21). The twelve links show how ignorance and craving keep the round (vaṭṭavaṭṭa · round one of three cycles turning in the wheel: defilements, kamma, results) of existence turning. The commentaries, and the Manual after them, spread the links over three lives: past causes, present results, present causes, future results. Saṃsāra, the round of births, appears as a process with no self that transmigrates, which avoids both eternalism (sassata-diṭṭhisassata-diṭṭhi · eternalism the view of a self that lasts for ever; one of the two extreme views) and annihilationism (uccheda-diṭṭhiuccheda-diṭṭhi · annihilationism the view that a self is destroyed at death; the other extreme view). SN 12.1 also states the chain in its cessation form: with the cessation of ignorance, formations cease, and so on to the end of the whole mass of suffering.

In a picture

A fire that lives on its fuel

From the canon · SN 12.52, 12.53 and 12.55

The chain of dependent origination goes on only as long as it is fed, and it ends when the feeding stops.

Imagine a great bonfire, as big as ten, twenty, thirty or forty cartloads of wood. Someone keeps throwing on dry grass, dry cowdung and dry wood. Fed like that, the fire burns for a very long time.

The Buddha says it is the same with a person who keeps looking for satisfaction in the things that can be clung to. In that person craving grows. Craving leads to clinging, clinging to becoming, becoming to birth, and birth to ageing, death, sorrow and pain. The whole mass of suffering burns on. (In these discourses the chain is told from craving onwards, not through all twelve links.)

Now imagine that no one adds any more fuel. When what it had is used up, the fire goes out. In the same way, when a person keeps looking at the danger in what can be clung to, craving ceases, and with it clinging, becoming, birth, and the whole mass of suffering.

In the next discourses the Buddha tells the same story with an oil lamp that burns as long as oil and wick are added (there he speaks of the things that fetter, rather than those that can be clung to), and with a great tree that lives as long as its roots draw up sap, and ends when it is cut down, uprooted, burnt and its ashes scattered.

Where the picture stops working: One fire and one fuel can make it look as if the round had a single cause. In the Abhidhamma no effect has a single cause: the formula names only the chief condition among many (Guide to CMA VIII §3). The commentary also reads the picture its own way: the fire is the round of the three planes, the fuel is kamma done through the six doors by way of craving, and the fire going out is an arahant's final passing away (SN commentary, CS §52).

Told in: SN 12.52, the great fire; SN 12.53, the oil lamp; SN 12.55, the great tree; Sāratthappakāsinī (commentary to the Saṃyutta) on SN 12.52, CS §52

The old road to the lost city

From the canon · SN 12.65

The eightfold path is found, not invented. Buddhas before walked it, and it had only to be found again.

A man wandering through a forest comes on an old road, the kind people used long ago. He follows it, and it leads him to an ancient city where people once lived, with parks, groves, ponds and walls, still lovely, though no one lived there any more.

He goes to the king and tells him what he has found. The king has the city rebuilt. In time it is full of people again, prosperous and busy.

The Buddha said his own discovery was like that. He had found the old road that the buddhas of the past had walked: the noble eightfold path, from right view to right concentration. Following it, he came to know ageing and death, where they come from, their ending, and the way to their ending; and then each link of the chain the same way, back to the formations. He made it known, and the holy life spread among many people.

Where the picture stops working: Reading the city as Nibbāna, as the commentary does, can make Nibbāna sound like a place one walks into. It is not a place. What the picture shows is that the way to it was walked before and can be walked again.

Told in: SN 12.65, the city; Sāratthappakāsinī (commentary to the Saṃyutta) on SN 12.65, CS §65

Whose child is this?

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XIX, CS §§678–679

Having seen mind and matter, one asks where they come from, as naturally as one asks whose a baby found lying in the street is. The answer is a family of conditions, neither a creator nor chance.

The Visuddhimagga says the meditator who wants to overcome doubt sets about it like a good physician who sees a disease and looks for where it came from; or like a kind man who sees a tiny baby lying on its back in the street and wonders whose child it can be, and thinks about its mother and father. So he looks for the causes and conditions of this mind and matter.

First he reasons that it is not without a cause, for then it would be the same everywhere, always, for everyone. Nor is it made by a Lord or anything of the kind, for there is nothing of that kind over and above mind and matter. So it must have causes and conditions. What are they?

He begins with the body, which, the sub-commentary says, is the coarser and plainer of the two. This body is not born inside lotuses or water lilies, or among gems and strings of pearls, but in a cramped and foul place between the stomach and the bowel, with the wall of the belly behind it and the backbone in front of it, like a worm in rotten fish. Ignorance, craving, clinging and kamma are its causes, because they produce it, and nutriment is its condition, because it supports it. Of these, ignorance, craving and clinging stand to the body as a mother to her child, as its decisive support; kamma as a father, who begets it; and nutriment as a nurse, who keeps it going.

For mind he uses another method, the sutta's: dependent on the eye and forms, eye-consciousness arises, and so on for each door.

Where the picture stops working: Parents are persons who outlive the birth; conditions are dhammas, and the kamma that begot this body ceased long ago. The mother, father and nurse are given for the body. Ignorance and the rest condition mind too, the sub-commentary notes, but the Visuddhimagga traces mind's conditions by another method. And a child is someone, while here there is no one besides the mind and matter whose conditions are sought (CMA IX §30 and Guide). The unclean birthplace is not decoration: the sub-commentary says it is there to turn the mind from delight in the body. The Visuddhimagga goes on to call one who has this knowledge a "lesser stream-enterer", who has found comfort and a footing in the Dispensation and is sure of his destination (XIX §691); the sub-commentary explains that destination as a good rebirth, so long as this knowledge is not lost. That is the commentary's honour for a worldling, not the path.

Told in: Visuddhimagga XIX (purification by overcoming doubt), CS §§678–679; the lesser stream-enterer, §691; SN 12.43, dependent on eye and forms (the method for mind); Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XIX §§679, 691; CMA IX §31 and Guide: purification by overcoming doubt

In depth

How the Manual presents it

VIII §3 gives the twelve links. Ignorance conditions formations, then consciousness, mentality-materiality, the six bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, and ageing and death with sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief and despair. VIII §4 lists seven ways to analyse the chain: three periods, twelve factors, twenty modes, three connections, four groups, three rounds and two roots (VIII §4–10, Table 8.1).

The periods (VIII §5) put ignorance and formations in the past life, birth and ageing and death in the future, and the eight middle links in the present.

What each link is

The Guide to VIII §3 names the dhammas behind the links. Formations are the volitions in the 29 mundane wholesome and unwholesome cittas. Consciousness is the 32 mundane resultant cittas, from rebirth-linking on. Mentality-materiality is the factors joined to them, with kamma-born matter. Becoming is twofold. Kamma-becoming (kamma-bhavakamma-bhava · kamma-process becoming becoming as the active side: the wholesome and unwholesome volitions that make for a new existence) is the kamma-making process. Rebirth-becoming (upapatti-bhavaupapatti-bhava · rebirth-process becoming becoming as the passive side: the resultant cittas, their factors and kamma-born matter of a new existence) is the 32 resultants with their factors and kamma-born matter.

The Guide adds a detail about feeling: neutral feeling too conditions craving, through its peacefulness.

Groups, connections, rounds, roots

There are four groups of five (VIII §7). The past causes are ignorance, formations, craving, clinging and becoming. The present results run from consciousness to feeling. The present causes are the same five, at work now. The future results are the same five results, shown as birth and ageing and death. That makes the twenty modes.

The three connections fall between formations and consciousness, between feeling and craving, and between becoming and birth. The three rounds (VIII §8) are defilements (ignorance, craving, clinging), kamma (formations and kamma-becoming) and results (the rest). The two roots (VIII §9) are ignorance, running from the past to feeling, and craving, running from the present to ageing and death.

Readings and misunderstandings

The Manual follows the commentaries (Vism XVII) in spreading the links over three lives (VIII §5). The Guide treats the three periods as a frame for showing the causal pattern, not a timetable: no link is confined to its period, and in any one life all twelve are at work and imply one another (Guide to VIII §5; see VIII §7). Vism XVII also works out which of the 24 conditions operate at each link. The suttas show variants. DN 15 runs a two-way bond between consciousness and mentality-materiality. The Vibhaṅga's Abhidhamma section reads the whole chain within a single moment of citta.

The chain is not a first-cause story. Ignorance has its own condition, the taints (MN 9). It is not a row of single causes either, since each link stands in many relations at once. "Birth" means the arising of the aggregates at rebirth-linking, not the day of delivery. SN 12.1 also states the chain in its cessation form: with the cessation of ignorance, formations cease, and so on to the end of the whole mass of suffering (SN 12.1). In AN 3.61 the two orders are given as two of the four truths: the arising order as the origin of suffering, the ceasing order as its cessation (AN 3.61, 15.1–16.14; see the concept The four noble truths). Stories that show it

Dhammadinnā on feeling

Disciples · MN 44

Questioned by the layman Visākha, the nun Dhammadinnā explained that lust lies latent in pleasant feeling, aversion in painful feeling and ignorance in neutral feeling. The Buddha said he would have answered in the same way.

This joins the colours of feeling in “A map of the eighty-nine” to the wheel in “The wheel across three lives”: feeling (link 7) is where craving (link 8) takes hold.

Two sheaves of reeds

Disciples · SN 12.67

Mahākoṭṭhita asked Sāriputta whether name-and-form and consciousness were made by oneself, by another, by both, or by neither. By none of these, said Sāriputta: each arises with the other as its condition, like two sheaves of reeds standing propped against each other. Pull one away and the other falls.

The two sheaves are consciousness on one side and name-and-form on the other (the mental factors and matter); “Mind and matter together” models the same mutual support: two currents that hold each other up, and that arise together at conception.

The night of awakening

The Buddha's life · MN 36; Ud 1.1–1.3

Under the Bodhi tree, in the first watch of the night he recalled his past lives; in the second he saw beings pass away and reappear according to their kamma; in the third he realised the destruction of the taints. In the days that followed he reviewed dependent origination forward and in reverse.

The three watches map onto the readings: past lives across the planes (“Thirty-one planes”), beings reborn by kamma and its fruit (“Kamma, death and rebirth”), and the wheel with its ending (“The wheel across three lives” and “The tree of purification”).

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Dependent origination is usually read across three lives. Where, in a single moment, could you watch one link become the next?

One way to see it, after you have tried

From feeling to craving, in the javanas that follow a pleasant feeling. The Vibhaṅga also teaches the whole series within a single moment of citta, in its Abhidhamma section, so the chain is not only a story of lives.
