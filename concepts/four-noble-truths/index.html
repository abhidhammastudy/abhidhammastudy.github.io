The four noble truths

ariya-sacca

The Buddha's four truths, which the Abhidhamma reads as a sorting of every mundane reality, with Nibbāna and the supramundane path set apart.

In brief

  • The four noble truths are suffering, its origin, its cessation and the path to its cessation: the frame of the Buddha's first sermon.
  • The Manual sorts realities under them: suffering is the mundane except craving, the origin is craving, cessation is Nibbāna, the path the eight path factors.
  • Each truth has a task: to be fully understood, abandoned, realised and developed.
  • At each of the four paths, one knowledge does all four tasks in a single moment.
  • “Suffering” here means what is conditioned and can be clung to, not only what hurts.

What it is

The four noble truths (ariya-saccaariya-sacca · noble truth suffering, its origin, its cessation, the path: the four noble truths) are suffering, its origin, its cessation and the path to its cessation, set out in the Buddha’s first sermon (SN 56.11). Each has its task: suffering is to be fully understood, its origin abandoned, its cessation realised, the path developed. Every Buddhist school teaches the four truths, though not every school makes them its frame or reads them alike. What the Theravāda Abhidhamma adds is a sorting. On its analysis each truth is a set of ultimate realities: every mundane citta, mental factor and kind of matter falls under the first two truths, Nibbāna is the third, and the fourth is eight mental factors of the path moment. On the Manual’s strict count, only the eight supramundane cittas and their factors, apart from the path’s eight factors, are left outside (Guide to VII §38; VII §40).

In a picture

A handful of leaves

From the canon · SN 56.31

Why the four noble truths sit at the centre: out of everything the Buddha knew, he taught what leads to freedom.

The Buddha was staying in a grove of rosewood trees near Kosambī. He picked up a few leaves and asked the monks: which are more, the leaves in my hand, or the leaves on the trees of this grove? The monks said the leaves in the grove were far more.

In the same way, he said, the things he had come to know but had not taught were far more than the things he taught. He left the rest untaught because it did not help: it did not lead to turning away, to peace, to awakening.

And what had he taught? This is suffering; this is its origin; this is its ending; this is the way to its ending. Those few he taught, because they help: they lead to turning away, to peace, to awakening and to Nibbāna. That, he told the monks, is what to work at.

Where the picture stops working: Read carelessly, it seems to put all detailed analysis, the Abhidhamma included, among the leaves of the grove. This site reads the Abhidhamma the other way, as a closer look at these same four truths, which the Manual itself sets out among its categories (CMA VII §§38–40).

In depth

How the Manual presents it

The Manual ends its survey of the whole with the four noble truths (VII §§38–40). The Guide gives four reasons for the word "noble". The noble ones penetrate them; they are the truths of the Noble One, the Buddha; discovering them makes a person noble; and they are real, never otherwise than they are (Guide to VII §38).

Then each truth is named in ultimate realities. Suffering is what the suttas list, from birth, ageing and death to the five aggregates of clinging that sum the list up. As realities it is everything of the three mundane planes except craving. Counted out, that is the 81 mundane cittas, all their mental factors but greed, and all 28 kinds of matter; the Guide says only "except craving", and the counts are this site's spelling-out. The origin is craving, which is the factor greed, in three aspects: craving for sense pleasures, for existence and for non-existence. Cessation is Nibbāna. The path is the eight path factors, but only as they arise in the four supramundane path cittas (Guide to VII §38).

The summary verse names what is left over. Strictly speaking, the path citta itself and its other factors, and the four fruitions with all of theirs, belong to no truth (VII §40; Guide to VII §40). That is the Manual's strict count, not the only one (see Two methods below). One overlap needs sorting. The suttas sum up suffering as the five aggregates of clinging, and those include craving. Buddhaghosa answers with a fourfold table: the clinging aggregates other than craving are both suffering and its noble truth, while the states that go with the path, and the fruitions, are suffering only as conditioned formations, not the truth the holy life is lived to understand (Vism XVI §566, commentarial).

Tasks, turnings, one moment

In the first sermon each truth is turned three times: this is suffering; it is to be fully understood; it has been fully understood. Four truths by three turnings make twelve aspects, and the Buddha claimed awakening only when all twelve were complete (SN 56.11). Each truth has its own task: suffering is to be fully understood, its origin abandoned, its cessation realised, the path developed (SN 56.29).

Before the path, knowing the truths is following knowledge (anubodha). It is mundane, and it reaches cessation and the path only by learning and hearsay. Penetration (paṭivedha) is supramundane and takes all four at once (Vism XVI §563). At the path moment one knowledge, with Nibbāna as its object, does the four tasks together (Guide to IX §34). This happens four times, once at each path, and each path abandons only its share of the defilements: the first cuts wrong view and doubt, and craving is gone entirely only at the fourth (IX §§38–41 with the Guides). The commentaries liken it to a lamp, which in one moment burns the wick, dispels the dark, makes light and uses up the oil; and in the same way to the sun, and to a boat that leaves one shore, crosses the stream, carries its cargo and reaches the far shore (Vism XXII §§839–841).

Distinctions that matter

Two senses of saccasacca · truth truth, and what is real: the four noble truths; in another sense also the two truths, conventional and ultimate. The two truths, conventional and ultimate (see the concept Two truths), are two levels of speaking. The four noble truths are something else: four sets of realities. Saccasacca · truth truth, and what is real: the four noble truths; in another sense also the two truths, conventional and ultimate means both "true" and "real", and the Guide's last reason for calling them noble is that they are real and unalterable (Guide to VII §38); on this reading 'noble realities' would fit as well as 'noble truths'.

Three kinds of suffering. Dukkhadukkha · bodily pain painful bodily feeling: only in unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness is wider than pain. The suttas name three kinds: the suffering of pain, the suffering of change, and the suffering of conditioned existence (SN 38.14; SN 45.165). Buddhaghosa assigns painful feeling to the first, pleasant feeling to the second, and neutral feeling with the other formations of the three planes to the third (Vism XVI §539, commentarial). So the first truth takes in pleasant and wholesome mundane states too. They are conditioned, they pass, and they belong to the round. The word shifts sense, then. As feeling, dukkhadukkha · bodily pain painful bodily feeling: only in unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness is pain; as a characteristic it marks everything conditioned, the path included; as the first truth it is what is conditioned and can be clung to. The noble truths use the third (Vism XVI §566, commentarial).

Two methods. The Manual's count matches the sutta method, with craving alone as the origin. The Vibhaṅga's Abhidhamma section drops the word "noble" and widens the origin step by step: craving, then all defilements, all unwholesome states, the wholesome roots with taints, and all tainted wholesome states. Each time, suffering is the rest (Vibh 4). Once the word "noble" is dropped, then, the good moments of ordinary practice can count under the origin rather than under suffering.

Truths and the wheel. In AN 3.61 the arising order of dependent origination is given as the origin of suffering, and its ceasing order as cessation (15.1–16.14). In the sutta explanation, ignorance, the first link, is not knowing these four truths (Guide to VIII §3).

Common misunderstandings

Suffering is not to be abandoned; it is to be fully understood. Abandoning is the task for craving. Cessation is not developed or produced; it is realised. Not every good state is the path: in the teaching of the noble truths only the eight factors of a supramundane path moment are the fourth truth, and a mundane kind thought, however useful in practice, counts with what is to be understood (Guide to VII §38; Vism XVI §564). Nor are the truths seen one by one. The Theravāda holds that all four are penetrated in a single moment (SN 56.30; Kv II.9).

Nor is the physician's diagnosis the tradition's own frame for the truths. The commentaries do give disease, its cause, its cure and the medicine, but as one image among several, beside a burden, taking it up, laying it down and the means of laying it down, and famine, drought, plenty and rain (Vism XVI §565). See the readings The four noble truths and The noble eightfold path.

Stories that show it

The night of awakening

The Buddha's life · MN 36; Ud 1.1–1.3

Under the Bodhi tree, in the first watch of the night he recalled his past lives; in the second he saw beings pass away and reappear according to their kamma; in the third he realised the destruction of the taints. In the days that followed he reviewed dependent origination forward and in reverse.

The three watches map onto the readings: past lives across the planes (“Thirty-one planes”), beings reborn by kamma and its fruit (“Kamma, death and rebirth”), and the wheel with its ending (“The wheel across three lives” and “The tree of purification”).

Koṇḍañña's eye of Dhamma

The Buddha's life · SN 56.11

At the first sermon in the Deer Park, while the Buddha was speaking, Koṇḍañña understood: “Whatever is subject to arising is all subject to cessation.” The Buddha exclaimed, “Koṇḍañña has understood!”

“The four noble truths” reads this sermon the Abhidhamma way: each truth a set of realities with its own task, and all four seen in a single moment. The commentaries take Koṇḍañña's insight as stream-entry: path knowledge took Nibbāna as its object and the first three fetters fell away. The sutta says only that the eye of Dhamma arose. The Saṃyutta commentary takes it here as the first path alone, and has him established in the fruit of stream-entry (SN-a on SN 56.11, CS §1081); that fruition follows the path in the same process is the Manual's account (CMA IX §34).

Yasa wakes in the night

Disciples · Vinaya, Mahāvagga I.7–8

Yasa woke in the night in his pleasure palace and saw his musicians asleep around him, sprawled with their instruments, drooling and muttering, like a charnel ground. The danger struck him, his mind settled in disenchantment, and he walked out of the city in the dark crying that it was oppressive. At the Deer Park the Buddha told him that here nothing was oppressive, and taught him; he entered the stream, and soon after was freed.

Danger and disenchantment here are plain words, not yet the knowledges of “Ten knowledges, one by one”: Yasa was weary of a house of pleasure, not yet of all formations. The site links the story to danger, disenchantment and the wish to be free as an image of what those knowledges feel like, not as the knowledges themselves. The canonical sequence after it fits the Manual's: the Dhamma-eye at the four truths, which the commentaries take as the path of stream-entry (CMA IX §34), and release while “reviewing the ground he had seen and known”. The Visuddhimagga says a stream-enterer who has reviewed goes on to the next path “on that same seat or later” by turning insight on formations again (Vism XXII §813). A Vinaya sub-commentary takes Yasa's “reviewing” as seeing the same truths again with the three higher paths (Vimativinodanī on Mv I.7, CS §28), so the site does not mark it as reviewing knowledge.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

In the teaching of the noble truths a kind thought and a moment of mindfulness fall under the truth of suffering. What is gained by calling them that?

One way to see it, after you have tried

'Suffering' here names what is conditioned and can be clung to, not what hurts. Good mundane states are still to be developed as practice, but as realities they belong with what is to be fully understood, not clung to. Only the path at the moment of awakening is the fourth truth (Guide to VII §38; Vism XVI §§564, 566).
