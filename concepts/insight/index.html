Insight

vipassanā

The knowledges that see mind and matter rise and fall.

In brief

  • Insight (vipassanā) is wisdom that sees mind and matter as they are, marked by impermanence, suffering and non-self.
  • It unfolds through seven purifications, from virtue and a concentrated mind up to the path, and within them through ten insight knowledges.
  • They begin with comprehension, which works on formations in groups, past, future and present, before the knowledges that watch present formations arise and pass.
  • Bright lights, bliss and other striking experiences along the way are imperfections of insight, and the meditator must see that they are not the path.
  • Insight takes only conditioned things as its object, so it ripens the mind for the path but does not itself realise Nibbāna, the unconditioned.
  • The reading Ten knowledges, one by one takes each knowledge in turn, with where later teachers differ.

What it is

Insight (vipassanāvipassanā · insight seeing mind and matter as impermanent, suffering and non-self) is wisdom (paññāpaññā · wisdom understanding things as they are; also called non-delusion and the faculty of wisdom) that sees mind and matter as they are: marked by impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkhadukkha · bodily pain painful bodily feeling: only in unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness) and non-self (anattā). It unfolds within the seven purifications, from purification of virtue to purification by knowledge and vision, and within them through the ten insight knowledges, from comprehension to conformity (IX §25). Insight takes conditioned dhammas as its object: it ripens the mind for the path, but it does not itself realise Nibbāna.

In a picture

The tree without its branches and leaves

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · AN 5.24

Each step of the path is the supporting condition of the next, beginning from virtue. Damage the branches and leaves, and nothing inside the tree grows to fullness.

In one who lacks virtue, the Buddha said, right concentration has lost its support. Without right concentration, the knowledge and vision of things as they really are has lost its support; without that, disenchantment and fading away; and without those, the knowledge and vision of release.

It is like a tree whose branches and leaves are damaged: its outer bark does not grow to fullness, nor its inner bark, nor its sapwood, nor its heartwood.

In one who has virtue it is the other way round. Each step has its support, as in a tree whose branches and leaves are whole the outer bark, the inner bark, the sapwood and the heartwood all grow to fullness. The same tree comes back in other suttas of the Aṅguttara, with longer chains of steps.

The commentary on the Aṅguttara reads the chain as the path of insight. The knowledge and vision of things as they really are is tender insight, beginning from the knowledge that defines mind and matter. Disenchantment is strong insight. Fading away is the path. The knowledge and vision of release is fruition, together with the reviewing knowledge.

Where the picture stops working: A tree grows by itself, slowly and without a break. Insight passes through distinct knowledges, and the path is a single moment (Guide to CMA I §§26–28; CMA IX §34). The sutta names virtue and right concentration but none of the purifications; setting them beside the purification of virtue and of mind (CMA IX §§28–29) is this site's reading. And "disenchantment" here, in the commentary's sense of strong insight, is wider than the single knowledge of disenchantment in the Manual's list (Guide to CMA IX §33); the commentary does not say where strong insight begins. So too, "tender insight" here is the commentary's name for all insight from defining mind and matter onward. That is wider than the Visuddhimagga's tender insight, which is only the first phase of the knowledge of rise and fall (Vism XX §731; Guide to CMA IX §32).

Told in: AN 5.24, the tree (an5.24:1.1–2.9); The same tree with longer chains: AN 6.50, AN 7.65, AN 8.81, AN 10.3; Manorathapūraṇī (commentary to the Aṅguttara) on AN 5.24, CS §24; CMA IX §§28–34: the seven purifications

The blind man and the cripple

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XVIII, CS §677; Atthasālinī, CS §498

Mind and matter each lack strength of their own and work only by leaning on each other. There is no third thing that walks.

A man blind from birth and a cripple who could only shuffle along on a low seat both wanted to go elsewhere. The blind man said: I can do what feet do, but I have no eyes to see where the ground is smooth or rough. The cripple said: I can do what eyes do, but I have no feet to go forward or back. The blind man, delighted, lifted the cripple onto his shoulders, and the cripple, sitting there, called out: leave the left, take the right; leave the right, take the left.

The Visuddhimagga brings it in as a simile that others cite (the commentary teachers, says its sub-commentary), and draws the point. Neither man goes by his own power or his own strength, and yet, leaning on each other, they do go. So mind has no power of its own to arise or to act, and matter has none either. Mind does not eat, drink, speak or take up a posture by itself, and matter has no wish to; but when there is mind's wish to eat or drink, to speak or move, matter eats, drinks, speaks and moves. It adds verses: as people cross the ocean depending on a ship, and the ship crosses depending on the people, so mind and matter each depend on the other.

The Atthasālinī, the commentary on the first book of the Abhidhamma, tells the story more fully. The two sat in a hall at the city gate. The cripple told the blind man of a district where food was plentiful and asked why he did not go and live there in comfort. Why not you? said the blind man. I have no feet, said the cripple; I have no eyes, said the blind man. Then let your feet be mine and my eyes be yours. With the cripple on his shoulders, holding his head with the left hand and pointing out the way with the right (a root here, a stone there), they reached the place together and lived there at ease.

Where the picture stops working: Two persons, one of them giving directions, make mind sound like a pilot: a self riding the body. Both texts rule this out by saying that neither has strength of its own, and the "mind" here is itself many dhammas, the four mental aggregates, arising and passing. The Visuddhimagga's sub-commentary raises the obvious objection, that a hundred blind men together see no better than one, and answers with the simile itself, recalling Sāriputta's words, before his two sheaves of reeds, that some wise people understand a meaning through a simile (SN 12.67, sn12.67:7.3). And the Atthasālinī says the picture is told for existence with all five aggregates. Elsewhere it would not hold: in the immaterial planes mind occurs without matter, and among the non-percipient beings matter occurs without mind (CMA VI §§28–29).

Told in: Visuddhimagga XVIII (purification of view), CS §677; Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XVIII §677; SN 12.67, the two sheaves of reeds (sn12.67:7.3); Atthasālinī, on the great resultant cittas, the miscellaneous similes (Vipākuddhārakathā), CS §498; CMA IX §30 and Guide: purification of view

Whose child is this?

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XIX, CS §§678–679

Having seen mind and matter, one asks where they come from, as naturally as one asks whose a baby found lying in the street is. The answer is a family of conditions, neither a creator nor chance.

The Visuddhimagga says the meditator who wants to overcome doubt sets about it like a good physician who sees a disease and looks for where it came from; or like a kind man who sees a tiny baby lying on its back in the street and wonders whose child it can be, and thinks about its mother and father. So he looks for the causes and conditions of this mind and matter.

First he reasons that it is not without a cause, for then it would be the same everywhere, always, for everyone. Nor is it made by a Lord or anything of the kind, for there is nothing of that kind over and above mind and matter. So it must have causes and conditions. What are they?

He begins with the body, which, the sub-commentary says, is the coarser and plainer of the two. This body is not born inside lotuses or water lilies, or among gems and strings of pearls, but in a cramped and foul place between the stomach and the bowel, with the wall of the belly behind it and the backbone in front of it, like a worm in rotten fish. Ignorance, craving, clinging and kamma are its causes, because they produce it, and nutriment is its condition, because it supports it. Of these, ignorance, craving and clinging stand to the body as a mother to her child, as its decisive support; kamma as a father, who begets it; and nutriment as a nurse, who keeps it going.

For mind he uses another method, the sutta's: dependent on the eye and forms, eye-consciousness arises, and so on for each door.

Where the picture stops working: Parents are persons who outlive the birth; conditions are dhammas, and the kamma that begot this body ceased long ago. The mother, father and nurse are given for the body. Ignorance and the rest condition mind too, the sub-commentary notes, but the Visuddhimagga traces mind's conditions by another method. And a child is someone, while here there is no one besides the mind and matter whose conditions are sought (CMA IX §30 and Guide). The unclean birthplace is not decoration: the sub-commentary says it is there to turn the mind from delight in the body. The Visuddhimagga goes on to call one who has this knowledge a "lesser stream-enterer", who has found comfort and a footing in the Dispensation and is sure of his destination (XIX §691); the sub-commentary explains that destination as a good rebirth, so long as this knowledge is not lost. That is the commentary's honour for a worldling, not the path.

Told in: Visuddhimagga XIX (purification by overcoming doubt), CS §§678–679; the lesser stream-enterer, §691; SN 12.43, dependent on eye and forms (the method for mind); Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XIX §§679, 691; CMA IX §31 and Guide: purification by overcoming doubt

In depth

How the Manual presents it

Chapter IX sets out the field of insight (IX §22–27). There are seven purifications, from the relay-chariots of MN 24. There are three characteristics: impermanence, suffering and non-self. There are three contemplations, one for each characteristic. There are ten insight knowledges, in the order they arise: comprehension, rise and fall, dissolution, fearfulness, danger, disenchantment, desire for deliverance, reflection, equanimity about formations and conformity. Last come the three emancipations and the three doors to them.

The purifications step by step

Purification of virtue is fourfold: restraint by the Pātimokkha, restraint of the senses, pure livelihood, and right use of the requisites (IX §28). Purification of mind is access and absorption concentration (IX §29; Vism XVIII §662). For one who goes by dry insight, momentary concentration, as steady as access, counts instead (Guide to IX §29); the pairing of pure insight with momentary concentration is in the Visuddhimagga's sub-commentary (on Vism I §§3–4). Purification of view discerns mind and matter by characteristic, function, manifestation and proximate cause (IX §30; Vism XVIII §§662–677). Overcoming doubt discerns their conditions in past, present and future (IX §31; Vism XIX §§678–691).

Path and not-path is the next purification (IX §32). Formations of past, future and present are gathered into groups, by aggregate and so on, and comprehended by the three marks (Vism XX §§692–722); then an early knowledge of rise and fall arrives (Vism XX §§723–731). With it come ten imperfections of insight. In the VisuddhimaggaVisuddhimagga · Path of Purification Buddhaghosa's fifth-century manual of practice and doctrine; source of Plates 12–13's order they are light, knowledge, zest, tranquillity, happiness, resolution, exertion, mindfulness, equanimity, and a subtle attachment to them all (Vism XX §§732–736); the Manual's verse gives the same ten in another order (IX §32). The meditator must see them as impermanent, suffering and non-self, and so see that they are not the path (Vism XX §736).

Purification of the way covers nine knowledges, from mature rise and fall to conformity (IX §33; Vism XXI §§737–805). Purification by knowledge and vision is the path knowledge itself (IX §34). Change-of-lineage falls between the last two purifications and belongs to neither (Table 9.2; Vism XXII §806).

Distinctions that matter

Insight is wisdom in sense-sphere cittas: the great (sense-sphere) wholesome cittas with knowledge, or functional ones in an arahant. Its objects are conditioned, mundane dhammas, never concepts and never Nibbāna. Nibbāna is known first by change-of-lineage, then by path and fruition, and afterwards by the reviewing cittas that look back on it (Guide to III §18; IX §34).

The mark that leads out gives the path its name (IX §35–36). Seeing non-self opens the void emancipation, impermanence the signless, and suffering the desireless. The commentaries add which faculty favours which door: faith leans toward impermanence, concentration toward suffering, wisdom toward non-self (Guide to IX §35; Vism XXI §§768–769, 772–774). When equanimity about formations and conformity are fully developed, they are called "insight leading to emergence" (IX §34); the VisuddhimaggaVisuddhimagga · Path of Purification Buddhaghosa's fifth-century manual of practice and doctrine; source of Plates 12–13 counts change-of-lineage in it too (Vism XXI §782).

Insight sees formations: the first truth, and craving among them. Its knowledge of the truths is following knowledge (anubodha), which is mundane and reaches cessation and the path only by learning and hearsay. Path knowledge penetrates all four at once (Vism XVI §563; Guide to IX §34). See the concept The four noble truths.

Common misunderstandings

The "sixteen knowledges" of many modern manuals are the Manual's ten with additions. Before them come defining mind and matter and discerning conditions. After them come change-of-lineage, path, fruition and reviewing. The items are all in Vism XVIII–XXII, but the count is a later arrangement used in modern teaching, for example in Mahasi Sayadaw's The Progress of Insight. The canon's own list, in the Paṭisambhidāmagga, is shorter: it counts fear, danger and disenchantment as one knowledge, and desire for deliverance, reflection and equanimity as one (Vism XXI §§755, 778–781).

Insight is not only thinking about impermanence. Comprehension works on formations in groups, by spans of time, before the knowledges that watch present formations arise and pass; dissolution then infers past and future from what it sees in the present (Vism XX; Vism XXI §745). Bright lights and bliss are imperfections, not attainments. The knowledges of fear and danger are judgements, not moods: the knowledge of fear does not itself fear (Vism XXI §751). Insight does not realise Nibbāna itself; it ripens the mind until the path does. See the readings Ten knowledges, one by one, The tree of purification and Mind and matter together.

Stories that show it

Makhādeva's first grey hair

Jātaka · Ja 9

King Makhādeva told his barber to report the first grey hair. When it came, the king held it in his hand, saw death approaching, handed the kingdom to his son and went forth.

A small object can carry a large process: the same grey hair can stir vanity or urgency (saṃvega), depending on how it is attended to (“The mango and the moment”). MN 83 adds the Buddha's own point: Makhādeva's good custom led to the Brahmā world; the custom he himself founded, the noble eightfold path, leads to Nibbāna.

Ānanda lies down

Disciples · Vinaya, Cūḷavagga XI

The First Council was to begin the next day, and Ānanda, who would recite the suttas, was not yet an arahant. He spent the night in mindfulness of the body. Near dawn he decided to lie down, and as he leaned back, before his head reached the pillow and after his feet had left the ground, his mind was freed.

The path process in “Other processes” runs at the mind door and needs no posture at all: preparation, access, conformity, cleansing (the name change-of-lineage takes for the higher paths), then path and fruition, in the space between standing and lying down.

Mahātissa and the laughing woman

Disciples · Visuddhimagga I §15

On the road to Anurādhapura for alms, the elder Mahātissa met a young wife going home after a quarrel, who laughed out loud on seeing him. He looked up and saw only the bones of her teeth; the perception of foulness he had practised came back to him, and he became an arahant where he stood. Asked by her husband whether a woman had passed, he said he had seen only a heap of bones going along the high road.

Straight after the story the Visuddhimagga explains restraint by the cognitive process of “The mango and the moment”: when a visible form strikes the eye there arise adverting, eye-consciousness, receiving, investigating and determining, then the javanas. At none of the moments before javana is there restraint or its lack; they arise only at javana. Guarding the doors means guarding the javanas: the Visuddhimagga's city with its gates shut keeps its goods safe even if the inner doors stand open. The process itself is the Manual's eye-door process (CMA IV §6). Restraint of the senses is the second of the four kinds of purified virtue (CMA IX §28), and the Visuddhimagga's placement of the story there is its own; calling it the ground of insight is the site's framing.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Insight looks at the same dhammas the Abhidhamma lists. What changes when a list becomes something seen?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Impermanence, suffering and non-self are seen in present dhammas, not inferred about them. Knowing their rise and fall replaces knowing about it. The list stays useful as a map of where to look.
