Javana

impulsion

The active phase of a cognitive process, where volition becomes kamma (except in an arahant).

In brief

  • Javana, or impulsion, is the active phase of a process of knowing: usually seven moments of one kind that run over the object.
  • Here the moment's ethical quality is fixed, and only here is new kamma made, except in an arahant, whose javanas are merely functional.
  • The moments before it, such as seeing and determining, are results of past kamma or mere functions, and they make no choice.
  • Which kind of javana follows depends on conditions, above all on wise or unwise attention, and on habit and company.
  • In an arahant, one fully awakened, the javanas are merely functional and leave no result.
  • So the javana is where training can make a difference, though the javanas too arise from conditions.

What it is

Only here is kamma made, by worldlings and trainees; in an arahant the javanas are merely functional (kiriyakiriya · functional a citta that neither makes kamma nor results from it, such as adverting) and leave no result. It is here that wise or unwise attention (yoniso / ayoniso manasikāra) bears fruit, so the javana is where training can make a difference, though the javanas too arise from conditions.

In depth

How the Manual presents it

Javana ("running", "impulsion") is the phase in which the mind runs over the object it has determined. Fifty-five cittas can perform it (III §9). They are the 12 unwholesome; the 21 wholesome; the 4 fruitions; and the 18 functionals, which are all the functionals except the two adverting cittas.

How many javanas run depends on the process (IV §21–22). In a sense-sphere process there are normally seven, or six with a very weak object, and five in the feeble process at the moment of dying. The commentators add that five also run in a faint (Guide to IV §21). The Buddha's reviewing at the Twin Miracle ran four or five. A beginner's first absorption runs a single jhāna javana, and direct knowledge always runs one. A path lasts one moment and is followed by two or three fruitions. In attainments such as jhāna and fruition, javanas may follow one another without set limit. Entering cessation, the fourth immaterial javana runs twice before consciousness stops (IV §22).

Javana and kamma

Here, for worldlings and trainees, volition becomes kamma. The arahant's javanas are functional (kiriyakiriya · functional a citta that neither makes kamma nor results from it, such as adverting) and make none. The fruitions are javanas too, but as resultants they make none either. In a sense-sphere process the javanas are all of one type. In an absorption or path process, three or four preliminary sense-sphere javanas give way to a sublime or supramundane one (IV §14). A mundane javana followed by another of the same kind, wholesome, unwholesome or functional, supports it by repetition (āsevanaāsevana · repetition Repetition is the way each javana strengthens the next, as a lesson is learned by reciting it.); the last preliminary javana so supports even the path. A path gives none, since what follows it is fruition, a resultant (Guide to VIII §13).

The commentaries, as the Guide reports, tie the timing of kamma's results to a javana's position (Guide to V §20). The first javana, the weakest of the seven, can ripen only in this life, or else it lapses. The seventh, the next weakest, can ripen only in the next life. The middle five can ripen in any life after the next, whenever they find the chance, and never lapse while the round lasts.

Where the room for choice lies

The cittas up to and including determining are results or functionals; they make no choice. Which kind of javana follows depends on conditions, above all on whether attention is wise or unwise (yoniso / ayoniso manasikāra), and on habit, background and company. These work as natural decisive support (VIII §17). A desirable object, the result of good kamma, can be met with greed, or with generosity and understanding.

The same rules say why bare will cannot simply seize the javana. Attention itself arises from conditions, and it is training and good company that make wise attention more likely.

Common misunderstandings

"Seven like javanas" does not mean seven separate decisions; they are one type, repeated. Javana is not slow deliberation either: a whole process is far quicker than reflection. Not every javana is ethically loaded. In an arahant all are functional, and the smile-producing citta is a rootless functional javana that only arahants have (I §10).

The term as a technical stage of the process is Abhidhamma and commentarial, not sutta usage. See the readings The mango and the moment, Two moments, side by side and Other processes.

Stories that show it

Bāhiya: in the seen, only the seen

Disciples · Ud 1.10

Bāhiya stopped the Buddha on his alms round, begging to be taught. The Buddha said: “In the seen there will be only the seen, in the heard only the heard, in the sensed only the sensed, in the cognized only the cognized.” Bāhiya became an arahant there and then, and was killed by a cow soon after.

“The mango and the moment” shows where the instruction bites, and the Udāna commentary reads it the same way: let the javanas stay at the measure of eye-consciousness, which neither lusts, hates nor is deluded. Seeing itself, at moment 5, is a result and makes no kamma. Liking, disliking and “I” enter only in the javanas.

Nanda, guardian of the doors

Disciples · Ud 3.2; AN 8.9; AN 1.230

The Buddha's half-brother Nanda was ordained on his wedding day and pined for his bride. In time he became an arahant, and the Buddha named him foremost among those who guard the sense doors: whichever way Nanda looked, he looked with full awareness, so that longing and grief could not flow in.

The Buddha met Nanda's desire where it was and led it past itself: once free, Nanda wanted nothing of the promise. The doors he then learned to guard are the doors of “The mango and the moment”. Guarding them means the seeing is not followed by unwholesome javanas.

Makhādeva's first grey hair

Jātaka · Ja 9

King Makhādeva told his barber to report the first grey hair. When it came, the king held it in his hand, saw death approaching, handed the kingdom to his son and went forth.

A small object can carry a large process: the same grey hair can stir vanity or urgency (saṃvega), depending on how it is attended to (“The mango and the moment”). MN 83 adds the Buddha's own point: Makhādeva's good custom led to the Brahmā world; the custom he himself founded, the noble eightfold path, leads to Nibbāna.

How a story is read here is a study aid, not the tradition's own reading.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Seeing is the result of past kamma, but the javanas make new kamma. Where is the room for choice in a moment of seeing?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Not in the seeing, nor in adverting and determining, which only turn. The room is in the javanas, shaped by how attention turned at adverting and determining, and by habit. Wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) is what makes the same mango an occasion for clarity instead of greed.
