Kamma

volition

Volition, and the way it ripens.

In brief

  • Kamma is volition (cetanā), the intention behind an act, not the act's outward effect and not its result.
  • The volition ceases at once but leaves a potency that ripens later, when conditions allow, as moments of experience such as seeing and as matter in the body.
  • The Manual sorts kamma four ways: by its function, by the order in which it ripens, by when it ripens and by the plane where it ripens.
  • Results follow the quality of the volition, its roots and its understanding, so a gift given with understanding can bring a better rebirth.
  • Kamma is not fate: not everything comes from past kamma, it is one condition among many, and each present volition is new.
  • Arahants, the fully awakened, make no new kamma, though kamma made earlier can still ripen while they live.

What it is

Kamma is volition (cetanācetanā · volition the willing that organises the other factors; as kamma, it bears results): “It is intention, monks, that I call kamma” (AN 6.63). The volition ceases at once, but it leaves a potency in the mental continuum that ripens, when conditions allow, as resultant consciousness (vipākavipāka · resultant a citta that is the ripening of past kamma; it makes no new kamma) and kamma-born matter. Kamma is not fate: it is one condition among many, and each present volition is new. The Manual sorts it four ways: by function, by order of ripening, by time of ripening and by place of ripening.

In a picture

A lump of salt in a cup, and in the Ganges

From the canon · AN 3.100

Kamma is not a fixed price. The same deed ripens differently depending on the person who did it and what else they have become.

Suppose someone said that however a person acts, he feels the result in just that measure. If that were so, the Buddha said, there would be no point in living the holy life, and no chance of ever ending suffering. But if a deed is felt in the way it is fit to be felt, then the holy life has a purpose, and suffering can be ended.

Take two people who do the same small bad deed. The first has not developed body, virtue, mind or wisdom; he is a small person, with little to him, and lives in misery. For him that small deed leads to hell. The second has developed all of these; he is not small but great, and lives without measure. For him the same deed is felt here, in this life, and not the least trace of it shows, let alone more.

It is like a lump of salt. Drop it into a small cup of water and the water can no longer be drunk. Drop the same lump into the river Ganges and the river is as drinkable as before.

In the same discourse the Buddha gives two more pictures. A poor man may be put in chains over half a coin, a coin or a hundred, where a rich man would not be. And a sheep-owner or butcher may beat, bind or fine a poor man who steals a sheep from him, but not a rich man, a king or a king's minister: him he can only beg, with joined hands, to give the sheep back or pay for it.

Where the picture stops working: It can sound as if a good life washes bad deeds away. It does not: in the sutta the small deed is still felt. What changes is how far it reaches, because results depend on many supporting and obstructing conditions (CMA V §§18–20 with the Guide). The commentary takes the developed person to be an arahant, who has no later life in which an old deed could ripen, but who still meets the results of past deeds in this one (AN commentary, CS §101).

In depth

How the Manual presents it

The Manual sorts kamma four ways (V §18–21). By function it is productive, supportive, obstructive or destructive. By order of ripening it is weighty, death-proximate, habitual or reserve. The weighty kinds are, on the unwholesome side, the five heinous crimes and fixed wrong view, and on the wholesome side, jhāna. By time it ripens in this life, in the next, at some later time, or becomes defunct. By place it is unwholesome, sense-sphere wholesome, fine-material wholesome or immaterial wholesome.

Kamma by door and its results

Unwholesome kamma runs along ten courses (V §22). By body: killing, stealing and sexual misconduct. By speech: false speech, slander, harsh speech and idle chatter. By mind: covetousness, ill will and wrong view. V §23 sorts these by root: killing, harsh speech and ill will spring from hatred; sexual misconduct, covetousness and wrong view from greed; the rest from either. Sense-sphere wholesome kamma has ten bases of merit (V §24). They are giving, virtue, meditation, reverence, service, sharing merit, rejoicing in others' merit, hearing the Dhamma, teaching it and straightening one's views.

Results follow grade as well as kind (V §29). Superior three-rooted wholesome kamma, done with a clean mind before and after, gives a three-rooted rebirth. Inferior three-rooted kamma, or superior two-rooted, gives a two-rooted rebirth. Inferior two-rooted kamma gives a rootless rebirth. The jhānas give rebirth in the matching Brahmā planes, graded limited, medium or superior (V §31).

Distinctions that matter

Kamma is the volition, not the deed's outward effect, and not its result (vipākavipāka · resultant a citta that is the ripening of past kamma; it makes no new kamma). Seeing a pleasant sight is a result; how one meets it in the javanas is new kamma. Volition is present in every citta, but it is kamma only in wholesome and unwholesome ones. Strictly, the three mental courses are the factors themselves: covetousness is greed, ill will is hatred, wrong view is the factor of wrong view. The other seven are the volition that drives the act (Guide to V §23). As a condition it is either conascent or asynchronous (VIII §14). The position of a javana sets when its kamma can ripen (Guide to V §20).

Common misunderstandings

Not everything is the result of past kamma. SN 36.21 names bile, phlegm, wind, their combination, climate, careless conduct and assault alongside it. The commentaries count kamma as one of five natural orders (niyāmaniyāma · order, law a fixed order of nature; the commentaries count five, among them the order of mind, which the cognitive process follows), with temperature, seeds, mind and the Dhamma.

Kamma is not fate: present volition is always new, and one kamma can be blocked or cut off by another. Arahants make no new kamma. Kamma they made earlier can still ripen while they live (Guide to V §20), and the commentaries tell Moggallāna's violent death this way. See the readings Kamma, death and rebirth and From kamma to its results.

Stories that show it

The last line of every Jātaka

Jātaka · Jātaka, closing identification

Each Jātaka ends by naming who was who: “At that time, so-and-so was the king, and I myself was the ascetic.” One continuity is followed across many lives as if it were one person.

Conventional truth at work (“A chariot of parts”). Ultimately there is only a stream of cittas and matter, each moment conditioning the next. “The Bodhisatta” names the stream: in Nāgasena's words to King Milinda, neither the same person, nor a different one.

Khantivādi, the teacher of patience

Jātaka · Ja 313

A drunken king, Kalābu, asked an ascetic what he taught. “Patience,” he said. To test him, the king had his hands, feet, nose and ears cut off. The ascetic felt no anger and wished the king long life. Leaving, the king was swallowed by the earth.

“An ensemble of fifty-two” at its sharpest: non-hatred (adosaadosa · non-hatred Non-hatred is the mind's not opposing its object: friendliness and patience, not only the absence of ill will.) and hatred (dosadosa · hatred aversion, from irritation to rage; root of two cittas, always with displeasure) never share a citta. The king's fate returns in “Kamma, death and rebirth” as kamma that ripens in this very life.

Rāhula and the mirror

The Buddha's life · MN 61

The Buddha asked his young son Rāhula what a mirror is for. “For reflection, sir.” “In the same way,” said the Buddha, “reflect before, during and after every action of body, speech and mind: does it lead to harm for myself, for others, or both?”

Reflection is a discipline for the javanas, where volition becomes kamma (“The mango and the moment” and “Kamma, death and rebirth”).

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Kamma is volition. Why does the same gift bear different results when given with or without understanding?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Results follow the volition and what accompanies it: its roots, knowledge, and the clarity before and after the act. A gift given with understanding comes from a three-rooted citta, which can bring a three-rooted rebirth.
