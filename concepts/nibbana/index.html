Nibbāna

unconditioned

The one unconditioned reality, known in the moments of awakening called path and fruition.

In brief

  • Nibbāna is the one unconditioned reality: it does not arise, stand or cease, and nothing produces it.
  • It is known directly in the moments of awakening called path and fruition, and also by change-of-lineage just before the first path and by the reviewing that follows each path.
  • The suttas call it the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion; the commentaries read this as a figure, naming Nibbāna by the ending that depends on it.
  • It is spoken of with residue, while the body and mind of an arahant, one fully awakened, remain, and without residue, when they cease; it is one Nibbāna either way.
  • It is not annihilation, not a place, not a consciousness and not a concept; the path realises it but does not make it.
  • The reading Nibbāna, the unconditioned takes these points in turn, with where teachers differ.

What it is

Nibbāna is the one unconditioned element (asaṅkhata-dhātuasaṅkhata-dhātu · unconditioned element Nibbāna, the one element not made by conditions). It does not arise, stand or cease, and nothing produces it; yet it serves as object for path and fruition consciousness, which know it directly, and for change-of-lineage and reviewing. The commentaries give its characteristic as peace (santisanti · peace peace; the characteristic of Nibbāna (Vism XVI §556)). It is described by negation, as the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion (SN 38.1; the commentaries take this as a figure: Nibbāna is that on which their ending depends, Vism XVI §559), and by image, as the deathless (amataamata · the deathless the deathless; a name of Nibbāna in the suttas (SN 43.25)). It is ultimate, not a concept, yet it lies outside the five aggregates and outside past, present and future. It is spoken of with residue (sa-upādisesasa-upādisesa · with residue the Nibbāna element with residue: the defilements ended while an arahant's aggregates remain (It 44; VI §31)), while the aggregates of an arahant remain, and without residue (anupādisesaanupādisesa · without residue the Nibbāna element without residue: named by the absence of aggregates after an arahant's last citta (It 44; VI §31 with the Guide; Vism XVI §561)), when they cease (It 44).

In depth

How the Manual presents it

The Manual treats Nibbāna briefly, at the end of the chapter on matter (VI §30–32). It is supramundane, to be realised by the knowledge of the four paths, and it is the object of paths and fruitions. The Manual explains its name as a departure (nikkhanta) from craving, which entangles (vāna). The Guide notes that the commentators prefer this derivation to the etymological one, "blowing out" of the fires.

By its own nature it is one. By the basis of distinction it is two: with residue, while an arahant's aggregates remain, and without residue, after his death. The commentaries call these the extinction of the defilements and the extinction of the aggregates. By aspect it is three: void (suññatasuññata · void void; an aspect of Nibbāna itself (VI §31), and, with signless and desireless, one of the three emancipations named after the insight that leads to the path), signless (animittaanimitta · signless without sign; an aspect of Nibbāna (VI §31), and the emancipation reached through the contemplation of impermanence) and desireless (appaṇihitaappaṇihita · desireless without hankering; an aspect of Nibbāna (VI §31), and the emancipation reached through the contemplation of suffering) (VI §31). These three name Nibbāna itself; the three doors of insight with the same names are contemplations of the conditioned (IX §35). The summary verse calls it deathless, endless, unconditioned and unsurpassed (VI §32).

Where it sits in the system

Nibbāna is one of the four ultimates, but it is no aggregate (VII §40). Among the bases and elements it belongs to the mental-object base and the mental-object element (VII §§36–37, 39). It is free of time (kālavimuttakālavimutta · free of time free of time: said of Nibbāna, which never arises or passes, and of concepts, which have no nature of their own (III §17 with the Guide), III §17 with the Guide): what it is has no arising, change or ceasing, whereas a concept stands outside time because it has no nature of its own to arise. It is called "name" (nāmanāma · mind mind: citta with its factors, which “bend” toward objects; the word also means “name”, from a root meaning "to bend") only in the sense that it makes things bend toward it: as objective predominance it draws the supramundane cittas toward itself (Guide to VIII §29).

It is a condition in three ways, all of them as object: as object, as object predominance and as object decisive support (VIII §17, §19; Table 8.3). It is never conditioned (Guide to VIII §1). Its knowers are the eight supramundane cittas. Just before the first path, change-of-lineage (gotrabhūgotrabhū · change-of-lineage the javana that leaves the sense-sphere lineage for jhāna, or turns to Nibbāna), a sense-sphere citta with knowledge, takes it as object; before the higher paths the same moment is called cleansing (vodānavodāna · cleansing the name of the change-of-lineage javana in the processes of the three higher paths) (IX §34 with the Guide). After each path, reviewing knowledge looks back on it in an ordinary mind-door process, mind-door adverting included (IX §34; Guide to III §18; PaṭṭhānaPaṭṭhāna the seventh Abhidhamma book: relates all dhammas by 24 conditions, Kusalattika, CS §410). A worldling short of the path can know of it only by inference (Vibhāvinī on VI §30).

The commentarial picture

The VisuddhimaggaVisuddhimagga · Path of Purification Buddhaghosa's fifth-century manual of practice and doctrine; source of Plates 12–13 gives its characteristic as peace (santisanti · peace peace; the characteristic of Nibbāna (Vism XVI §556)), its function as not passing away, or comforting, and its manifestation as the signless, or freedom from proliferation (Vism XVI §556). It then argues that Nibbāna is not mere absence: if it were, right practice would be fruitless; and it is not mere destruction, for arahantship too is described as destruction, and a destruction would be temporary and conditioned. The suttas call Nibbāna destruction figuratively, since it is the decisive support for that ending (Vism XVI §§557–559; SN 38.1–38.2). The path reaches it but does not produce it (§560), and it is one goal for all who reach it (§561). Later manuals add that, if Nibbāna were destruction, change-of-lineage and the path would have no object (Abhidhammāvatāra ch. 11), and argue by inference that something other than formations and concepts must be the object that cuts defilements (Vibhāvinī on VI §30).

The Milindapañha compares it to the wind, real though no one can show its shape or colour. The suttas speak of it plainly: there is an unborn, unmade, unconditioned; were there not, no escape from the born would be known (Ud 8.3; It 43).

Common misunderstandings

Nibbāna is not annihilation. Without residue, the aggregates simply arise no more, and no self was there to be destroyed. It is not a place, a heaven or a universal consciousness, for it is not a citta and lies outside all thirty-one planes. Nor is it a concept: concepts are exactly what is left once the four ultimates, Nibbāna among them, have been counted (VIII §29). And the path does not produce it. The path realises it (sacchikātabba); it was not made. Nor is it the attainment of cessation, which the VisuddhimaggaVisuddhimagga · Path of Purification Buddhaghosa's fifth-century manual of practice and doctrine; source of Plates 12–13 calls neither conditioned nor unconditioned (Vism XXIII §884).

For how it is reached, see the readings The tree of purification and Four noble persons; for the whole account, Nibbāna, the unconditioned.

    Stories that show it

    The last line of every Jātaka

    Jātaka · Jātaka, closing identification

    Each Jātaka ends by naming who was who: “At that time, so-and-so was the king, and I myself was the ascetic.” One continuity is followed across many lives as if it were one person.

    Conventional truth at work (“A chariot of parts”). Ultimately there is only a stream of cittas and matter, each moment conditioning the next. “The Bodhisatta” names the stream: in Nāgasena's words to King Milinda, neither the same person, nor a different one.

    Vakkali and the body

    Disciples · SN 22.87

    The monk Vakkali longed to keep looking at the Buddha. The Buddha said: “Enough, Vakkali. What is there to see in this foul body? Whoever sees the Dhamma sees me.”

    The commentary takes this Dhamma as the ninefold supramundane Dhamma; the sutta goes straight on to teach him the five aggregates, impermanent and not self. Seen that way, even “the Buddha” is a convention for aggregates, the shift “A chariot of parts” asks of you.

    Nun Vajirā's chariot

    Disciples · SN 5.10

    Māra asked the nun Vajirā who had created “this being”. She answered: “Why do you assume a being? This is a heap of formations. As the word ‘chariot’ is used when the parts are assembled, so ‘a being’ is the convention when the aggregates are present.”

    Her reply is the hinge of “A chariot of parts”: the five aggregates underneath, the convention “a being” on top of them.

    Reflect and discuss

    Opening question

    Nibbāna is unconditioned and is not a citta. How can a citta take it as its object?

    One way to see it, after you have tried

    Change-of-lineage, path, fruition and reviewing cittas take Nibbāna as their object. They know it; they do not produce it. It conditions them as object, as predominant object and as object decisive support, without itself being conditioned by anything.
