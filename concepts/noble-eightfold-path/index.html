The noble eightfold path

ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga

Eight factors, right view to right concentration. In the strict sense, eight mental factors arising together in the moment of a supramundane path.

In brief

  • The noble eightfold path has eight factors, from right view to right concentration, grouped as wisdom, virtue and concentration.
  • In the strict sense they are mental factors arising together in the moment of a supramundane path.
  • Before that moment, in daily practice, the factors arise a few at a time, at different moments.
  • Above the first jhāna level the path has seven factors, since right intention is then absent.
  • Right speech, action and livelihood are abstinences: the refraining from wrong speech, action and livelihood.

What it is

The noble eightfold path (ariya aṭṭhaṅgika maggaariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga · noble eightfold path right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration; in the strict sense, eight mental factors arising together in a supramundane path citta) is the fourth truth: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration. The suttas gather them into three trainings: virtue, concentration and wisdom (MN 44). The Theravāda Abhidhamma reads each factor as a mental factor (cetasikacetasika · mental factor a mental factor: arises and ceases with a citta, sharing its object and base): wisdom, initial application, the three abstinences, energy, mindfulness and one-pointedness. The factors can be mundane, developed a few at a time, or supramundane. In the strict sense the path of the four truths is all eight arising together in one supramundane path citta, with Nibbāna as object.

In depth

How the Manual presents it

Among the requisites of enlightenment the Manual lists the eight path factors and names the mental factor behind each (VII §30 and Guide; VII §32 and Table 7.3). Right view is wisdom. Right intention is initial application, turned to renunciation, goodwill and harmlessness. Right speech, right action and right livelihood are the three abstinences (II §6). Right effort is energy, working as the four supreme efforts. Right mindfulness is mindfulness, as the four foundations. Right concentration is one-pointedness, defined by the four jhānas.

In its mixed categories the Manual counts twelve path factors: these eight and four wrong ones, wrong view, wrong intention, wrong effort and wrong concentration (VII §17 and Guide). They come to nine mental factors, since the last three wrong ones are initial application, energy and one-pointedness in unwholesome company, and only wrong view is a factor of its own. Path factors arise only in rooted cittas (VII §22).

Of their occurrence the Manual says that in the mundane the path factors arise according to circumstances, in the course of the six purifications, and that in the supramundane they arise together, with right intention dropping out above the first jhāna (VII §33; Guide to VII §§32–33). Only those supramundane eight are the fourth noble truth (Guide to VII §38).

Four senses of “path”

The word "path" has four senses here, and the Abhidhamma keeps them apart. First, the twelve path factors of the mixed categories, right and wrong. Wrong view and its companions are a path too, a road to the woeful planes (Guide to VII §17). Second, the path among the requisites of enlightenment: the eight right factors, mundane or supramundane (VII §§30, 33). Third, the fourth noble truth: the eight only as they arise in the four supramundane path cittas (Guide to VII §38). Fourth, the path citta itself (maggamagga · path (citta) a supramundane citta that arises once, cutting defilements, with Nibbāna as object), taken as a whole. On the Manual's strict count, apart from its eight factors it stands outside the four truths, and so do the four fruitions (VII §40; Guide to VII §40).

It follows that mundane right view and mundane right effort belong, in the teaching of the noble truths, to the truth of suffering (Guide to VII §38; Vism XVI §564). They are still the practice. As realities they are among what is to be understood.

Before and at the path

In ordinary wholesome moments the factors come a few at a time. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī already counts a five-factored path in the first wholesome citta of the sense sphere: view, intention, effort, mindfulness and concentration (Dhs §89). The abstinences arise in the mundane only in the eight great wholesome cittas, only when one actually refrains, one at a time, and only for the kind of wrong in view; they do not arise in mundane jhāna or in an arahant's functional cittas (II §6 and §15 with the Guides). The commentaries call this gradual development the preliminary path (pubbabhāga-maggapubbabhāga-magga · preliminary path a commentarial name for the path factors as developed in ordinary moments, before the supramundane path).

At the path moment the factors are together in one citta, on one object, Nibbāna. All three abstinences are present, and each works in full, cutting the disposition to every form of its wrong (Guide to II §15). A path at the level of the first jhāna has all eight. From the second jhāna up, initial application is absent and seven remain (Guide to VII §§32–33; II §19). One who reaches the path by bare insight, without jhāna, has a path at the first-jhāna level (Guide to I §§30–31), and so, the commentaries say, an eightfold one (Vism XXI §798). The same factors recur in fruition, as results (Guide to II §15), but fruition is not the fourth truth (Guide to VII §40).

Common misunderstandings

The eight are not steps done one after another. They are factors, and at the path they arise at once. Right speech is not pleasant talk: as a path factor it is the abstinence from wrong speech. The Abhidhamma counts no factor of wrong mindfulness and no separate factors of wrong speech, action or livelihood, though the suttas use those names (MN 117), so its wrong path factors number four (Guide to VII §17). Good habits are not the fourth truth: they belong to the mundane path, which in the teaching of the noble truths counts with suffering. See the readings The noble eightfold path and The four noble truths.

Stories that show it

The whole of the holy life

Disciples · SN 45.2

Ānanda said that good friendship is half of the holy life. “Not so, Ānanda,” said the Buddha. “Good friendship is the whole of the holy life.” A monk with a good friend can be expected to develop the noble eightfold path.

In “The noble eightfold path” the path, in the strict sense, is eight factors in one citta at the moment of the path; this story is about how they come to arise at all. A good friend is a natural decisive support from outside.

How a story is read here is a study aid, not the tradition's own reading.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

If the noble path in the strict sense lasts one moment, what is someone who 'practises the eightfold path' doing?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Developing the same factors in ordinary moments, a few at a time, through the purifications of virtue, mind and wisdom (VII §33). The commentaries call this the preliminary path. In the teaching of the noble truths it still counts under suffering, but it is what matures into the one moment where all eight arise together.
