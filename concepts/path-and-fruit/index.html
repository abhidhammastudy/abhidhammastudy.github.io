Path and fruition

four paths

The four moments of awakening that cut defilements for good, each followed by its fruition.

In brief

  • Path and fruition are the moments of awakening that take Nibbāna, the unconditioned, as their object.
  • There are four paths, stream-entry, once-return, non-return and arahantship, and each arises only once and cuts certain defilements for good.
  • Each path lasts a single moment and is followed at once by two or three moments of its fruition, on the same object.
  • The stream-enterer ends wrong view and doubt first; greed and hatred are weakened and then removed only by the later paths.
  • Noble persons can later re-enter their fruition as an absorption with Nibbāna as its object.
  • With the fourth path the round of kamma and rebirth is cut at its roots, ignorance and craving.

What it is

Path and fruition (magga, phala) are the supramundane (lokuttaralokuttara · supramundane beyond the world: the four paths, four fruitions and Nibbāna) cittas that take Nibbāna as object. Each of the four paths arises only once and abandons defilements for good (samuccheda-pahānasamuccheda-pahāna · abandoning by cutting off the path's way of abandoning a defilement: for good, never to arise again): stream-entry, once-return, non-return, arahantship. Fruition follows at once, on the same object. With the fourth path the round of kamma and rebirth is cut at its roots, ignorance and craving.

In depth

How the Manual presents it

There are eight supramundane cittas: four paths, which are wholesome, and four fruitions, which are resultant. Counted by the jhāna on which each rests, they become forty (I §26–28, §30–31).

In the path process (IV §14; IX §34), two or three insight moments of preparation, access and conformity come first. Then change-of-lineage, taking Nibbāna as object, crosses from the worldling's lineage to the noble one's. The path follows for a single moment. Two or three moments of fruition come next, three for one of keen faculties, whose preparation moment drops out. Then the mind falls back into the life-continuum, and reviewing follows. Reviewing looks back over path, fruition and Nibbāna, and often, though not always, over what has been abandoned and what is still left (IX §34).

What each path cuts

In one moment the path does four tasks (IX §34). It fully understands suffering, abandons its origin, realises cessation and develops the path. Its path factors arise together in that one moment, the three abstinences among them (II §15). All eight are there only in a path at the level of the first jhāna; at higher levels right intention, which is initial application, drops out, and seven remain (VII §33; II §19). A bare-insight path is at the first-jhāna level (Guide to I §§30–31), and so, the commentaries say, eightfold (Vism XXI §798).

The four persons follow from what is cut (IX §38–41, Table 9.3). The stream-enterer cuts identity view, doubt and clinging to rules and rituals. He is free of the woeful planes and has at most seven more lives, and he is of three kinds: seven-times-at-most, family-to-family and one-seed. Of the fourteen unwholesome factors he has removed wrong view and doubt, and, say the commentaries, envy and avarice. The once-returner weakens lust, hatred and delusion. The non-returner removes sensual lust and ill will entirely, and with them hatred, worry and greed for sense objects; if he dies before arahantship, he is reborn in a fine-material world. The arahant cuts the five higher fetters: lust for fine-material and for immaterial existence, conceit, restlessness and ignorance.

Fruition and cessation

Each noble person can re-enter his own fruition, an absorption with Nibbāna as object (IX §42). The attainment of cessation is open only to non-returners and arahants who have mastered all eight jhānas. It cannot be entered in the immaterial worlds. The meditator ascends through the jhānas, contemplating each with insight up to the base of nothingness, then attends to four preliminary duties (pubbakicca); the last is not a command over death but a check that his life will last the seven days (Guide to IX §43; Vism XXIII §874, §878). Then two javanas of neither-perception-nor-non-perception run, and mind stops. On emergence a single moment of non-return or arahant fruition arises (IX §43–44; IV §22).

Common misunderstandings

A path never repeats. Each arises only once in the whole of saṃsāra, and its work is permanent abandoning (samuccheda-pahānasamuccheda-pahāna · abandoning by cutting off the path's way of abandoning a defilement: for good, never to arise again). Stream-entry leaves greed and hatred in place, except in the grade that could lead to a woeful rebirth (Vism XXII §830): it ends views before passions. The path does not create Nibbāna; it realises it. The names void, signless and desireless come from the insight that led to the path (IX §36); by object and quality every path and fruit has all three (IX §37), since Nibbāna itself is void, signless and desireless (VI §31). The reviewing count of nineteen, five for each of the first three paths and four for the arahant, who has nothing left to review, is commentarial (Guide to IX §34). See the readings Four noble persons, The four noble truths and The noble eightfold path.

Stories that show it

Makhādeva's first grey hair

Jātaka · Ja 9

King Makhādeva told his barber to report the first grey hair. When it came, the king held it in his hand, saw death approaching, handed the kingdom to his son and went forth.

A small object can carry a large process: the same grey hair can stir vanity or urgency (saṃvega), depending on how it is attended to (“The mango and the moment”). MN 83 adds the Buddha's own point: Makhādeva's good custom led to the Brahmā world; the custom he himself founded, the noble eightfold path, leads to Nibbāna.

Ānanda lies down

Disciples · Vinaya, Cūḷavagga XI

The First Council was to begin the next day, and Ānanda, who would recite the suttas, was not yet an arahant. He spent the night in mindfulness of the body. Near dawn he decided to lie down, and as he leaned back, before his head reached the pillow and after his feet had left the ground, his mind was freed.

The path process in “Other processes” runs at the mind door and needs no posture at all: preparation, access, conformity, cleansing (the name change-of-lineage takes for the higher paths), then path and fruition, in the space between standing and lying down.

The night of awakening

The Buddha's life · MN 36; Ud 1.1–1.3

Under the Bodhi tree, in the first watch of the night he recalled his past lives; in the second he saw beings pass away and reappear according to their kamma; in the third he realised the destruction of the taints. In the days that followed he reviewed dependent origination forward and in reverse.

The three watches map onto the readings: past lives across the planes (“Thirty-one planes”), beings reborn by kamma and its fruit (“Kamma, death and rebirth”), and the wheel with its ending (“The wheel across three lives” and “The tree of purification”).

How a story is read here is a study aid, not the tradition's own reading.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

A path lasts a single moment, yet it changes a person for good. How can one moment do so much?

One way to see it, after you have tried

The path citta cuts defilements that would otherwise go on conditioning later cittas. Its effect is what never arises again. Fruition follows at once, taking the same Nibbāna as its object.
