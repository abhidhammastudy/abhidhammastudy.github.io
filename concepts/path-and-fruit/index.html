Path and fruition

four paths

The four moments of awakening that cut defilements for good, each followed by its fruition.

In brief

  • Path and fruition are the moments of awakening that take Nibbāna, the unconditioned, as their object.
  • There are four paths, stream-entry, once-return, non-return and arahantship, and each arises only once and cuts certain defilements for good.
  • Each path lasts a single moment and is followed at once by two or three moments of its fruition, on the same object.
  • The stream-enterer ends wrong view and doubt first; greed and hatred are weakened and then removed only by the later paths.
  • Noble persons can later re-enter their fruition as an absorption with Nibbāna as its object.
  • With the fourth path the round of kamma and rebirth is cut at its roots, ignorance and craving.

What it is

Path and fruition (magga, phala) are the supramundane (lokuttaralokuttara · supramundane beyond the world: the four paths, four fruitions and Nibbāna) cittas that take Nibbāna as object. Each of the four paths arises only once and abandons defilements for good (samuccheda-pahānasamuccheda-pahāna · abandoning by cutting off the path's way of abandoning a defilement: for good, never to arise again): stream-entry, once-return, non-return, arahantship. Fruition follows at once, on the same object. With the fourth path the round of kamma and rebirth is cut at its roots, ignorance and craving.

In a picture

The leap, the moon and the archer

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXII, CS §§807–809

What each moment at the threshold of the path does: conformity clears the way, change-of-lineage sees Nibbāna for the first time, and the path breaks through greed, hatred and delusion never broken before.

The leap. A man wants to land on the far side of a wide ditch. He runs, grabs a rope or pole hanging from a tree on this side, springs up, swings across leaning towards the far bank, lets go when he is above it, and lands there, a little unsteadily, until he steadies himself. The Visuddhimagga says the run is the knowledge of rise and fall and the insights after it; the rope or pole is one of the aggregates, seen as impermanent, suffering or not-self. The spring, the lean and arriving above the far bank are the three conformity moments. Letting go and landing is change-of-lineage, the first moment that has Nibbāna as its object. It is unsteady because no moment before it has taken that object, so it has nothing to repeat; what makes him stand firm is the path.

The moon. A man goes out at night to see the moon, but clouds hide it. A first wind blows away the thick clouds, a second the middling ones, a third the thin ones, and then he sees the moon. The winds can clear clouds but cannot see; the man can see but cannot clear clouds. So the conformity moments dispel the darkness that hides the truths but cannot see Nibbāna, and change-of-lineage sees Nibbāna but cannot dispel that darkness.

The archer. An archer stands, his face wrapped in cloth, on a turning wheel, with a hundred planks set up some way off. A helper spins the wheel and, when the archer faces the planks, gives him a sign with a tap of a stick. Not letting go of that signal, the archer shoots, and the arrow goes through all hundred planks. The signal is change-of-lineage; the archer is the path knowledge, which splits masses of greed, hatred and delusion never split before.

Where the picture stops working: The leaper and the archer are each one person all through. At the threshold there is no one: each stage is a different moment of mind, each conditioning the next. The Visuddhimagga speaks of three conformity moments. The Manual names the moments before change-of-lineage preparation, access and conformity, and allows two or three (CMA IX §34); the Guide explains that one of unusually keen faculties skips preparation and has two. And the passage is told of the first path, stream-entry, which does not yet end all greed, hatred and delusion.

Told in: Visuddhimagga XXII (purification by knowledge and vision), CS §§807–809; CMA IX §34 and Guide: the path process

The snake taken for a fish

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §758 (with §791)

Why the meditator who wants to be free turns back and looks at formations again: laying the three characteristics on them is the way to let them go safely, as swinging the snake is the way to be rid of it.

A man who wanted to catch fish set a fish-trap in the water. He put his hand in through the mouth of the trap and, under the water, took hold of a snake by the neck. He was pleased: he had caught a fish, and a big one. He lifted it out to look, and when he saw three svastika marks on it he knew it for a snake, and was afraid. He saw the danger, he was weary of holding it, and he wanted to let it go.

He went about it carefully. He freed his hand from the coils, beginning at the tail, swung the snake two or three times round above his head to tire it out, and threw it away. Then he scrambled up the bank of the pond and stood there looking back the way he had come: he had escaped from the jaws of a great snake.

The Visuddhimagga tells the story for the knowledge of reflection and keys it step by step. Swinging the snake until it is too weak to turn and bite is the meditator laying the three characteristics on formations until they are too weak to present themselves again as permanent, pleasant, beautiful or self; then he can let them go, and let them go well.

Later, among its twelve similes of the insight that leads to emergence, the Visuddhimagga takes up the same story, which it calls the simile of the black snake, and carries it on to the end: flinging the snake away is change-of-lineage, standing looking back along the way he came is the path, and going on to stand in a place where there is nothing to fear is fruition.

Where the picture stops working: The man is in danger from the snake and gets free by force. Insight weakens formations only by seeing them as they are, and the one who holds and lets go is a way of speaking: what holds is clinging, what lets go is a run of moments of knowledge. The man is afraid; the knowledge of fear is not (Vism XXI §751). The story's steps are not wholly separate experiences: the Ancients call fear, danger and disenchantment one knowledge with three names, and the Paṭisambhidāmagga, quoted there, says they are one in meaning and differ only in the letter (Vism XXI §755); the same is said of desire for deliverance, reflection and equanimity (Vism XXI §§778–781). The story has no step for equanimity or conformity: it goes straight from the swinging to the throw. And standing looking back along the way he came looks like reviewing, but the Visuddhimagga keys it to the path; reviewing comes only after fruition (CMA IX §34).

Told in: Visuddhimagga XXI (knowledge of reflection), CS §758; Visuddhimagga XXI (the twelve similes of insight leading to emergence), CS §§789, 791; Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XXI §758 (the trap, the black snake, the unwinding); CMA IX §33 and Guide: the nine insight knowledges; IX §34: change-of-lineage, path and fruition

Water poured where the fire was

From the commentaries · Vibhāvinī on CMA IX §34, CS §59

Fruition cuts nothing new. Right after the path it calms the heat that is left in a mind whose defilements the path has already cut.

The Manual says that after the path two or three moments of fruition consciousness arise and cease, and then the mind sinks back into the life-continuum.

The Vibhāvinī, the commentary on the Manual, pictures those moments. A fire has been carried away, but the ground where it burned is still hot, and water is poured on it from pots to cool it. So the two or three fruition cittas, two or three according to how the path arose, occur in a continuum whose defilements have already been cut off, and calm the distress that remains there.

Where the picture stops working: Someone pours the water, from outside and after a pause. Fruition is the path's own result, arising at once, with no gap and no one doing it (Vism XXII §811; CMA IX §34). Whether there are two moments or three depends on the path process: three for one of keen faculties who skips the moment of preparation, two for others (Guide to CMA IX §34); the Visuddhimagga rejects the view that there can be one or four. The picture covers only these first moments. Fruition can later be entered again and, with practice, held for many moments in the attainment of fruition, which the picture does not show (Guide to CMA I §§26–28; CMA IX §42 and Guide). And "distress" is the Vibhāvinī's word; it does not mean that defilements cut by this path remain.

Told in: Vibhāvinī (Abhidhammatthavibhāvinī) on CMA IX §34, CS §59; Visuddhimagga XXII, CS §811: two or three fruition cittas, the path's immediate result; CMA IX §34 and Guide: the path process; IX §42 and Guide: the attainment of fruition

In depth

How the Manual presents it

There are eight supramundane cittas: four paths, which are wholesome, and four fruitions, which are resultant. Counted by the jhāna on which each rests, they become forty (I §26–28, §30–31).

In the path process (IV §14; IX §34), two or three insight moments of preparation, access and conformity come first. Then change-of-lineage, taking Nibbāna as object, crosses from the worldling's lineage to the noble one's. The path follows for a single moment. Two or three moments of fruition come next, three for one of keen faculties, whose preparation moment drops out. Then the mind falls back into the life-continuum, and reviewing follows. Reviewing looks back over path, fruition and Nibbāna, and often, though not always, over what has been abandoned and what is still left (IX §34).

What each path cuts

In one moment the path does four tasks (IX §34). It fully understands suffering, abandons its origin, realises cessation and develops the path. Its path factors arise together in that one moment, the three abstinences among them (II §15). All eight are there only in a path at the level of the first jhāna; at higher levels right intention, which is initial application, drops out, and seven remain (VII §33; II §19). A bare-insight path is at the first-jhāna level (Guide to I §§30–31), and so, the commentaries say, eightfold (Vism XXI §798).

The four persons follow from what is cut (IX §38–41, Table 9.3). The stream-enterer cuts identity view, doubt and clinging to rules and rituals. He is free of the woeful planes and has at most seven more lives, and he is of three kinds: seven-times-at-most, family-to-family and one-seed. Of the fourteen unwholesome factors he has removed wrong view and doubt, and, say the commentaries, envy and avarice. The once-returner weakens lust, hatred and delusion. The non-returner removes sensual lust and ill will entirely, and with them hatred, worry and greed for sense objects; if he dies before arahantship, he is reborn in a fine-material world. The arahant cuts the five higher fetters: lust for fine-material and for immaterial existence, conceit, restlessness and ignorance.

Fruition and cessation

Each noble person can re-enter his own fruition, an absorption with Nibbāna as object (IX §42). The attainment of cessation is open only to non-returners and arahants who have mastered all eight jhānas. It cannot be entered in the immaterial worlds. The meditator ascends through the jhānas, contemplating each with insight up to the base of nothingness, then attends to four preliminary duties (pubbakicca); the last is not a command over death but a check that his life will last the seven days (Guide to IX §43; Vism XXIII §874, §878). Then two javanas of neither-perception-nor-non-perception run, and mind stops. On emergence a single moment of non-return or arahant fruition arises (IX §43–44; IV §22).

Common misunderstandings

A path never repeats. Each arises only once in the whole of saṃsāra, and its work is permanent abandoning (samuccheda-pahānasamuccheda-pahāna · abandoning by cutting off the path's way of abandoning a defilement: for good, never to arise again). Stream-entry leaves greed and hatred in place, except in the grade that could lead to a woeful rebirth (Vism XXII §830): it ends views before passions. The path does not create Nibbāna; it realises it. The names void, signless and desireless come from the insight that led to the path (IX §36); by object and quality every path and fruit has all three (IX §37), since Nibbāna itself is void, signless and desireless (VI §31). The reviewing count of nineteen, five for each of the first three paths and four for the arahant, who has nothing left to review, is commentarial (Guide to IX §34). See the readings Four noble persons, The four noble truths and The noble eightfold path.

Stories that show it

Makhādeva's first grey hair

Jātaka · Ja 9

King Makhādeva told his barber to report the first grey hair. When it came, the king held it in his hand, saw death approaching, handed the kingdom to his son and went forth.

A small object can carry a large process: the same grey hair can stir vanity or urgency (saṃvega), depending on how it is attended to (“The mango and the moment”). MN 83 adds the Buddha's own point: Makhādeva's good custom led to the Brahmā world; the custom he himself founded, the noble eightfold path, leads to Nibbāna.

Ānanda lies down

Disciples · Vinaya, Cūḷavagga XI

The First Council was to begin the next day, and Ānanda, who would recite the suttas, was not yet an arahant. He spent the night in mindfulness of the body. Near dawn he decided to lie down, and as he leaned back, before his head reached the pillow and after his feet had left the ground, his mind was freed.

The path process in “Other processes” runs at the mind door and needs no posture at all: preparation, access, conformity, cleansing (the name change-of-lineage takes for the higher paths), then path and fruition, in the space between standing and lying down.

Mahātissa and the laughing woman

Disciples · Visuddhimagga I §15

On the road to Anurādhapura for alms, the elder Mahātissa met a young wife going home after a quarrel, who laughed out loud on seeing him. He looked up and saw only the bones of her teeth; the perception of foulness he had practised came back to him, and he became an arahant where he stood. Asked by her husband whether a woman had passed, he said he had seen only a heap of bones going along the high road.

Straight after the story the Visuddhimagga explains restraint by the cognitive process of “The mango and the moment”: when a visible form strikes the eye there arise adverting, eye-consciousness, receiving, investigating and determining, then the javanas. At none of the moments before javana is there restraint or its lack; they arise only at javana. Guarding the doors means guarding the javanas: the Visuddhimagga's city with its gates shut keeps its goods safe even if the inner doors stand open. The process itself is the Manual's eye-door process (CMA IV §6). Restraint of the senses is the second of the four kinds of purified virtue (CMA IX §28), and the Visuddhimagga's placement of the story there is its own; calling it the ground of insight is the site's framing.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

A path lasts a single moment, yet it changes a person for good. How can one moment do so much?

One way to see it, after you have tried

The path citta cuts defilements that would otherwise go on conditioning later cittas. Its effect is what never arises again. Fruition follows at once, taking the same Nibbāna as its object.
