Rūpa

matter

Matter: 28 kinds, always arising in groups of eight or more.

In brief

  • Matter (rūpa) is the physical side of experience: the body, its senses and the objects they meet.
  • Its core is the four great elements, read as qualities: earth as hardness, water as cohesion, fire as heat and air as motion.
  • Twenty-four further kinds are derived from these four, twenty-eight in all, and none of them knows an object.
  • Matter never occurs alone: it arises in inseparable groups of eight or more qualities, from four origins, kamma, mind, temperature and nutriment.
  • A unit of matter lasts seventeen moments of mind, and mind and matter condition each other in set ways.
  • Seeing the body as just elements that arise and break up loosens the hold of the thought “this body is mine”.

What it is

Matter (rūparūpa · matter matter: the third reality, 28 kinds; as an aggregate it is called form) is the physical side of experience: the body, its senses and the objects they meet. The word means what is “afflicted” (ruppati), altered by cold, heat, hunger and thirst. Its core is the four great elements (mahābhūtamahābhūta · great elements earth, water, fire and air: the primary matter all other matter depends on): earth as hardness, water as cohesion, fire as heat, air as motion and support; twenty-four kinds are derived from them (upādā-rūpaupādā-rūpa · derived matter the 24 kinds of matter that depend on the four great elements). Matter knows no object (anārammaṇaanārammaṇa · without object without object; said of Nibbāna in Ud 8.1, which the Udāna commentary reads as: unlike a mind, it takes no object (cf. Kv XIX.6)). It arises in inseparable groups (kalāpakalāpa the smallest group of matter: eight or more kinds arising and ceasing together) of eight or more, from four origins (kamma, citta, temperature, nutriment), and a unit of it lasts seventeen mind-moments.

In depth

How the Manual presents it

There are four great essentials and twenty-four kinds of derived matter, twenty-eight in all (VI §2–5). Eighteen are "concretely produced" (nipphannanipphanna · concrete matter the 18 kinds with a nature of their own, fit objects for insight), with a nature of their own. They are the four elements; the five sensitivities of eye, ear, nose, tongue and body; four objects (visible form, sound, smell and taste); the female and male faculties; the heart-base; the life faculty; and nutritive essence. The tangible is not a separate kind: it is earth, fire and air as touched.

Ten are not concretely produced (anipphannaanipphanna · non-concrete matter the 10 qualities and modes of concrete matter, such as space and decay). They are modes or marks of the others: space; bodily and vocal intimation; lightness, malleability and wieldiness; and the four characteristics of production, continuity, decay and impermanence.

Origins and groups

Matter has four origins (VI §9–15): kamma, citta, temperature (the fire element) and nutriment. Eighteen kinds can be kamma-born, fifteen mind-born, thirteen temperature-born and twelve nutriment-born. The four characteristics come from none of them.

Matter never occurs alone. It arises in groups (kalāpakalāpa the smallest group of matter: eight or more kinds arising and ceasing together), of which the Manual lists twenty-one: nine kamma-born, six mind-born, four temperature-born and two nutriment-born (VI §16–22). Every group holds the eight inseparables (avinibbhogaavinibbhoga · inseparables the eight kinds in every kalāpa: four elements, colour, smell, taste, nutritive essence): the four elements with colour, smell, taste and nutritive essence. Kamma-born matter arises at every sub-moment from rebirth-linking on, and it stops at the seventeenth citta before death. What remains is a temperature-born body (VI §24–25).

A unit of matter lasts seventeen mind-moments. The exceptions are intimation, which lasts one mind-moment, and the four characteristics (Guide to IV §6 and its note).

Distinctions that matter

The elements are qualities, not stuffs. Earth is hardness or softness, water is cohesion, fire is heat or cold, air is motion or support. Water cannot be touched: cohesion is known only by the mind. Matter as a whole is rootless, without an object, mundane and of the sense sphere (VI §6).

The Manual also sorts matter by pairs (VI §7). Internal matter is the five sensitivities. The bases are those five and the heart. The doors are the five and the two intimations. The faculties are the five, the two sex faculties and life. The gross kinds are twelve and the subtle sixteen.

On the heart-base, the canonical Paṭṭhāna says only "that matter" on which mind depends. It is the commentaries that identify it with the heart (Guide to III §20).

Common misunderstandings

Kalāpas are not atoms in the Greek sense: they are not hard bits of stuff, and they cannot be seen. They are groups of qualities that arise and cease together. Nor is matter treated as the opposite of mind: each conditions the other in set ways, through base, object, the postnascence condition and the four origins (VIII §15–16).

The suttas use the analysis as practice: the body is viewed as "just elements", like a butcher at the crossroads who no longer thinks "cow" (MN 10; worked out in Vism XI). See the readings How matter is made and Mind and matter together.

Stories that show it

Pukkusāti and the elements

Disciples · MN 140

One night in a potter's shed, the Buddha taught a stranger, Pukkusāti, to see his body as earth, water, fire, air, space and consciousness, none of it “mine”. Pukkusāti recognised his teacher, but while seeking robes for ordination he was killed by a cow. The Buddha declared him a non-returner.

The four great elements are the core of the 28 kinds of matter in “How matter is made”. Analysing the body into elements is also one of the subjects in “Forty meditation subjects”.

How a story is read here is a study aid, not the tradition's own reading.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Matter knows nothing. Why does a teaching about liberation list 28 kinds of it?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Because experience is partly made of it. The sense bases and their objects are rūpa, and the body is the first ground of mindfulness. Seeing matter arise and break up loosens the hold of "this body is mine".
