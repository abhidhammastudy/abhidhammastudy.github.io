Two truths

conventional, ultimate

Two ways of speaking: of persons and things, and of the ultimate dhammas they resolve into. The starting point of the Abhidhamma.

In brief

  • The Abhidhamma speaks in two registers: conventional truth, which names persons and things, and ultimate truth, which speaks of the realities they resolve into.
  • Ultimate realities (dhammas) have their own nature and cannot be broken down further; the Manual counts four: consciousness, mental factors, matter and Nibbāna.
  • Conventional speech is not false: saying “I” or “my bowl” is no lie, and the Buddha spoke this way throughout the suttas.
  • What the analysis rejects is the belief that such words name a lasting, independent owner.
  • Names and the things they designate are concepts; they are not ultimately real, yet the mind can still take them as objects.
  • Insight starts here, by seeing mind and matter rise and fall in place of “a being”.

What it is

The Abhidhamma speaks in two registers. Conventional truth (sammuti-sacca) names persons, chariots and selves; it is valid for living and speaking, and it is not a lie. Ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca) speaks of dhammasdhamma an ultimate reality, bearing its own nature; not a mere name or concept: phenomena that bear their own nature (sabhāvasabhāva · own nature the nature a dhamma bears by itself, which makes it an ultimate reality rather than a concept) and cannot be analysed further. Names and the things they designate are concepts (paññattipaññatti · concept a concept: a name, or what a name refers to, such as “chariot”) and belong to the first register; the four ultimate realities belong to the second. Moving between them does not deny the world. It redescribes it so that the view of a lasting self (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) finds no ground.

In depth

How the Manual presents it

The Manual opens by naming what the Abhidhamma speaks of, taken at the level of ultimate reality: four things only, citta, cetasika, rūpa and Nibbāna (I §2). Counted out, that is 89 (or 121) kinds of citta, 52 mental factors, 28 kinds of matter and the one Nibbāna. The first three are conditioned; Nibbāna alone is not.

The Guide to I §2 sets the frame. Conventional realities (sammutisammuti · convention conventional truth: speech about persons and things; true, but not ultimate) are what ordinary thought and speech refer to: beings, persons, men and women, and the seemingly stable objects of the everyday world. Ultimate realities (paramatthaparamattha · ultimate what remains after analysis: citta, cetasika, matter and Nibbāna, from parama, highest, and attha, thing) are the dhammas that have a nature of their own (sabhāvasabhāva · own nature the nature a dhamma bears by itself, which makes it an ultimate reality rather than a concept) and cannot be broken down any further. They are ultimate in two senses: as what finally exists, and as what right knowledge finally knows. The Guide likens the analysis to pressing oil out of sesame seed.

Two ways of teaching

Bodhi's Introduction to the Manual ties the two truths to two styles of teaching. The suttas use conventional speech (vohāra) and speak freely of "I", "you" and living beings; this is the figurative teaching (pariyāya-desanā), adapted to its hearers. The Abhidhamma confines itself to dhammas, their characteristics, functions and relations: the literal teaching (nippariyāya-desanā). The Introduction adds that this is a difference of emphasis, not a wall: each Piṭaka uses both registers.

The suttas already know the distinction. Bhikkhunī Vajirā says that "being" is a word for a heap of formations, as "chariot" is for assembled parts (SN 5.10), and AN 2.25 contrasts a teaching whose meaning must be drawn out with one whose meaning is already drawn out. The technical pair sammutisammuti · convention conventional truth: speech about persons and things; true, but not ultimate and paramatthaparamattha · ultimate what remains after analysis: citta, cetasika, matter and Nibbāna is worked out in the Kathāvatthu and above all in the commentaries.

Concepts in their place

Concepts (paññattipaññatti · concept a concept: a name, or what a name refers to, such as “chariot”) are what is left when the four ultimates have been counted (VIII §29). The Guide to §30 sorts the meanings that concepts make known into six kinds: forms such as land and mountain, collections such as house and chariot, directions, times such as morning and month, spaces such as a well or a cave, and the signs of meditation such as the kasiṇa sign. A concept is not real in the ultimate sense, yet it can be the object of a citta (VIII §30). It also conditions mind as object and as decisive support (VIII §17). The counterpart sign of jhāna and the "beings" of loving-kindness are concepts (IX §17–18). The summary verse (VIII §32) shows a word being understood: an ear-door process hears the sound, and later mind-door processes grasp the meaning. The names themselves are sorted a second way, also into six (VIII §31): from "feeling", a name for something real, to "king's son", where neither part is real. For the long story, see the reading Names and the named.

Common misunderstandings

"Conventional" does not mean false. Saying "I" or "my bowl" is not a lie, and the Buddha used such speech throughout the suttas. What the analysis rejects is the belief that the word names a lasting, independent owner.

"Ultimate" does not mean permanent or hidden behind appearances. Every conditioned dhamma lasts a single moment of arising, standing and dissolving. "Own nature" means only that hardness is hardness and feeling is feeling, each with its own mark. It does not mean that a dhamma exists apart from conditions or in all three times. The Theravāda rejected that view, held by the Sarvāstivāda, in the Kathāvatthu (Kv I.6); see the reading Other maps of the same ground. And Nibbāna is ultimate, not a concept, even though it is mostly described by negations.

Why it matters

The Guide to I §2 notes that an untrained mind does not see the ultimates: it sees the everyday shapes that concepts lay over them. Wise attention (yoniso manasikārayoniso manasikāra · wise attention attending in a way that leads to wholesome javanas; unwise attention leads to unwholesome) is what gets beneath them. This is where insight starts. Purification of view (IX §30) is the discerning of mind and matter, nāma and rūpa, in place of "a being". The meditation subjects of calm may be concepts, but the objects of insight must be ultimate, conditioned dhammas, for only those show rise and fall.

Stories that show it

The last line of every Jātaka

Jātaka · Jātaka, closing identification

Each Jātaka ends by naming who was who: “At that time, so-and-so was the king, and I myself was the ascetic.” One continuity is followed across many lives as if it were one person.

Conventional truth at work (“A chariot of parts”). Ultimately there is only a stream of cittas and matter, each moment conditioning the next. “The Bodhisatta” names the stream: in Nāgasena's words to King Milinda, neither the same person, nor a different one.

Vakkali and the body

Disciples · SN 22.87

The monk Vakkali longed to keep looking at the Buddha. The Buddha said: “Enough, Vakkali. What is there to see in this foul body? Whoever sees the Dhamma sees me.”

The commentary takes this Dhamma as the ninefold supramundane Dhamma; the sutta goes straight on to teach him the five aggregates, impermanent and not self. Seen that way, even “the Buddha” is a convention for aggregates, the shift “A chariot of parts” asks of you.

Nun Vajirā's chariot

Disciples · SN 5.10

Māra asked the nun Vajirā who had created “this being”. She answered: “Why do you assume a being? This is a heap of formations. As the word ‘chariot’ is used when the parts are assembled, so ‘a being’ is the convention when the aggregates are present.”

Her reply is the hinge of “A chariot of parts”: the five aggregates underneath, the convention “a being” on top of them.

How a story is read here is a study aid, not the tradition's own reading.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

If a person is only a convention, who is responsible for a deed?

One way to see it, after you have tried

The continuum is. The cittas whose volition was kamma condition later cittas in the same stream, and convention names that stream "Devadatta". The analysis takes away only the idea of an owner standing outside the process. The Milindapañha's answer to whether the one reborn is the same person is "neither the same nor another".
