Mental factor

Hatred

dosa

Hatred is the mind's striking out against its object, from mild irritation to fury. It always comes with displeasure, and roots two cittas.

In depth

Hatred, dosadosa · hatred aversion, from irritation to rage; root of two cittas, always with displeasure, is the mind's striking against its object, and covers every degree of aversion: ill will, irritation, annoyance, resentment, fury. It is the second unwholesome root and is found in the two cittas accompanied by aversion, one unprompted and one prompted (II §13). In those cittas it is joined occasionally, and one at a time, by envy, avarice or worry, and in the prompted one, sometimes, by sloth and torpor.

Two features set it apart. Its feeling is always displeasure, and displeasure is found nowhere else (III §§3–4). And it appears in five of the Manual's categories of defilement: the bodily knot of ill will, the hindrance of ill will, the latent disposition to aversion, the fetter of aversion in both lists, and the defilement of hatred (Table 7.1). Of the ten unwholesome courses of action, killing, harsh speech and ill will always spring from it (V §23). The path of non-returning uproots it (IX §40 with the Guide; Table 9.3).

What newcomers miss is how much counts as dosa. Because displeasure always comes with aversion, sadness, grief, fear and boredom are all, in this analysis, forms of hatred. It burns, the commentaries say, the very mind and body it arises in.

One who is angry with another, the commentaries observe, is like someone who wants to hit his enemy with a burning coal or a handful of dung: he burns or soils his own hand first.

Simile, after Visuddhimagga IX

Not to be confused with

  • worry. Worry is remorse over one's own deeds and arises only in a hate-rooted citta, always with hatred. Hatred can arise without worry.
  • fear of wrongdoing. Anxious fear is a form of hatred, with displeasure. Fear of wrongdoing is a beautiful factor that holds back from harm without any such distress.

In your own experience. Notice sadness, without judging it, and look for the faint pushing-away inside it, the wish that things were not so. That pushing is dosa.

Manual: II §4; II §13; III §3; III §5; V §23; Table 7.1; IX §40 · Suttas: MN 21, DN 33, SN 46.55

Stories that show it

  • Kisāgotamī and the mustard seed: her grief is displeasure, a form of aversion
  • Khantivādi, the teacher of patience: the drunken king's rage
  • Dhammadinnā on feeling: aversion lies latent in painful feeling
  • Aṅgulimāla and the stones: a bandit who laid waste villages and towns
  • Devadatta's weighty kamma: the rock, the archers and the elephant
  • Why Moggallāna was beaten: turning on his own blind parents
  • Cūḷapanthaka's white cloth: hate is dust
  • Paṭācārā and the water: grief that drove her out of her mind
  • Nakulapitā, sick in body, not in mind: grief, which arises only with aversion, is what he trains to stop
  • Subha and the dog: anger and irritability ripen as ugliness
  • The second dart: resisting pain with grief: the second dart
  • Pilindavaccha's old word: absent: he speaks so not from hatred
