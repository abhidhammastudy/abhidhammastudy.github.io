Compassion

In depth

Compassion, karuṇākaruṇā · compassion Compassion is the wish that suffering be removed from others., is the wish that the suffering of others be removed. It finds others' pain unbearable, appears as the absence of cruelty, and is set off by seeing people helpless in their suffering (II §7). It has done its work, the commentaries say, when cruelty quietens, and has gone wrong when it slides into sorrow.

It is one of only two illimitables counted as factors in their own right; loving-kindness and equanimity are modes of non-hatred and neutrality of mind. It is unfixed and arises in twenty-eight cittas: the eight great wholesome, the eight great functional, and the first four jhānas in their wholesome, resultant and functional forms (II §15). It is absent from the fifth jhāna, since at jhāna level it arises only with joyful feeling, and the fifth jhāna has equanimity. It is absent from sense-sphere resultants and supramundane cittas, whose objects are never the concept of a being. And it never shares a citta with appreciative joy, since their objects and modes are contrary (II §§19, 21, 23). Some teachers held that it cannot arise with equanimous feeling in the sense sphere; the Manual's author seems not to agree.

What newcomers miss is that true compassion is never sad. The Guide notes that sorrow belongs only to unwholesome cittas. The moment compassion becomes distress at another's pain, the citta has changed: it is now aversion, with displeasure, wearing compassion's face.

The commentaries picture a mother with four children. Toward the sick one she feels compassion: she wants nothing but for the illness to go.

Simile, after Visuddhimagga IX

Not to be confused with

  • non-hatred. Non-hatred, as loving-kindness, wishes beings well. Compassion is a distinct factor that arises only when their suffering is the object.
  • hatred. Grief at another's pain is displeasure, which comes only with hatred. Compassion stays clear and steady, and can come with joy or equanimity.

In your own experience. When you meet someone's pain, notice whether the mind leans toward them or recoils into its own discomfort. The steady leaning, wishing the pain gone, is karuṇā; the recoil is its near enemy.
