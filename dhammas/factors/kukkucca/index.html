In depth

Worry, kukkuccakukkucca · worry Worry is regret over what was done or left undone, the mind going back over the past with distress., is remorse: regret after the fact for wrong done and good left undone. It grieves over what one did and what one failed to do, and it shows as remorse (II §4). It arises occasionally in the two hate-rooted cittas, without envy or avarice (II §§13, 26). Since it lives only there, it always carries displeasure and aversion, aversion turned on one's own past.

With restlessness it forms one hindrance, the two sharing the work of disturbing the mind and the remedy of calm (VII §8). The twofold tranquillity, of the factors and of consciousness, is the beautiful pair that opposes it (II §5). The path of non-returning, which uproots hatred, uproots worry with it (Guide to IX §40; Table 9.3).

The point newcomers usually miss is that worry in this technical sense looks backward. The English word suggests anxiety about the future, which the Abhidhamma would treat as a form of hatred or fear, not as this factor. Nor is recognising a fault the same as worry. The suttas advise seeing a wrong as wrong, resolving not to repeat it and moving on; the brooding self-reproach that follows is the hindrance, and it helps no one.

The Buddha likens a mind gripped by restlessness and worry to a slave, who cannot go where he wishes; abandoning that hindrance is like gaining one's freedom.

Simile, after DN 2

Not to be confused with

  • restlessness. Restlessness is agitation in every unwholesome citta; worry is the specific distress of regret, occurring only occasionally with hatred.
  • shame. Shame recoils from wrongdoing without distress and is beautiful. Worry is distressed self-recrimination and is unwholesome.

In your own experience. When an old mistake surfaces, notice whether the mind turns to learning from it or begins to chew on it. The chewing, with its sour displeasure, is kukkucca.
