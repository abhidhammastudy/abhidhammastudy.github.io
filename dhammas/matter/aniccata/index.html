Impermanence: matter's breaking up and vanishing.

In depth

Impermanence, aniccatāaniccatā · impermanence Impermanence: matter's breaking up and vanishing., is the breaking up of matter: its complete dissolution. Its function is to make matter subside, and it shows itself as destruction and falling away. The Manual also calls it the death of matter. It is non-concrete, the last of the four characteristics, with no origin of its own and no membership in any material group.

The commentaries assign it to the final phase of a material instant, its dissolution. Like decay, it is absent at the moment of conception, which has only arising. Every material group that arises meets it; there are no exceptions.

Two confusions are common. First, impermanence here is a specific item in the list of matter, the dissolving of matter, while the impermanence taught as one of the three marks applies to every conditioned dhamma, mental as well as material. Second, because the characteristics are non-concrete, they are not themselves the objects of insight. Insight takes the eighteen concrete kinds of matter as its object, and sees them as impermanent, painful and not self. Impermanence is what is seen in matter, not a further thing to look at.

Not to be confused with

  • impermanence as a mark. The mark of impermanence belongs to all conditioned dhammas. This entry is matter's own dissolution, counted as a kind of matter.
  • the death of a being. A being's death is the end of a life faculty and the cessation of kamma-born matter. The impermanence of matter is the passing of every group, at every moment.

In your own experience. Rest attention on a single sensation, a point of warmth or pressure, and watch for its fading rather than its arising.

Manual: VI §3; VI §4; VI §15; VI §22; VI §29
