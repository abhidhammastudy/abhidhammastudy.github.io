The path of insight

Insight meditation as one road, from a clean life to the moment of seeing Nibbāna: each stage with what is seen there, what to watch for, a picture from the texts and a life that shows it.

The Manual lays out the way of insight as seven purifications, and inside them ten knowledges, each seeing a little more clearly that mind and matter are impermanent, unsatisfactory and not a self (CMA IX §§22–34). The stages follow one another in this order in the texts. How long each stage takes the texts hardly say, and how it is met differs from person to person.

Choose a stage on the road to read about it. The descriptions follow the Manual and the Visuddhimagga; where a word or a grouping is a later teacher's, it says so.

A word before the stages: this map is for understanding the path, not a checklist to measure yourself by. Mahasi Sayadaw (1904–1982), the Burmese teacher whose descriptions are used most below, advised that a pupil who practises under a teacher should not learn the stages beforehand: those who know them from books or talks, he says, meet these ups and downs late in the practice, and he set them out for the sake of those who must practise without a teacher (Practical Insight Meditation, BPS ed., pp. 34–35). Nor are they everyone's experience. Bhikkhu Anālayo, a modern scholar-monk, notes that in the study he cites (Kornfield 1979) the knowledges showed only for a minority even of those on intensive retreat ("The Insight Knowledge of Fear and Adverse Effects of Mindfulness Practices", Mindfulness 10, 2019, p. 2180). Most notes below on what a stage "can be like" are modern teachers' reports from such retreats, named each time, and given so that the texts can be imagined; whether anyone has reached a stage is a question for a teacher, not for this page.

I–II · The ground

Purification of virtue

sīla-visuddhi

What is seen

Nothing is "seen" yet; this is the ground. The Manual names four kinds of virtue, explained in the Guide for a monk: keeping the monastic code, restraint at the senses, a pure livelihood, and using robes, food, lodging and medicine with reflection (CMA IX §28 and Guide). For a lay person the Visuddhimagga names the five precepts (Vism I §13). Its fruit is a mind without remorse, which can settle (Vism I §§9, 11).

What it can be like

The canon pictures virtue by what it leads to. Asked what good conduct is for, the Buddha answers with a chain: it leaves nothing to regret, and that brings gladness, then rapture, calm, happiness and concentration, and a concentrated mind sees things as they are (AN 10.1, an10.1:2.1–11.10). In the site's everyday terms: a day with nothing on one's conscience is easier to sit after. Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, has retreatants put right even an old wrong: one who has spoken with contempt of a noble person apologises, in person, through the teacher, or inwardly if the person cannot be reached (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 1).

What to watch for

The seven are reached in order, each the support of the one after (Guide to CMA IX §22), like relay chariots, each for the sake of the next (MN 24). The site's note: the chariot is left, but virtue is kept all the way.

A common misreading

"I must be morally perfect before I can begin." On this site's reading, the texts give virtue a practical job rather than a pass mark: a mind with nothing to regret can settle (AN 10.1; Vism I §§9, 11). And one part of virtue, restraint at the senses, is itself mindfulness on meeting sights and sounds, which a lay person can practise all day (CMA IX §28 and Guide).

In practice

For a lay meditator: the five precepts always, and the eight on observance days (Vism I §13); many retreats today keep the eight (a modern custom). And restraint at the senses during the day, which the Manual itself counts as virtue (CMA IX §28 and Guide).

In a picture

The tree without its branches and leaves

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · AN 5.24

Each step of the path is the supporting condition of the next, beginning from virtue. Damage the branches and leaves, and nothing inside the tree grows to fullness.

In one who lacks virtue, the Buddha said, right concentration has lost its support. Without right concentration, the knowledge and vision of things as they really are has lost its support; without that, disenchantment and fading away; and without those, the knowledge and vision of release.

It is like a tree whose branches and leaves are damaged: its outer bark does not grow to fullness, nor its inner bark, nor its sapwood, nor its heartwood.

In one who has virtue it is the other way round. Each step has its support, as in a tree whose branches and leaves are whole the outer bark, the inner bark, the sapwood and the heartwood all grow to fullness. The same tree comes back in other suttas of the Aṅguttara, with longer chains of steps.

The commentary on the Aṅguttara reads the chain as the path of insight. The knowledge and vision of things as they really are is tender insight, beginning from the knowledge that defines mind and matter. Disenchantment is strong insight. Fading away is the path. The knowledge and vision of release is fruition, together with the reviewing knowledge.

Where the picture stops working: A tree grows by itself, slowly and without a break. Insight passes through distinct knowledges, and the path is a single moment (Guide to CMA I §§26–28; CMA IX §34). The sutta names virtue and right concentration but none of the purifications; setting them beside the purification of virtue and of mind (CMA IX §§28–29) is this site's reading. And "disenchantment" here, in the commentary's sense of strong insight, is wider than the single knowledge of disenchantment in the Manual's list (Guide to CMA IX §33); the commentary does not say where strong insight begins. So too, "tender insight" here is the commentary's name for all insight from defining mind and matter onward. That is wider than the Visuddhimagga's tender insight, which is only the first phase of the knowledge of rise and fall (Vism XX §731; Guide to CMA IX §32).

In a life

  • Nanda, guardian of the doors · Ud 3.2; AN 8.9; AN 1.230
    The Buddha's half-brother Nanda was ordained on his wedding day and pined for his bride. In time he became an arahant, and the Buddha named him foremost among those who guard the sense doors: whichever way Nanda looked, he looked with full awareness, so that longing and grief could not flow in.
  • Rāhula and the mirror · MN 61
    The Buddha asked his young son Rāhula what a mirror is for. “For reflection, sir.” “In the same way,” said the Buddha, “reflect before, during and after every action of body, speech and mind: does it lead to harm for myself, for others, or both?”
  • Dīghāvu on his sickbed · SN 55.3
    The lay disciple Dīghāvu lay gravely ill, his pain growing, and the Buddha came to his house. Finding that he already had the faith and virtue of a stream-enterer, the Buddha gave him more to do: see all formations as impermanent, the impermanent as suffering, suffering as not self, and keep in mind giving up, fading and ceasing. Dīghāvu died soon after, and the Buddha declared him a non-returner.
  • Mahātissa and the laughing woman · Visuddhimagga I §15
    On the road to Anurādhapura for alms, the elder Mahātissa met a young wife going home after a quarrel, who laughed out loud on seeing him. He looked up and saw only the bones of her teeth; the perception of foulness he had practised came back to him, and he became an arahant where he stood. Asked by her husband whether a woman had passed, he said he had seen only a heap of bones going along the high road.

Purification of mind

citta-visuddhi

What is seen

A mind steady and clear enough to look. The Manual names access and absorption concentration (CMA IX §29); the Guide adds, for one who goes by insight alone, momentary concentration (Guide to CMA IX §29). Either way the hindrances are held off (Vism IV §58).

What it can be like

Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, describes it from noting practice. At first a wandering thought is noticed only some while after it began. As concentration strengthens, each noting seems to fall on its object, even to strike it; a stray thought is caught as it arises and fades once it is noted, and the mind is then called unhindered. That steadiness, lasting a moment at a time and renewed, is what he calls purification of mind (The Progress of Insight, tr. Nyanaponika, (b) "The Purification of Mind").

What to watch for

The texts describe more than one route: calm first, then insight; insight first, then calm; the two together; or a mind first stirred up about the teaching that later settles (AN 4.170). The Visuddhimagga itself names a pure-insight meditator who begins from the four elements (Vism XVIII §664); that this person's concentration is "momentary" is said by the Visuddhimagga's sub-commentary (on Vism I §§3–4).

A common misreading

"A concentrated mind has no thoughts in it." In absorption the mind rests wholly on one object, but for one who goes by insight alone the steadiness is momentary (Guide to CMA IX §29), and Mahasi Sayadaw's account is of thoughts that still come but are caught at once and let go (The Progress of Insight, (b)). On this site's reading, the trouble is not the thought but the thought that runs on unnoticed.

In practice

Notice which hindrance is present when you sit, and know it as present; when the mind is sluggish, rouse it; when it is restless, calm it; mindfulness is useful at all times (MN 10; SN 46.53).

In a picture

The toddler and the strong man

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga IV, CS §58

The difference between access concentration and full absorption (jhāna).

Lift a small child and stand it on its feet, and it falls down again and again. A strong man can get up from his seat and stand all day long.

The Visuddhimagga says concentration grows like that. In the stage called access, the factors of jhāna have arisen but are not yet strong. The mind takes the meditation sign as its object for a while, then drops back into the life-continuum, the mind's resting state, then takes up the sign again: the child standing and falling.

In absorption the factors are strong. Once the mind has cut through the life-continuum, it can go on in one unbroken run of wholesome moments for a whole night or a whole day: the strong man standing.

Where the picture stops working: The strong man fits absorption once it is mastered: in a beginner's first absorption the jhāna javana runs only once before the mind falls back into the life-continuum, and only with practice does the run grow longer (CMA IV §22 and Guide). And the man stands by his own strength, while the run of javanas is held up by no one: each javana is a condition for the next by repetition (CMA VIII §13).

Told in: Visuddhimagga IV (the earth kasiṇa), CS §58; CMA IV §14 and Guide: the absorption process; IV §22 and Guide: javana in attainments; IX §§17–18: access and absorption

The muddy lake and the clear lake

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · AN 1.45–46

Only a mind that is not clouded can see. Purification of mind is for the sake of seeing.

Suppose a pool of water is cloudy, stirred up and muddy. A man with good eyes stands on the bank, but he cannot see the shells, the gravel and pebbles, or the shoals of fish, moving or at rest, because the water is clouded. In the same way, the Buddha said, a monk whose mind is clouded cannot know his own good, or the good of others, or of both, or reach any knowledge and vision beyond the ordinary that is fit for the noble ones.

Suppose instead the pool is clear, settled and unclouded. The same man on the bank sees the shells, the gravel and pebbles, and the fish, moving or at rest. So a monk whose mind is unclouded can know his own good and others', and reach that knowledge and vision.

The commentary on the Aṅguttara says what clouds the mind: it is wrapped in the five hindrances; the clear mind is one freed from them. The knowledge and vision fit for the noble ones it takes to be the divine eye, insight knowledge, the path and fruition, and reviewing. The Vibhāvinī, the commentary on the Manual, defines purification of mind in the same terms: it is so called because it cleans the mind by freeing it of the hindrances.

Where the picture stops working: Water clears if it is left alone; a mind is cleared by effort, and by the right kind of attention. Nor does the picture say how clear is clear enough. The Manual makes purification of mind access or absorption concentration (CMA IX §29); the Guide adds that for one who takes the vehicle of pure insight it is momentary concentration, steady enough to count as access. Tying the two lakes to this purification is the site's reading, through the Vibhāvinī's definition; neither the sutta nor its commentary speaks of the purifications. And a clear lake shows what is there only to someone who looks: the seeing is the work of the purifications that follow (CMA IX §§30–34).

III · Purification of view

Defining mind and matter

nāmarūpa-pariggaha

What is seen

Only mind and matter, each known by its characteristic, function, manifestation and proximate cause, and no being or person besides them (CMA IX §30; Vism XVIII §§671–672). It is called purification of view because it clears away the view of a self (Guide to CMA IX §30).

What it can be like

The canon's picture is a clear, well-cut gem with a coloured thread running through it, which someone with good eyes takes in hand and sees as two: this is the gem, and this the thread (DN 2, dn2:84.1–84.5). The commentary gives the key: the gem is the body, the thread is the knowing that takes it as object, and the moment both stand out is the moment body and mind are seen as two (Sv on DN 2, CS §235). Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, made it a test for lay listeners: wiggle a finger and see whether you can tell the intention from the movement; an honest beginner, he says, cannot, while for a practised meditator the object and the noting of it are as separate as a wall and a stone that strikes it (Fundamentals of Vipassanā Meditation, "Things Fall Apart").

What to watch for

The site's note: seeing that there is "only mind and matter" can become a new opinion to hold. The point is to see it in what is happening now, not to adopt it as a belief.

A common misreading

"Mind means thinking." In the Abhidhamma, mind is the knowing of an object (Guide to CMA I §2), so even the bare knowing of a touch or a sound is mind. Mahasi Sayadaw says the knowledge can be quite plain: a simple meditator sees only the rising and the knowing of it, the falling and the knowing of it, and nothing besides, while a learned one puts the same seeing in terms of mind and matter, and both are describing this knowledge (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 16–17).

In practice

The Visuddhimagga gives several ways in: by the four elements, the eighteen elements, the twelve bases or the five aggregates (Vism XVIII §§664–668). If mind does not yet show, keep defining matter; as matter becomes clear, mind becomes evident by itself, through contact, feeling or consciousness (Vism XVIII §§669–670). Do not turn to mind after only one or two kinds of matter (§671).

The blind man and the cripple

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XVIII, CS §677; Atthasālinī, CS §498

Mind and matter each lack strength of their own and work only by leaning on each other. There is no third thing that walks.

A man blind from birth and a cripple who could only shuffle along on a low seat both wanted to go elsewhere. The blind man said: I can do what feet do, but I have no eyes to see where the ground is smooth or rough. The cripple said: I can do what eyes do, but I have no feet to go forward or back. The blind man, delighted, lifted the cripple onto his shoulders, and the cripple, sitting there, called out: leave the left, take the right; leave the right, take the left.

The Visuddhimagga brings it in as a simile that others cite (the commentary teachers, says its sub-commentary), and draws the point. Neither man goes by his own power or his own strength, and yet, leaning on each other, they do go. So mind has no power of its own to arise or to act, and matter has none either. Mind does not eat, drink, speak or take up a posture by itself, and matter has no wish to; but when there is mind's wish to eat or drink, to speak or move, matter eats, drinks, speaks and moves. It adds verses: as people cross the ocean depending on a ship, and the ship crosses depending on the people, so mind and matter each depend on the other.

The Atthasālinī, the commentary on the first book of the Abhidhamma, tells the story more fully. The two sat in a hall at the city gate. The cripple told the blind man of a district where food was plentiful and asked why he did not go and live there in comfort. Why not you? said the blind man. I have no feet, said the cripple; I have no eyes, said the blind man. Then let your feet be mine and my eyes be yours. With the cripple on his shoulders, holding his head with the left hand and pointing out the way with the right (a root here, a stone there), they reached the place together and lived there at ease.

Where the picture stops working: Two persons, one of them giving directions, make mind sound like a pilot: a self riding the body. Both texts rule this out by saying that neither has strength of its own, and the "mind" here is itself many dhammas, the four mental aggregates, arising and passing. The Visuddhimagga's sub-commentary raises the obvious objection, that a hundred blind men together see no better than one, and answers with the simile itself, recalling Sāriputta's words, before his two sheaves of reeds, that some wise people understand a meaning through a simile (SN 12.67, sn12.67:7.3). And the Atthasālinī says the picture is told for existence with all five aggregates. Elsewhere it would not hold: in the immaterial planes mind occurs without matter, and among the non-percipient beings matter occurs without mind (CMA VI §§28–29).

Told in: Visuddhimagga XVIII (purification of view), CS §677; Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XVIII §677; SN 12.67, the two sheaves of reeds (sn12.67:7.3); Atthasālinī, on the great resultant cittas, the miscellaneous similes (Vipākuddhārakathā), CS §498; CMA IX §30 and Guide: purification of view

In a life

  • Nun Vajirā's chariot · SN 5.10
    Māra asked the nun Vajirā who had created “this being”. She answered: “Why do you assume a being? This is a heap of formations. As the word ‘chariot’ is used when the parts are assembled, so ‘a being’ is the convention when the aggregates are present.”
  • Pukkusāti and the elements · MN 140
    One night in a potter's shed, the Buddha taught a stranger, Pukkusāti, to see his body as earth, water, fire, air, space and consciousness, none of it “mine”. Pukkusāti recognised his teacher, but while seeking robes for ordination he was killed by a cow. The Buddha declared him a non-returner.
  • Sāmāvatī and the burning palace · Ud 7.10; Udāna commentary; Dhammapada commentary, vv. 21–23
    While King Udena was away in his park, the women's quarters of his palace burned, and five hundred women died with Queen Sāmāvatī at their head. When the monks asked about them, the Buddha said that among them were stream-enterers, once-returners and non-returners, and that none had died without fruit. The commentaries say that as the fire came, Sāmāvatī told her women to be heedful, and they turned to watching feeling itself.

IV · Overcoming doubt

Discerning conditions

paccaya-pariggaha

What is seen

That mind and matter have causes, neither none nor a creator: this body comes from ignorance, craving, clinging and kamma and is kept up by food; seeing happens because eye and form meet; and as now, so in the past and future (CMA IX §31; Vism XIX §§678–679).

What it can be like

The Visuddhimagga passes on a saying of "the ancients", earlier teachers: the eye does not see, for it has no mind, and the mind does not see, for it has no eye; seeing happens where door and object meet, and "the eye sees" is a way of speaking, as one says "he shoots with a bow" (Vism I §15). Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, reports how conditions begin to show in practice: the intention to bend an arm is now plain before the bending, so that mind seems to go first, and it becomes clear that there is no knowing without something to be known (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 17–18).

What to watch for

The Visuddhimagga calls someone with this knowledge a "lesser stream-enterer", who has found comfort and a footing in the teaching (Vism XIX §691); its sub-commentary adds that no path or fruit has yet been reached, so this is still an ordinary person. Doubts such as "what was I, what will I be" fall away here (Vism XIX §680).

A common misreading

"Discerning conditions means remembering past lives." The Visuddhimagga reasons from the present: as now, so in the past and the future (Vism XIX §679); Mahasi Sayadaw describes it as a reflection that comes between notings (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 19). Nor are visions a sign of progress: images may come here as if seen with the eyes, from a radiant Buddha to corpses, and he calls them products of an imagination sharpened by concentration, like dreams, neither to be enjoyed nor feared (pp. 20–21).

In practice

Sāriputta sets out the conditions for seeing: an eye that is intact, a sight that comes into range, and attention to match; with the eye intact, if the sight or the attention is missing, no seeing arises (MN 28, mn28:27.1–27.3). The site's suggestion, after Vism XIX §§679–681: in daily life, notice the conditions of an experience in the same way; a mood follows a thought; an act follows an intention.

Whose child is this?

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XIX, CS §§678–679

Having seen mind and matter, one asks where they come from, as naturally as one asks whose a baby found lying in the street is. The answer is a family of conditions, neither a creator nor chance.

The Visuddhimagga says the meditator who wants to overcome doubt sets about it like a good physician who sees a disease and looks for where it came from; or like a kind man who sees a tiny baby lying on its back in the street and wonders whose child it can be, and thinks about its mother and father. So he looks for the causes and conditions of this mind and matter.

First he reasons that it is not without a cause, for then it would be the same everywhere, always, for everyone. Nor is it made by a Lord or anything of the kind, for there is nothing of that kind over and above mind and matter. So it must have causes and conditions. What are they?

He begins with the body, which, the sub-commentary says, is the coarser and plainer of the two. This body is not born inside lotuses or water lilies, or among gems and strings of pearls, but in a cramped and foul place between the stomach and the bowel, with the wall of the belly behind it and the backbone in front of it, like a worm in rotten fish. Ignorance, craving, clinging and kamma are its causes, because they produce it, and nutriment is its condition, because it supports it. Of these, ignorance, craving and clinging stand to the body as a mother to her child, as its decisive support; kamma as a father, who begets it; and nutriment as a nurse, who keeps it going.

For mind he uses another method, the sutta's: dependent on the eye and forms, eye-consciousness arises, and so on for each door.

Where the picture stops working: Parents are persons who outlive the birth; conditions are dhammas, and the kamma that begot this body ceased long ago. The mother, father and nurse are given for the body. Ignorance and the rest condition mind too, the sub-commentary notes, but the Visuddhimagga traces mind's conditions by another method. And a child is someone, while here there is no one besides the mind and matter whose conditions are sought (CMA IX §30 and Guide). The unclean birthplace is not decoration: the sub-commentary says it is there to turn the mind from delight in the body. The Visuddhimagga goes on to call one who has this knowledge a "lesser stream-enterer", who has found comfort and a footing in the Dispensation and is sure of his destination (XIX §691); the sub-commentary explains that destination as a good rebirth, so long as this knowledge is not lost. That is the commentary's honour for a worldling, not the path.

V · What is and is not the path

Comprehension

sammasana-ñāṇa

What is seen

Formations in groups, all of them marked as impermanent, suffering and non-self: matter of the past, future and present, near and far, then feeling, perception, formations and consciousness the same way (CMA IX §32 and Guide).

What it can be like

The Visuddhimagga's most ordinary pictures are here. The body when hungry is drawn and dull, like a burnt stump or a crow hiding in a charcoal basket, and when fed it is full and soft; the matter of the hungry hour ceases right there and never reaches the fed one, nor the fed the hungry. So too the body in hot weather and in cool, and in a glad mood and a sad one; and outside, an asoka shoot turns from red through green to yellow and drops (Vism XX §§712–716).

What to watch for

This knowledge works on formations in groups and stretches of time, down to the moment (CMA IX §32 and Guide). The next knowledge, rise and fall, watches them arise and pass in the present (Guide to CMA IX §32).

A common misreading

"Comprehension means thinking hard about impermanence." The Visuddhimagga sets it out as applying the three marks, span by span (Vism XX §§706–716), and thoughts about the marks may come thick and fast here. Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, advises noting such reflections and going back to watching: too much reflecting, he says, holds insight back, and comprehension grows clearer at the later stages even without it (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 22–23).

In practice

One method in the Visuddhimagga shortens the span step by step: a hundred years, its three stages, a decade, a year, a season, a day, down to the six parts of a single step (Vism XX §§706–710). Begin with whatever is clearest to you (Vism XX §694).

The lamp and the three men

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XX, CS §711

How insight looks at matter in ever shorter spans of time, from a whole lifetime down to a single phase of a step, and finds that nothing passes over from one span to the next.

A man from the borderland, who knew torches of wood and grass but had never seen a lamp, came to a city and saw an oil lamp burning in the market street. He thought it wonderful. A first man told him there was nothing to wonder at: when the oil and the wick are used up, the flame goes out and leaves no trace.

A second man said that was a coarse way to see it. As the wick burns down, the flame in each third of it goes out without passing over into the next third. A third man said that too was coarse. At each finger's breadth, each half finger's breadth, each thread and each fibre of the wick, the flame goes out without passing to the next, and apart from the fibre that is burning no flame can be pointed to at all.

The Visuddhimagga uses the lamp for the meditator who examines matter by its lifespan. Seen coarsely, matter ends at the end of a hundred-year life. Seen more finely, it ends with each stage of life: youth, middle age, old age. Then with each span of ten, five, four, three, two and one years; each season, of four months and of two; each fortnight, each day and night, and each of the six parts of a day and night; and at last with each movement, each phase of a single step. Nothing passes over from one span to the next.

Where the picture stops working: The picture stops at a phase of a step. This is comprehension "by way of duration": seeing the three characteristics in ever shorter spans of time (CMA IX §32 and Guide). The Manual adds that comprehension also works by continuity and by moment, and that the knowledge of rise and fall then watches momentary things arise and pass. The lamp does not reach that far.

V · What is and is not the path

Rise and fall, the tender phase

taruṇa-udayabbaya-ñāṇa

What is seen

Formations in the present, arising and passing: by their conditions, as each comes with its conditions and goes with them, and by moment, as they appear and break up now (CMA IX §32; Vism XX §§723–731).

What it can be like

Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, describes the first rush of this knowledge. Noting speeds up and seems to plunge into each object; a bright light may appear; rapture brings goose-flesh, tears, trembling, a feeling of being on a swing; faith grows strong, one wants to urge others to meditate, and one may think there is nothing more to know (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 23–25). These are the ten imperfections in a teacher's words; the next stage learns to see through them.

What to watch for

This is where the ten imperfections appear: light, knowledge, joy, tranquillity, strong faith and the rest (Guide to CMA IX §32; Vism XX §732). They come only to one who is practising rightly and has begun insight (Vism XX §732); the texts make them neither a goal nor a sign of progress, and taking them for the path is the very mistake they warn of (Vism XX §733).

A common misreading

"If nothing special happens, I am failing." The Visuddhimagga says the imperfections come to one practising rightly, in very different strengths (Vism XX §§732–733), and makes them neither a goal nor a measure. Mahasi Sayadaw names the opposite trap, at an earlier stage: some meditators, finding nothing extraordinary, grow slack, and should simply note the slackness and go on (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 21).

In practice

Keep watching whatever is present arising and passing, rather than looking for any particular experience.

The dewdrop at sunrise

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XX, CS §731

How formations look once the knowledge of rise and fall has begun: always new, gone almost as soon as they come, and without a core.

When the truths, dependent origination, its methods and the characteristics have become plain to the meditator, the Visuddhimagga says, formations appear to him as always new. So these things arise, he sees, that never arose before, and what has arisen ceases.

And not only new: they appear to last only a little while, like a drop of dew when the sun comes up, a bubble on water, a line drawn on water with a stick, a mustard seed balanced on the point of an awl, or a flash of lightning.

V · What is and is not the path

Seeing the imperfections for what they are

maggāmagga-vavatthāna

What is seen

That light, knowledge, joy, tranquillity, happiness, resolve, energy, mindfulness, equanimity and, subtlest, a delight in them all, are not the path. Nine of the ten are not unwholesome in themselves; they are called imperfections because clinging grows on them. The tenth, the quiet delight in it all, is itself a defilement, and hard to recognise as one. Each can be taken as "mine", with "how fine", or simply relished (Vism XX §§733–735). This act of telling path from not-path gives the fifth purification its name (CMA IX §32).

What it can be like

Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, tells this lesson against himself. The first three meditators from his home village worked so hard that they carried home untouched the cigars and betel quids they had brought for the week; within three days they saw light, felt deep calm and said they had found the truth only in old age, but he let them enjoy it without telling them to note the joy, and in four more days they went no further (Fundamentals of Vipassanā Meditation, "Rediscovery"). His advice: note a light as "bright" until it goes; if it is too strong to fade, turn firmly to something in the body, and do not look to see whether it is still there, since looking brings it back (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 25–26).

What to watch for

The one test the Visuddhimagga gives works for all ten: this has arisen; it is impermanent and conditioned; not mine, not I, not my self. Seeing them so, the meditator knows that insight free of them is the path (Vism XX §736; CMA IX §32).

A common misreading

Two opposite mistakes: "these states are the goal", and "these states are dangerous, so I must push them away". The Visuddhimagga's view is neither: nine of the ten are not unwholesome in themselves but become a ground for clinging (Vism XX §735), and the remedy is to see each as arisen, impermanent and not mine, and go on (§736). Mahasi Sayadaw says the same in a meditator's terms: the wrong attitude is to cherish them, the right one to note them without attachment until they pass (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 26).

In practice

If something unusual comes (a light, a rush of joy, a deep calm), note it as arisen and passing, and go back to your meditation subject; the one it misleads is the one who drops his subject to sit enjoying it (Vism XX §§733, 736). Do not take it as a sign of progress, and not having such things is no failure. The site's own advice: do not chase it or try to bring it back; lights or sensations that go on outside meditation are a matter for a doctor.

In a picture

The elder and the conjured elephant

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XX, CS §733

Bright, calm and powerful states can persuade a practised meditator that he has arrived. A sudden test of what he reacts to shows what is still there.

The ten imperfections of insight, the Visuddhimagga says, arise in one who practises rightly and has begun insight; not in a noble disciple who has already penetrated, nor in one who practises wrongly, a lazy one who has dropped his meditation subject. The first is the light. When it appears, the meditator thinks no such light has ever come to him before, and that he must have reached the path and its fruit. He takes what is not the path for the path, leaves his meditation subject, and sits enjoying the light. This error comes mostly to one who has both calm and insight: because defilements held down by his attainments do not stir, he believes he is an arahant.

The elder Dhammadinna of Talaṅgara, an arahant and a teacher of many monks, sat one day in his day-quarters and wondered whether his own teacher, the elder Mahānāga of Uccavālika, had finished the work of a monk. He saw that Mahānāga was still a worldling, and that unless he went to him he would die one. He went through the air by his psychic power, paid his respects, and said he had come with a question. He asked a thousand, and the old elder answered every one without hesitating. Your knowledge is very sharp, venerable sir, said Dhammadinna; when did you reach this? Sixty years ago, said Mahānāga. Can you use concentration too? That is not hard. Then make an elephant.

Mahānāga made an elephant, all white. Now make it charge at you, said Dhammadinna, ears spread, tail out, trunk in its mouth, trumpeting. He did, and when he saw the terrible thing bearing down on him he jumped up to run. Dhammadinna reached out, caught the edge of his robe, and asked: venerable sir, is there fear in one whose taints are gone? Then Mahānāga knew he was still a worldling. He asked Dhammadinna to be his support and sat down at his feet. I came for that, said Dhammadinna; do not worry. He gave him a meditation subject, and Mahānāga went up onto his walking path and at the third step reached arahantship.

The Visuddhimagga adds that the elder was of hating temperament, and that monks like this waver over the light. Its sub-commentary explains: in one of that temperament fear comes suddenly, even if he is brave; and such a temperament is quick to let go, which is why he broke through so fast.

Where the picture stops working: No light appears in the episode itself; the Visuddhimagga tells it under the light and closes by saying that monks like this waver over the light. The test is a psychic feat no reader can copy, and fear is not the test the CMA gives. Bhikkhu Bodhi's Guide gives the way through: recognise these states as by-products of maturing insight, see them as impermanent, suffering and not-self, and go on (Guide to CMA IX §32). Nor are the ten unwholesome in themselves: the Visuddhimagga says the light and the rest are called imperfections because they are a basis for imperfection; only the attachment is both an imperfection and its basis. Taken as mine, as wonderful, or relished, each becomes a grasping of view, conceit or craving (Vism XX §735). Telling what is the path from what is not is the purification named after it (CMA IX §32). The "sixty years" are the time since his supposed attainment, not his years as a monk.

VI · The way

Rise and fall, mature

udayabbaya-ñāṇa

What is seen

Rise and fall is taken up again. Clouded by the imperfections, it could not make out the three marks as they really are; free of them, it can (Vism XXI §738). Each mark had been hidden, and is now seen through (Vism XXI §739).

What it can be like

Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher: each object now seems to vanish in the very place where it arose, without moving on, and each occurrence is plainly a different one from the one before (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 26). The Visuddhimagga has the same refrain, said of the parts of a step and of the hungry and the fed body: it ceases right here, without reaching the next (Vism XX §§710, 712).

What to watch for

Already at comprehension the Visuddhimagga, following an older exposition, says: not "I see", but formations seeing formations; so view, conceit and craving get no hold (Vism XX §721). The site's note: this holds at every stage after.

A common misreading

"This is the best there is; I can relax now." Mahasi Sayadaw says a meditator may well think so here, and should not slacken (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 26). Or the opposite, "my meditation object has faded; I have gone backwards": Matara Sri Ñāṇārāma, a modern Sri Lankan teacher, tells of a meditator who goes to his teacher fed up because the breath and the felt shape of his body have grown faint, and is told that these were concepts, and their fading is the perception of a solid whole beginning to break up (The Seven Stages of Purification, p. 46), the look of a solid whole that, the Visuddhimagga says, hides non-self (Vism XXI §739).

In a picture

The iron rods and the lump of foam

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §739

What hides the three characteristics, and what it looks like when the cover is broken: things come apart like separate rods, and the solid mass melts like foam.

Why do the three characteristics not show? The Visuddhimagga answers: impermanence does not show because rise and fall are not attended to, and continuity hides it. Suffering does not show because the constant oppression is not attended to, and the postures hide it. Not-self does not show because things are not resolved into their elements, and compactness hides it. When rise and fall are grasped and continuity is broken, impermanence shows as it really is; when the oppression is attended to and the postures are seen through, suffering shows; when the elements are taken apart and the compactness is broken up, not-self shows.

This comes after the imperfections, when the knowledge of rise and fall, freed of them, is taken up again for just this purpose: to mark the characteristics.

The sub-commentary gives two pictures. Continuity: to one who rightly watches rise and fall, the arising phase of a state never reaches its falling phase, nor the falling its arising, and states no longer show up as joined in a series. They show up unconnected, like separate iron rods, and impermanence becomes plainer still.

The postures it explains without a picture: a change of posture that relieves the pain born in another works as its cover, and so with each of them. Once this is seen for what it is, the ceaseless oppression of formations shows.

Compactness, it says, is of three kinds: of mass, taking states that hold each other up as one thing; of function, taking their different functions as one; and of object, taking their different ways of taking an object as one. When knowledge separates the elements (earth is one thing, water another; contact one thing, feeling another), these compactnesses dissolve like a lump of foam rubbed in the hand, and not-self shows: these are mere states, empty, occurring as their conditions allow.

Where the picture stops working: These are two pictures and one explanation, all from the sub-commentary, not the Visuddhimagga, which gives none. Rods lying apart are lasting things side by side; states are not, for each rises and falls in a moment. The foam recalls the lump of foam in SN 22.95, there a picture of form, but the sub-commentary does not cite it; the link is ours. Breaking the compact destroys nothing: the single mass was never there. The Manual does not name the three concealments; it uses continuity only as a way of comprehending (CMA IX §32 and Guide), and the three are the Visuddhimagga's (XXI §739).

VI · The way

Dissolution

bhaṅga-ñāṇa

What is seen

Only the ending of things. Formations show up quickly and attention rests on their breaking up; and the mind that knows them is seen breaking up too (Vism XXI §§741–745; Guide to CMA IX §33).

What it can be like

Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, tells of his cousin, one of those first village meditators, who reached this knowledge in his second week. Noting the rising and falling, he could not find the shape of his abdomen, and touched it with his hand to see whether his body was still there; the ground and the trees he looked at seemed to be breaking up, and he wondered whether his eyesight was failing. Mahasi told him that what he saw was true (Fundamentals of Vipassanā Meditation, "Rediscovery").

What to watch for

The Visuddhimagga lists fearlessness among this knowledge's benefits (Vism XXI §749).

A common misreading

"I have lost my insight", or "something is wrong with my eyes". Mahasi Sayadaw answers both: the mind used to delight in shapes and forms, and now that only their vanishing shows, it feels unsatisfied; this is progress, not loss (The Progress of Insight, section 5; Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 27–28). The Visuddhimagga's account agrees in substance: attention no longer reaches the arising or the presence of things, only their ceasing (Vism XXI §741).

In a picture

Bubbles in heavy rain

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §748; Dhp 170

At the knowledge of dissolution attention no longer reaches the arising of things; it rests on their breaking up, everywhere and all the time.

As the meditator goes on weighing mind and matter as impermanent, suffering and not-self, his knowledge grows sharp and formations come into view quickly. Then his mindfulness no longer reaches their arising, their standing or their going on; it settles on their ending, their breaking up. He sees formations breaking like a fragile pot, scattering like fine dust, crackling like sesame seeds roasting in a pan.

It is as if a man with good eyes stood on the bank of a pond or a river while heavy rain was falling, and watched big bubbles rise on the surface one after another and quickly burst. In the same way he sees all formations breaking, breaking.

It was of such a meditator, the Visuddhimagga says, that the Buddha spoke the verse: the king of death does not see one who looks on the world as one would look at a bubble, or at a mirage (Dhp 170).

The commentary on the Dhammapada gives the verse a story. Five hundred monks, making little progress in the forest, were coming back to the Buddha for a better meditation subject, contemplating a mirage on the way. As they reached the monastery it began to rain, and they watched the bubbles rise and burst under the downpour and took them as their object: this body too arises and breaks like a bubble. The Buddha spoke the verse to them, and when it ended they reached arahantship where they stood.

Where the picture stops working: The man on the bank watches bubbles burst without bursting himself. At this knowledge there is no safe onlooker: the Visuddhimagga says the meditator sees the object break up, and then, with a later mind, the breaking up of the mind that saw it, so that both the known and the knowing are seen to dissolve (Vism XXI §§741–742). Seeing only breaking does not mean that nothing arises; it is where attention rests: arising is simply not taken up (the sub-commentary on §748; Guide to CMA IX §33). And the verse's promise, that the king of death does not see, is fulfilled only with arahantship, after which there is no further becoming (so the sub-commentary); the knowledge of dissolution is a stage on the way, and leads on to the knowledge of fear.

VI · The way

Fearfulness

bhaya-ñāṇa

What is seen

Everything that keeps dissolving, in any realm and any time, appears as something to be feared (Vism XXI §750).

What it can be like

Here the texts and the modern reports have to be read together, and the site gives both. The Visuddhimagga's own pictures are of terror: formations appear as lions and tigers do to a timid man, or as the death of her sons does to a mother; yet it says the knowledge itself does not fear (Vism XXI §§750–751). Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, writes that the mind itself then takes on the aspect of fear and is as if without protection (The Progress of Insight, section 6; his translator notes that the Pāli word covers both fear felt and fearfulness seen, n. 37), and that he may feel weak in body and mind, dejected, with nothing to lean on (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 29). Matara Sri Ñāṇārāma, a modern Sri Lankan teacher, says the meditator can be unusually frightened here and must note the fright, or it will not leave him (The Seven Stages of Purification, pp. 47–48). Mahasi's everyday image: to take these vanishing things for a refuge is like taking shelter in an old house on the point of collapse (Fundamentals of Vipassanā Meditation, "Rediscovery").

What to watch for

The knowledge itself does not fear: it judges, like a man standing by pits of glowing embers who knows that whoever falls in will burn (Vism XXI §751). The texts count fear, danger and disenchantment as one knowledge with three names (Vism XXI §755).

A common misreading

Two opposite mistakes: "I am terrified, so I have reached the knowledge of fear", and "I am afraid, so I am doing it wrong". The Visuddhimagga puts the knowledge in the seeing, not the feeling (Vism XXI §751); and in the Abhidhamma's terms the two are different moments of mind. Displeasure arises only in cittas rooted in aversion (CMA I §5 and Guide), and the sub-commentaries class fear as trembling of mind with aversion (Majjhima sub-commentary on MN 4, CS §35); the commentary even separates it from a "fear" that is knowledge (Sv on DN 2, CS §159). Insight knowledge is wisdom in a wholesome citta (CMA I §13; Guide to CMA IV §14). So, on this site's reading, the two may follow each other quickly in one stream, but no single moment is both. Mahasi Sayadaw, who reports the fear, draws a line himself: if one stops noting, grief and fear take over, and that fear, he says, is not connected with insight (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 29); the site's own advice is that fear which does not settle is a matter for a teacher and, if it persists, for a professional.

In a picture

The mother at the execution ground, and the pits of embers

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §§750–751

Seeing past formations gone and present ones going, the meditator knows that those still to come will go the same way, and lets go of attachment to them. The knowledge that sees this is a judgement, not a fright.

As the meditator keeps watching dissolution, the Visuddhimagga says, formations in every kind of existence come to appear as a great terror, as lions, tigers, leopards, bears and hyenas, spirits and ogres, savage bulls and dogs, elephants in rut, deadly snakes, thunderbolts, charnel grounds, battlefields and blazing pits of embers appear to a timid man who wants to live in comfort. He sees: past formations have ceased, present ones are ceasing, and those still to come will cease in the same way.

A woman's three sons had offended the king, who ordered them beheaded. She went with them to the place of execution. When she saw the eldest's head cut off, and the middle son's being cut, she let go of her attachment to the youngest: he too will go the way of the others.

Another woman, whose children never survived, had borne ten. Nine were dead, the tenth was dying in her arms, and she was carrying another in her womb. Seeing the nine dead and the tenth dying, she let go of her attachment to the child she was carrying: it too will be like them.

Then the Visuddhimagga asks whether the knowledge of fear is itself afraid, and answers no: it only judges. Someone with good sight looking at three pits of glowing embers by the city gate feels no fear for himself; he simply judges that whoever falls in will be badly hurt. So too a man looking at three stakes set in a row, of acacia wood, of iron and of gold. In the same way the knowledge of fear only judges, of the three kinds of becoming, which are like those pits and stakes, that past formations have ceased, present ones are ceasing, and future ones will cease.

Where the picture stops working: Both mothers are in anguish, as the story implies, and the timid man is frightened; the knowledge is neither. The Visuddhimagga says plainly that the knowledge of fear does not fear, and its sub-commentary explains why: fearing belongs to a mind with aversion, while knowledge simply knows what is to be feared as fearful. What the Visuddhimagga draws from the mothers is the letting go of attachment; the onlooker at the pits is the picture of the knowledge itself. "Fearful" here is the sense in which the Manual calls formations suffering ("in the sense of fearfulness", CMA IX §32); the Guide explains that what is impermanent offers no security, and so is to be feared. And fear is not a separate experience from danger and disenchantment: the Ancients call them one knowledge with three names, and the Paṭisambhidāmagga, quoted there, says they are one in meaning and differ only in the letter (Vism XXI §755).

The snake taken for a fish

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §758 (with §791)

Why the meditator who wants to be free turns back and looks at formations again: laying the three characteristics on them is the way to let them go safely, as swinging the snake is the way to be rid of it.

A man who wanted to catch fish set a fish-trap in the water. He put his hand in through the mouth of the trap and, under the water, took hold of a snake by the neck. He was pleased: he had caught a fish, and a big one. He lifted it out to look, and when he saw three svastika marks on it he knew it for a snake, and was afraid. He saw the danger, he was weary of holding it, and he wanted to let it go.

He went about it carefully. He freed his hand from the coils, beginning at the tail, swung the snake two or three times round above his head to tire it out, and threw it away. Then he scrambled up the bank of the pond and stood there looking back the way he had come: he had escaped from the jaws of a great snake.

The Visuddhimagga tells the story for the knowledge of reflection and keys it step by step. Swinging the snake until it is too weak to turn and bite is the meditator laying the three characteristics on formations until they are too weak to present themselves again as permanent, pleasant, beautiful or self; then he can let them go, and let them go well.

Later, among its twelve similes of the insight that leads to emergence, the Visuddhimagga takes up the same story, which it calls the simile of the black snake, and carries it on to the end: flinging the snake away is change-of-lineage, standing looking back along the way he came is the path, and going on to stand in a place where there is nothing to fear is fruition.

Where the picture stops working: The man is in danger from the snake and gets free by force. Insight weakens formations only by seeing them as they are, and the one who holds and lets go is a way of speaking: what holds is clinging, what lets go is a run of moments of knowledge. The man is afraid; the knowledge of fear is not (Vism XXI §751). The story's steps are not wholly separate experiences: the Ancients call fear, danger and disenchantment one knowledge with three names, and the Paṭisambhidāmagga, quoted there, says they are one in meaning and differ only in the letter (Vism XXI §755); the same is said of desire for deliverance, reflection and equanimity (Vism XXI §§778–781). The story has no step for equanimity or conformity: it goes straight from the swinging to the throw. And standing looking back along the way he came looks like reviewing, but the Visuddhimagga keys it to the path; reviewing comes only after fruition (CMA IX §34).

VI · The way

Danger

ādīnava-ñāṇa

What is seen

No shelter anywhere in becoming: whatever arises is a danger, and safety lies only in what does not arise (Vism XXI §§752–753).

What it can be like

Matara Sri Ñāṇārāma, a modern Sri Lankan teacher, says the meditator feels as if he had come upon a thicket full of leopards, bears, snakes and robbers, with no safe part to choose (The Seven Stages of Purification, p. 48). Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, describes the fault-finding thoughts that come, that it was not good to have been born and that seeking happiness in these things is in vain, and has them noted like anything else (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 30). The canon's plainest case is a frail nun, Dhammā, leaning on a stick on her alms round, who fell to the ground and, seeing the danger in the body, was freed (Thig 1.17; the verse names no stage, but its commentary reads it as the contemplation of danger and the knowledges after it, ThigA on Thig 1.17).

What to watch for

The Visuddhimagga adds that the words "non-arising is safety" are said also to comfort those whose hearts are alarmed by seeing the danger (Vism XXI §753).

A common misreading

"Seeing danger means life is bad and I should despair." The same knowledge that sees danger in whatever arises sees safety in what does not arise (Vism XXI §§752–753). Mahasi Sayadaw tells retreatants that dejection at these stages is a sign of progress (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 29), a teacher's reassurance inside a guided retreat; the site's own advice is that a low mood that lasts into daily life is not a stage to wait out, but something to raise with a teacher and, if it persists, with a professional.

In a picture

The vipers, the killers and the empty village

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · SN 35.238

There is no shelter anywhere in the elements, the aggregates or the senses; safety is only on the far shore. The Visuddhimagga takes up these images for the knowledge of danger.

A man who wants to live and not to die, who wants happiness and shrinks from pain, is handed four vipers of fierce heat and deadly venom. He is told that he must rouse them, bathe them, feed them and put them to rest, each at its proper time, and that if any one of them is angered he will die, or suffer close to death. Afraid of the vipers, he runs off this way or that.

He is told that five murderous enemies are on his heels, to kill him wherever they find him. He runs. He is told that a sixth killer, a hidden one who moves in close to him, is following with a raised sword to cut off his head. He runs, and comes to an empty village: every house he enters is void, every pot he touches is hollow. He is told that village-raiding bandits are coming into it now. He runs again.

He comes to a great stretch of water. The near shore is dangerous and frightening, the far shore safe, and there is no ferry and no bridge. He gathers grass, sticks, branches and leaves, binds them into a raft and, relying on it and working with hands and feet, crosses safely. Crossed over, gone beyond, the brahmin stands on dry ground.

The Visuddhimagga, describing the knowledge of danger, uses the same images without naming the sutta. To one with this knowledge no shelter or refuge appears anywhere in any kind of existence. The three kinds of becoming appear like pits full of glowing embers, the four great elements like deadly vipers, the five aggregates like murderers with raised swords, the six inner sense bases like an empty village, the six outer ones like village-raiding bandits, the stations of consciousness and the abodes of beings as burning with eleven fires; and all formations as a boil, a disease, a dart, a calamity, an affliction: without any satisfaction, a great heap of danger.

Where the picture stops working: A chase story is full of panic and flight. The knowledge of danger sees; it does not run. The Visuddhimagga's own picture for it is a timid man who, in his fright, sees danger on every side (XXI §752), but the point is what he sees, not his fright: of the knowledge of fear it says plainly that it does not fear (§751). In the sutta the picture teaches the elements, aggregates and sense bases, and is not tied to any stage of insight. The Visuddhimagga also moves one image: it gives the raised swords to the five aggregates, where in the sutta the sword belongs to the sixth killer, relishing and lust. The raft and the far shore reach beyond this knowledge to the path and to arahantship: the knowledge of danger only sees that nothing on the near shore is safe, and that there is security only in the unconditioned (Guide to CMA IX §33). The Visuddhimagga counts it one knowledge with fear and disenchantment, under three names (Vism XXI §755). The eleven fires it names match those of SN 35.28; that link is ours, for the Visuddhimagga cites nothing here.

VI · The way

Disenchantment

nibbidā-ñāṇa

What is seen

The mind turns away from formations of every kind and finds no delight in any of them (Vism XXI §754).

What it can be like

Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, gives an everyday image: as a traveller sick of a dirty road still has to keep putting one foot in front of the other, the meditator is tired of noting yet goes on noting (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 30). Even the thought of the happiest life brings no pleasure, and between notings comes the thought that only the ending of all this would be happiness (The Progress of Insight, section 8).

What to watch for

It is not a low mood. The Visuddhimagga's pictures: a royal swan that cares nothing for a filthy pond by a village gate but delights in the great lakes, a lion that takes no joy in a golden cage; so the mind, delighting in no formation, delights in the contemplation and in peace (Vism XXI §754).

A common misreading

"My retreat, my teacher, my life is the problem." Matara Sri Ñāṇārāma, a modern Sri Lankan teacher, says that here a meditator may want to stop, to go somewhere else, even take a dislike to his teacher, and that the teacher should see that the discontent with formations has been shifted onto the practice, the lodging or other people (The Seven Stages of Purification, p. 49); Mahasi Sayadaw says this is not dissatisfaction with meditation but the knowledge itself (The Progress of Insight, section 8). The site's note: disenchantment as a knowledge is about formations; if it turns into hopelessness about one's life, that is a matter for a teacher and, if it persists, for a professional.

VI · The way

Desire for deliverance

muñcitukamyatā-ñāṇa

What is seen

Clinging to none of it, the mind wants to be free of the whole field of formations (Vism XXI §756).

What it can be like

Matara Sri Ñāṇārāma, a modern Sri Lankan teacher, draws a restless monk: he gets up and paces, sits again, turns his seat to face another way, folds and refolds his robe, and makes plans to change his belongings, repair his dwelling, even change other people's attitudes; each impulse, he says, is to be noted and not acted on (The Seven Stages of Purification, p. 50). Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher: bodily pains often come, and between notings the wish to be free of it all (The Progress of Insight, section 9).

What to watch for

The texts count desire for deliverance, reflection and equanimity as one knowledge with three names (Vism XXI §§778–781).

A common misreading

"I'll stop noting; that will set me free." Mahasi Sayadaw says some meditators try it, but rising and falling, bending and intending go on anyway, and so does the knowing of them; seeing that freedom does not come by stopping, they go back to noting, gladly (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 31). The Visuddhimagga's next step says the same in its own way: the way out is to look again (Vism XXI §§757–758).

In a picture

The snake taken for a fish

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §758 (with §791)

Why the meditator who wants to be free turns back and looks at formations again: laying the three characteristics on them is the way to let them go safely, as swinging the snake is the way to be rid of it.

A man who wanted to catch fish set a fish-trap in the water. He put his hand in through the mouth of the trap and, under the water, took hold of a snake by the neck. He was pleased: he had caught a fish, and a big one. He lifted it out to look, and when he saw three svastika marks on it he knew it for a snake, and was afraid. He saw the danger, he was weary of holding it, and he wanted to let it go.

He went about it carefully. He freed his hand from the coils, beginning at the tail, swung the snake two or three times round above his head to tire it out, and threw it away. Then he scrambled up the bank of the pond and stood there looking back the way he had come: he had escaped from the jaws of a great snake.

The Visuddhimagga tells the story for the knowledge of reflection and keys it step by step. Swinging the snake until it is too weak to turn and bite is the meditator laying the three characteristics on formations until they are too weak to present themselves again as permanent, pleasant, beautiful or self; then he can let them go, and let them go well.

Later, among its twelve similes of the insight that leads to emergence, the Visuddhimagga takes up the same story, which it calls the simile of the black snake, and carries it on to the end: flinging the snake away is change-of-lineage, standing looking back along the way he came is the path, and going on to stand in a place where there is nothing to fear is fruition.

Where the picture stops working: The man is in danger from the snake and gets free by force. Insight weakens formations only by seeing them as they are, and the one who holds and lets go is a way of speaking: what holds is clinging, what lets go is a run of moments of knowledge. VI · The way

Reflection

paṭisaṅkhā-ñāṇa

What is seen

Wanting out, the meditator turns back to the same formations and goes over them as impermanent, suffering and not self, in many ways. This is how one gets free, the Visuddhimagga says, like a man who finds he has grabbed a snake, not a fish, and swings it round till it is weak enough to throw away (Vism XXI §§757–758).

What it can be like

Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher: pains may come, and the meditator cannot settle in any posture; sitting, he thinks walking will be better; walking, he wants to sit; he shifts his limbs, and wants to go elsewhere or to lie down (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 31–32).

A common misreading

"My noting is not close enough; my insight is not clear." Mahasi Sayadaw says the meditator is in fact doing well but feels otherwise, because he is too eager to see the three marks fully (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 31–32). His advice for the restlessness is to try to keep to one posture (p. 32), but his basic instructions are just as clear that when fatigue or stiffness becomes unbearable, one changes position, noting the intention first (p. 6); the site's own advice: nothing here is a reason to endure pain.

VI · The way

Equanimity about formations

saṅkhārupekkhā-ñāṇa

What is seen

Formations are seen as empty of self and of anything belonging to a self, and the mind, dropping both fear and delight, looks on them evenly (Vism XXI §§760–767; Guide to CMA IX §33).

What it can be like

Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher: noting now runs smoothly, as if without effort; touch feels soft as cotton wool; objects become few and easy; rapture, calm and a brightness like a clear sky may come, but they no longer carry the meditator away (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 32–33). Matara Sri Ñāṇārāma, a modern Sri Lankan teacher, compares it to a chariot drawn by well-trained horses, and says thoughts of liking and disliking slide off the mind like water drops off a lotus leaf (The Seven Stages of Purification, p. 52).

What to watch for

If it sees Nibbāna as peace it goes on towards the path; if not, it turns back to formations again and again, like a ship's crow that flies off to find land and returns when it sees none (Vism XXI §767).

A common misreading

"The goal is near; now I must push." Mahasi Sayadaw: anxiety, anticipation and extra effort make the noting slack, and the meditator slips back; some rise and fall back several times; the way is to go on steadily, neither pushing nor relaxing (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 33–35), and his image for those ups and downs is the Visuddhimagga's ship's crow (p. 35; Vism XXI §767). On this site's reading, this equanimity is not coldness towards people: in the Visuddhimagga it is the end of taking formations as "I" or "mine" (Vism XXI §766).

The man who let his wife go, and the direction crow

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §§766–767

Equanimity about formations is the end of "mine", not coldness: with nothing taken as I or mine, fear and delight both drop away. And while it does not yet see Nibbāna as peace, it keeps coming back to formations.

A man had a wife he loved so dearly that he could not bear to be without her for a moment. He held her as his own above everything, and if he saw her standing or sitting, talking or laughing with another man, he was angry and deeply unhappy. Later he saw her faults, wanted to be free, and let her go: he no longer took her as his. From then on, whatever she did and with whomever, he was neither angry nor unhappy, only uninvolved and even.

So it is with the meditator, the Visuddhimagga says. Wanting to be free of all formations, he examines them by the contemplation of reflection, finds nothing in them to take as I or mine, gives up both fear and delight, and becomes uninvolved and even towards all formations. His mind then draws back from every kind of existence and does not reach out to it, as drops of water on a lotus leaf that slopes a little gather up and run off instead of spreading, or as a feather or a strip of sinew shrivels in the fire (it quotes AN 7.49 for the feather and sinew).

Sea traders take aboard a bird they call the direction crow. When a storm drives the ship far out and no land can be seen, they let it go. It rises from the masthead and flies to every quarter. If it sees the shore, it flies straight there; if not, it comes back to the mast again and again. In the same way, if this knowledge sees Nibbāna, the state of peace, as peace, it lets go of all formations and launches into Nibbāna; if not, it goes on again and again with formations as its object.

Meanwhile it keeps working over formations in many ways, like flour turned on a winnowing tray, or cotton with its seeds taken out being carded with a bow. The sub-commentary explains both pictures: the coarse is separated from the fine, as insight grows finer with each new round of comprehension.

Where the picture stops working: Read carelessly, the wife's story praises indifference to people. It is about formations, and about the end of taking them as mine; what is dropped is fear at their failing and delight in their success (so the sub-commentary). The calm is earned: the Visuddhimagga says the man lets go because he wants to be free, and the sub-commentary counts all insight from desire for deliverance until equanimity as reflection. The Ancients call the three one knowledge with three names, and the Paṭisambhidāmagga says they are one in meaning (Vism XXI §§778–781). The crow is restless, the knowledge even; and its coming back is not failure, only a knowledge not yet ripe. When the crow reaches the shore it sounds as if equanimity itself takes Nibbāna as its object. The sub-commentary says that here the knowledge is spoken of as one with conformity and change-of-lineage; the first moment that takes Nibbāna as object is change-of-lineage (CMA IX §34 and Guide). The bird recalls the land-spotting bird of the suttas: in DN 11 it stands for a monk who searched for an answer as far as the Brahmā world and came back to the Buddha, and in AN 6.54 for a monk turned out of his monasteries who came back to him. The Visuddhimagga cites neither; the parallel is ours.

VI · The way

Conformity

anuloma-ñāṇa

What is seen

The last two or three moments of insight, just before change-of-lineage, still taking formations as impermanent, suffering or not self (CMA IX §34 and Guide). It agrees with the eight knowledges before it and with the thirty-seven aids to awakening that the path will bring (Vism XXI §804).

What it can be like

The texts make this a matter of two or three moments of mind (CMA IX §34). Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, describes the run-up as meditators know it: noting grows faster and clearer, and one of the three marks shows two or three times or more in quick succession, just before the change (The Progress of Insight, section 12; "three or four times" in Practical Insight Meditation, p. 36).

What to watch for

With equanimity it is called insight leading to emergence, the peak of insight (CMA IX §34; Vism XXI §782).

A common misreading

"Conformity is a stage to work at." Matara Sri Ñāṇārāma, a modern Sri Lankan teacher: there is nothing new to do; it arises by itself when equanimity about formations is ripe (The Seven Stages of Purification, p. 53).

In a picture

The leap, the moon and the archer

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXII, CS §§807–809

What each moment at the threshold of the path does: conformity clears the way, change-of-lineage sees Nibbāna for the first time, and the path breaks through greed, hatred and delusion never broken before.

The leap. A man wants to land on the far side of a wide ditch. He runs, grabs a rope or pole hanging from a tree on this side, springs up, swings across leaning towards the far bank, lets go when he is above it, and lands there, a little unsteadily, until he steadies himself. The Visuddhimagga says the run is the knowledge of rise and fall and the insights after it; the rope or pole is one of the aggregates, seen as impermanent, suffering or not-self. The spring, the lean and arriving above the far bank are the three conformity moments. Letting go and landing is change-of-lineage, the first moment that has Nibbāna as its object. It is unsteady because no moment before it has taken that object, so it has nothing to repeat; what makes him stand firm is the path.

The moon. A man goes out at night to see the moon, but clouds hide it. A first wind blows away the thick clouds, a second the middling ones, a third the thin ones, and then he sees the moon. The winds can clear clouds but cannot see; the man can see but cannot clear clouds. So the conformity moments dispel the darkness that hides the truths but cannot see Nibbāna, and change-of-lineage sees Nibbāna but cannot dispel that darkness.

The archer. An archer stands, his face wrapped in cloth, on a turning wheel, with a hundred planks set up some way off. A helper spins the wheel and, when the archer faces the planks, gives him a sign with a tap of a stick. Not letting go of that signal, the archer shoots, and the arrow goes through all hundred planks. The signal is change-of-lineage; the archer is the path knowledge, which splits masses of greed, hatred and delusion never split before.

Where the picture stops working: The leaper and the archer are each one person all through. At the threshold there is no one: each stage is a different moment of mind, each conditioning the next. The Visuddhimagga speaks of three conformity moments. The Manual names the moments before change-of-lineage preparation, access and conformity, and allows two or three (CMA IX §34); the Guide explains that one of unusually keen faculties skips preparation and has two. And the passage is told of the first path, stream-entry, which does not yet end all greed, hatred and delusion.

VII · Knowledge and vision

The path

magga-ñāṇa

What is seen

A single moment that takes Nibbāna as object and uproots for good the defilements this path abandons. In that one moment it fully understands suffering, abandons its origin, realises its ending and develops the way (CMA IX §34 and Guide; Vism XXII §839, the lamp).

What it can be like

The texts say what the path does, not how it feels. The canon's words for what is seen are short: whatever is subject to arising is subject to ceasing (Vin Mv I.7.6, pli-tv-kd1:7.6.5; the same words in the first sermon, sn56.11:11.1–11.2), and the commentary on the first sermon takes that "eye of the Dhamma" there as the path of stream-entry, though elsewhere it can mean any of the first three paths (SN-a on SN 56.11, CS §1081). Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, passes on what meditators told him: that object and noting stopped all at once, like a creeper cut with a knife or a heavy load put down (Practical Insight Meditation, p. 36). The site's note: these are second-hand reports of how the moment seemed, not descriptions of Nibbāna; in the Abhidhamma the path is a single moment of mind that takes Nibbāna as its object (CMA IX §34; Guide to CMA I §§26–28).

What to watch for

There are four paths. The first, stream-entry, abandons wrong view and doubt (CMA IX §38); the Guide names the three fetters it cuts: self-view, doubt and clinging to rules and rituals (Guide to CMA IX §38). The others go further (§§39–41).

A common misreading

"After the path, greed and anger are gone." Stream-entry cuts three fetters, self-view, doubt and clinging to rules and rituals (Guide to CMA IX §38); at the next path greed, hate and delusion are thinned, their grosser forms gone, and they end only at the later paths (CMA IX §§39–41 and Guide). The canon has a lay noble disciple, Mahānāma the Sakyan (a once-returner, the commentary says), puzzled that greed, hate and delusion still sometimes took hold of his mind; the Buddha told him something in him was not yet given up (MN 14, mn14:2.1–3.3; Ps on MN 14, CS §175).

In a picture

The leap, the moon and the archer

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXII, CS §§807–809

What each moment at the threshold of the path does: conformity clears the way, change-of-lineage sees Nibbāna for the first time, and the path breaks through greed, hatred and delusion never broken before.

The leap. A man wants to land on the far side of a wide ditch. He runs, grabs a rope or pole hanging from a tree on this side, springs up, swings across leaning towards the far bank, lets go when he is above it, and lands there, a little unsteadily, until he steadies himself. The Visuddhimagga says the run is the knowledge of rise and fall and the insights after it; the rope or pole is one of the aggregates, seen as impermanent, suffering or not-self. The spring, the lean and arriving above the far bank are the three conformity moments. Letting go and landing is change-of-lineage, the first moment that has Nibbāna as its object. It is unsteady because no moment before it has taken that object, so it has nothing to repeat; what makes him stand firm is the path.

The moon. A man goes out at night to see the moon, but clouds hide it. A first wind blows away the thick clouds, a second the middling ones, a third the thin ones, and then he sees the moon. The winds can clear clouds but cannot see; the man can see but cannot clear clouds. So the conformity moments dispel the darkness that hides the truths but cannot see Nibbāna, and change-of-lineage sees Nibbāna but cannot dispel that darkness.

The archer. An archer stands, his face wrapped in cloth, on a turning wheel, with a hundred planks set up some way off. A helper spins the wheel and, when the archer faces the planks, gives him a sign with a tap of a stick. Not letting go of that signal, the archer shoots, and the arrow goes through all hundred planks. The signal is change-of-lineage; the archer is the path knowledge, which splits masses of greed, hatred and delusion never split before.

Where the picture stops working: The leaper and the archer are each one person all through. At the threshold there is no one: each stage is a different moment of mind, each conditioning the next. The Visuddhimagga speaks of three conformity moments. The Manual names the moments before change-of-lineage preparation, access and conformity, and allows two or three (CMA IX §34); the Guide explains that one of unusually keen faculties skips preparation and has two. And the passage is told of the first path, stream-entry, which does not yet end all greed, hatred and delusion.

The tree without its branches and leaves

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · AN 5.24

Each step of the path is the supporting condition of the next, beginning from virtue. Damage the branches and leaves, and nothing inside the tree grows to fullness.

In one who lacks virtue, the Buddha said, right concentration has lost its support. Without right concentration, the knowledge and vision of things as they really are has lost its support; without that, disenchantment and fading away; and without those, the knowledge and vision of release.

It is like a tree whose branches and leaves are damaged: its outer bark does not grow to fullness, nor its inner bark, nor its sapwood, nor its heartwood.

In one who has virtue it is the other way round. Each step has its support, as in a tree whose branches and leaves are whole the outer bark, the inner bark, the sapwood and the heartwood all grow to fullness. The same tree comes back in other suttas of the Aṅguttara, with longer chains of steps.

The commentary on the Aṅguttara reads the chain as the path of insight. The knowledge and vision of things as they really are is tender insight, beginning from the knowledge that defines mind and matter. Disenchantment is strong insight. Fading away is the path. The knowledge and vision of release is fruition, together with the reviewing knowledge.

Where the picture stops working: A tree grows by itself, slowly and without a break. Insight passes through distinct knowledges, and the path is a single moment (Guide to CMA I §§26–28; CMA IX §34). The sutta names virtue and right concentration but none of the purifications; setting them beside the purification of virtue and of mind (CMA IX §§28–29) is this site's reading. And "disenchantment" here, in the commentary's sense of strong insight, is wider than the single knowledge of disenchantment in the Manual's list (Guide to CMA IX §33); the commentary does not say where strong insight begins. So too, "tender insight" here is the commentary's name for all insight from defining mind and matter onward. That is wider than the Visuddhimagga's tender insight, which is only the first phase of the knowledge of rise and fall (Vism XX §731; Guide to CMA IX §32).

The snake taken for a fish

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §758 (with §791)

Why the meditator who wants to be free turns back and looks at formations again: laying the three characteristics on them is the way to let them go safely, as swinging the snake is the way to be rid of it.

A man who wanted to catch fish set a fish-trap in the water. He put his hand in through the mouth of the trap and, under the water, took hold of a snake by the neck. He was pleased: he had caught a fish, and a big one. He lifted it out to look, and when he saw three svastika marks on it he knew it for a snake, and was afraid. He saw the danger, he was weary of holding it, and he wanted to let it go.

He went about it carefully. He freed his hand from the coils, beginning at the tail, swung the snake two or three times round above his head to tire it out, and threw it away. Then he scrambled up the bank of the pond and stood there looking back the way he had come: he had escaped from the jaws of a great snake.

The Visuddhimagga tells the story for the knowledge of reflection and keys it step by step. Swinging the snake until it is too weak to turn and bite is the meditator laying the three characteristics on formations until they are too weak to present themselves again as permanent, pleasant, beautiful or self; then he can let them go, and let them go well.

Later, among its twelve similes of the insight that leads to emergence, the Visuddhimagga takes up the same story, which it calls the simile of the black snake, and carries it on to the end: flinging the snake away is change-of-lineage, standing looking back along the way he came is the path, and going on to stand in a place where there is nothing to fear is fruition.

Where the picture stops working: The man is in danger from the snake and gets free by force. Insight weakens formations only by seeing them as they are, and the one who holds and lets go is a way of speaking: what holds is clinging, what lets go is a run of moments of knowledge. The man is afraid; the knowledge of fear is not (Vism XXI §751). The story's steps are not wholly separate experiences: the Ancients call fear, danger and disenchantment one knowledge with three names, and the Paṭisambhidāmagga, quoted there, says they are one in meaning and differ only in the letter (Vism XXI §755); the same is said of desire for deliverance, reflection and equanimity (Vism XXI §§778–781). The story has no step for equanimity or conformity: it goes straight from the swinging to the throw. And standing looking back along the way he came looks like reviewing, but the Visuddhimagga keys it to the path; reviewing comes only after fruition (CMA IX §34).

Fruition

phala

What is seen

Two or three moments that follow the path at once, with the same object, Nibbāna; later the noble person can enter fruition again as an attainment, as CMA IX §42 describes (CMA IX §34).

A common misreading

"Fruition is the bliss that comes afterwards." Mahasi Sayadaw, a modern Burmese teacher, reports that after the path some meditators are flooded with faith, rapture and happiness for an hour or two or more, as if in a broad and brightly lit space, and cannot note anything for a while (The Progress of Insight, section 17); he describes it after the path, fruition and reviewing, not as fruition. In the Abhidhamma fruition is the two or three moments right after the path, with Nibbāna as object, and the later attainment of the same (CMA IX §§34, 42); on its analysis the joy that follows is in sense-sphere cittas, which may even review Nibbāna, but are not fruition.

Water poured where the fire was

From the commentaries · Vibhāvinī on CMA IX §34, CS §59

Fruition cuts nothing new. Right after the path it calms the heat that is left in a mind whose defilements the path has already cut.

The Manual says that after the path two or three moments of fruition consciousness arise and cease, and then the mind sinks back into the life-continuum.

The Vibhāvinī, the commentary on the Manual, pictures those moments. A fire has been carried away, but the ground where it burned is still hot, and water is poured on it from pots to cool it. So the two or three fruition cittas, two or three according to how the path arose, occur in a continuum whose defilements have already been cut off, and calm the distress that remains there.

Where the picture stops working: Someone pours the water, from outside and after a pause. Fruition is the path's own result, arising at once, with no gap and no one doing it (Vism XXII §811; CMA IX §34). Whether there are two moments or three depends on the path process: three for one of keen faculties who skips the moment of preparation, two for others (Guide to CMA IX §34); the Visuddhimagga rejects the view that there can be one or four. The picture covers only these first moments. Fruition can later be entered again and, with practice, held for many moments in the attainment of fruition, which the picture does not show (Guide to CMA I §§26–28; CMA IX §42 and Guide). And "distress" is the Vibhāvinī's word; it does not mean that defilements cut by this path remain.

Reviewing

paccavekkhaṇa-ñāṇa

What is seen

Afterwards the mind looks back over the path, the fruit and Nibbāna, and, usually, over the defilements abandoned and those still remaining (CMA IX §34 and Guide).

What it can be like

The Theragāthā opens by saying that the elders, having seen with insight and reached the state that does not pass, spoke their verses reviewing what they had done (Thag 1.1, thag1.1:3.1–3.4), and its commentary reads this as the reviewing knowledges, all nineteen (ThagA on the opening verses). On that reading, which the commentary extends to the nuns' verses too, many of these verses can be heard as reviewing put into words, like Subhūti's: his hut is roofed and sheltered, so let the rain come (thag1.1:4.1–4.4).

A common misreading

Two things with the same name are not this knowledge. The section called "Reviewing" in Mahasi Sayadaw's Practical Insight Meditation (pp. 40–41) is an exercise of deliberately going back over earlier knowledges for set periods; and when Yasa, already a stream-enterer, became an arahant while reviewing the ground he had seen (Vin Mv I.7.11, pli-tv-kd1:7.11.1), that was further practice. Reviewing knowledge (paccavekkhaṇapaccavekkhaṇa · reviewing after attainment, knowledge looking back on path, fruition and Nibbāna, and usually, not always, on the defilements abandoned and those that remain) comes of itself right after path and fruit, and need not cover everything: the defilements, the Guide says, are usually but not always reviewed (Guide to CMA IX §34), and the Visuddhimagga's example of one who did not review them is Mahānāma (Vism XXII §812; MN 14).

What insight sees in everything

From comprehension to conformity, each knowledge sees the same three marks more clearly: what arises ceases (impermanence), what ceases cannot be a safe resting place (suffering), and nothing in it is a self or a self's (non-self) (Guide to CMA IX §§23, 32; Vism XXI §760). The Visuddhimagga says what keeps them hidden: an unbroken run of moments hides impermanence, changing posture hides suffering, and the look of a solid whole hides non-self (Vism XXI §739).

The iron rods and the lump of foam

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §739

What hides the three characteristics, and what it looks like when the cover is broken: things come apart like separate rods, and the solid mass melts like foam.

Why do the three characteristics not show? The Visuddhimagga answers: impermanence does not show because rise and fall are not attended to, and continuity hides it. Suffering does not show because the constant oppression is not attended to, and the postures hide it. Not-self does not show because things are not resolved into their elements, and compactness hides it. When rise and fall are grasped and continuity is broken, impermanence shows as it really is; when the oppression is attended to and the postures are seen through, suffering shows; when the elements are taken apart and the compactness is broken up, not-self shows.

This comes after the imperfections, when the knowledge of rise and fall, freed of them, is taken up again for just this purpose: to mark the characteristics.

The sub-commentary gives two pictures. Continuity: to one who rightly watches rise and fall, the arising phase of a state never reaches its falling phase, nor the falling its arising, and states no longer show up as joined in a series. They show up unconnected, like separate iron rods, and impermanence becomes plainer still.

The postures it explains without a picture: a change of posture that relieves the pain born in another works as its cover, and so with each of them. Once this is seen for what it is, the ceaseless oppression of formations shows.

Compactness, it says, is of three kinds: of mass, taking states that hold each other up as one thing; of function, taking their different functions as one; and of object, taking their different ways of taking an object as one. When knowledge separates the elements (earth is one thing, water another; contact one thing, feeling another), these compactnesses dissolve like a lump of foam rubbed in the hand, and not-self shows: these are mere states, empty, occurring as their conditions allow.

Where the picture stops working: These are two pictures and one explanation, all from the sub-commentary, not the Visuddhimagga, which gives none. Rods lying apart are lasting things side by side; states are not, for each rises and falls in a moment. The foam recalls the lump of foam in SN 22.95, there a picture of form, but the sub-commentary does not cite it; the link is ours. Breaking the compact destroys nothing: the single mass was never there. The Manual does not name the three concealments; it uses continuity only as a way of comprehending (CMA IX §32 and Guide), and the three are the Visuddhimagga's (XXI §739).

Insight weakens formations only by seeing them as they are, and the one who holds and lets go is a way of speaking: what holds is clinging, what lets go is a run of moments of knowledge. The man is afraid; the knowledge of fear is not (Vism XXI §751). The story's steps are not wholly separate experiences: the Ancients call fear, danger and disenchantment one knowledge with three names, and the Paṭisambhidāmagga, quoted there, says they are one in meaning and differ only in the letter (Vism XXI §755); the same is said of desire for deliverance, reflection and equanimity (Vism XXI §§778–781). The story has no step for equanimity or conformity: it goes straight from the swinging to the throw. And standing looking back along the way he came looks like reviewing, but the Visuddhimagga keys it to the path; reviewing comes only after fruition (CMA IX §34).
