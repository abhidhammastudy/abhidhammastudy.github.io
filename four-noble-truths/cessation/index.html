The third noble truth

The cessation of suffering nirodha

When craving fades and ends completely, suffering ends too: that ending is Nibbāna, the one reality not made by causes.

In brief

  • The third truth is the ending of suffering, by the complete fading and ending of craving (SN 56.11).
  • That ending is Nibbāna, which is unconditioned: the path reaches it and does not make it.
  • Its task is to be realised, never developed or produced.
  • Cessation is defined as the ending of craving, the cause, and not of suffering directly.

What it means

In the sermon cessation is the complete fading and ending of that same craving (SN 56.11, sn56.11:4.7). It is called cessation because in reaching it craving ceases, and the Manual explains the word nibbānanibbāna the unconditioned: the ending of craving; object of path and fruition itself as a departure from craving (CMA VI §30 and Guide).

Cessation is not an event the path brings about. Nibbāna is unconditioned, reached by the path and not produced by it (Vism XVI §560, commentarial).

Why define it by the cause and not by suffering itself? Because, Buddhaghosa says, the Tathāgatas are like lions, who go for the cause, while other teachers are like dogs, who go for the result: they would end suffering by tormenting the body (Vism XVI §554, commentarial). And it comes third in the order to show that when the cause ends, the result ends (Vism XVI §536).

Its task: to be realised · sacchikātabba

Cessation is to be realised (SN 56.11, sn56.11:7.2). This task is not done by working on cessation itself: it is realised when the path that is developed reaches it. Before the path it is known only by learning and hearsay; penetration, which is supramundane, takes all four truths at once (Vism XVI §563, commentarial).

In Abhidhamma terms

Nibbānanibbāna the unconditioned: the ending of craving; object of path and fruition, realised by the eradication of craving (Guide to CMA VII §38). With the path it makes the second pair, beyond the round: both supramundane, the one the object, the other what takes it as object (Vism XVI §569, commentarial).

Not to be confused with

Something to be developed or produced. Only the path is developed; cessation is realised. In the commentaries' image of the lamp, which in one moment burns the wick, dispels the dark, makes light appear and uses up the oil, cessation is the using-up of the oil, in the same moment as the other three (Vism XXII §839, commentarial).

A story

Koṇḍañña's eye of Dhamma: At the first sermon in the Deer Park, while the Buddha was speaking, Koṇḍañña understood: “Whatever is subject to arising is all subject to cessation.” The Buddha exclaimed, “Koṇḍañña has understood!”
