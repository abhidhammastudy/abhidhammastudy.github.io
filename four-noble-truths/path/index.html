The fourth noble truth

The path to its cessation magga

The way to that ending is the noble eightfold path: right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration.

In brief

  • The fourth truth is the way to the end of suffering: the noble eightfold path, which the first sermon calls the middle way (SN 56.11).
  • Its task is to be developed: it is the only one of the four to be developed.
  • As the fourth truth it is only supramundane: the path factors as they arise together in the four path cittas, all eight, or seven at the higher levels of absorption.
  • The good moments of ordinary practice lead to it, but in the teaching of the noble truths they still count under suffering.

What it means

The sermon begins with the path before it reaches the truths. Between a life given over to sense pleasure and a life of self-torment, the Buddha says, lies a middle way, and that middle way is the noble eightfold path (SN 56.11, sn56.11:2.1–3.4). As the fourth truth it is the eight factors, right view to right concentration (sn56.11:4.9–4.10).

The path closes a circle. Its first factor, right view, is knowledge of suffering, its origin, its cessation and the way to its cessation (SN 45.8, sn45.8:3.1–3.2). The path begins by knowing the four truths, and it is itself the last of them.

It reaches cessation without making it: it takes Nibbāna as its object (Vism XVI §§560, 569, commentarial). At each of the four paths one knowledge does all four tasks at once: it fully understands suffering, abandons craving, realises cessation and develops the path (CMA IX §34 and Guide).

Its task: to be developed · bhāvetabba

The path is the one truth to be developed (SN 56.11, sn56.11:8.2). Before the path moment its factors are developed in varying combinations, according to circumstances, in the course of the six purifications (CMA VII §33 and Guide). At the path moment they are developed by conascence: they arise together and support each other, as in the commentaries' images of the lamp, the rising sun and the boat (Vism XXII §§839–841, commentarial).

In Abhidhamma terms

Mental factors, only as they arise in the four supramundane path cittas (Guide to CMA VII §38): wisdom as right view, initial application as right intention, the three abstinences, energy as right effort, mindfulness, and one-pointedness as right concentration. A path at the level of the first jhāna has all eight; from the second jhāna up, initial application and with it right intention is absent, and the path has seven (CMA II §19; Guide to VII §§32–33). On the Manual's strict count the path citta itself and its other factors, and the fruitions, fall outside all four truths (CMA VII §40 and Guide).

Not to be confused with

Every good state. Mundane right view or right effort is still the practice, and the path in a looser sense, but as the fourth noble truth the path is only supramundane: ordinary good moments count under suffering (Guide to VII §38; Vism XVI §564, commentarial).

A story

The whole of the holy life: Ānanda said that good friendship is half of the holy life. “Not so, Ānanda,” said the Buddha. “Good friendship is the whole of the holy life.” A monk with a good friend can be expected to develop the noble eightfold path.
