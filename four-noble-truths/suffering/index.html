The first noble truth

Suffering dukkha

More than pain: birth, ageing and death, and everything we can cling to, pleasant things included, comes from conditions and passes away.

In brief

  • Suffering is the first of the four noble truths, set out in the Buddha's first sermon (SN 56.11).
  • The word means more than pain: it names what is conditioned and can be clung to, pleasant states included.
  • Its task is to be fully understood, not got rid of.
  • On the Manual's count it is everything of the three mundane planes except craving: the round of existence.

What it means

In the first sermon the Buddha names suffering first: birth, ageing, sickness and death, meeting what is unloved, parting from what is loved, not getting what one wishes; in short, the five aggregates of clinging (SN 56.11, sn56.11:4.2).

The suttas know three kinds of suffering: suffering as pain, suffering in change, and the suffering of formations (SN 38.14; SN 45.165). The commentary puts painful feeling under the first, pleasant feeling under the second, and neutral feeling, with all the other formations of the three planes, under the third (Vism XVI §539, commentarial). So the first truth takes in pleasant and wholesome mundane states too. They are conditioned, they pass, and they belong to the round.

Why does it come first? The commentary's answer is that suffering is gross and shared by all; then comes its cause, then its ending, then the way to it (Vism XVI §536, commentarial).

Its task: to be fully understood · pariññeyya

Suffering is to be fully understood, not got rid of (SN 56.11, sn56.11:5.2). The common slip in practice is to fight it: resisting pain only adds a second dart, mental pain on top of the bodily (SN 36.6). Abandoning is the task for craving, not for suffering. Before the path, this understanding is insight: seeing the formations of the three planes as impermanent, suffering and not self (CMA IX §§32–33).

In Abhidhamma terms

Everything of the three mundane planes except craving (Guide to CMA VII §38). Counted out, which is this site's spelling-out and not the Guide's: the 81 mundane cittas, every mental factor that arises in them except greed, and all 28 kinds of matter. So, in the teaching of the noble truths, even the path factors of ordinary good moments belong here (Guide to VII §38; Vism XVI §564, commentarial). As realities they are among what is to be understood; as practice they are still to be developed.

Not to be confused with

Suffering as a feeling. As feeling, dukkhadukkha · bodily pain painful bodily feeling: only in unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness is pain; as the first truth it is what is conditioned and can be clung to. The supramundane states are conditioned too, but they are not aggregates of clinging, and so not this truth (Vism XVI §566, commentarial).

A story

Kisāgotamī and the mustard seed: Mad with grief, Kisāgotamī carried her dead child from house to house asking for medicine. The Buddha asked her for a mustard seed from a house where no one had died. She found none, laid the child down, and understood that death comes to all.
