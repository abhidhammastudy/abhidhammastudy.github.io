A chariot of parts

Where the Abhidhamma begins, with a nun's answer to Māra, and how a person is taken apart without being called a lie.

About 7 minutesManual I §2 (the four ultimate realities)

In brief

  • Asked by Māra who made this being, the nun Vajirā answered that "a being", like "chariot", is what we say when the parts are present.
  • Speech about persons and things is conventional truth, not falsehood; ultimate truth is what remains when analysis reaches things that keep their own nature.
  • The Abhidhamma finds four ultimate realities: consciousness (citta), the mental factors that colour it, matter, and Nibbāna, which neither arises nor passes away.
  • The five aggregates of the suttas are counted again in these terms, and Nibbāna is in none of them, since it is where clinging ends.
  • Every later chapter builds on this list, so mind, kamma and rebirth are all described without ever needing a self to hold them together.

The nun Vajirā was sitting in meditation in the Blind Men’s Grove when Māra came to unsettle her. Who made this being, he asked her; where does it come from, and where does it go? Her answer is a good place to begin the Abhidhamma. Why do you assume there is a being? This is a heap of mere formations. When the parts are put together we say “chariot”. When the aggregates are present, we say “a being”.noteRetold after SN 5.10. Bhikkhu Sujato’s translation is in the public domain on SuttaCentral.

She did not say there is no chariot. She said what the word refers to. That distinction, between what is true in speech and what remains when speech is analysed, is the first thing the Abhidhamma asks a reader to hold.

Two ways of being true

The commentaries call them the two truths. Conventional truth, sammutisammuti · convention conventional truth: speech about persons and things; true, but not ultimate, is how people speak: persons, chariots, mangoes, “I” and “you”. It is not false. The Buddha used it all his life, and no vow, debt or kindness could be stated without it. Ultimate truth, paramatthaparamattha · ultimate what remains after analysis: citta, cetasika, matter and Nibbāna, is what the analysis ends in: things that keep their own nature however closely you look.noteThe pair first appears as technical terms in the Kathāvatthu and is systematised in the commentaries. They connect it with a distinction the suttas already make (AN 2.25), between discourses whose meaning must be drawn out and discourses whose meaning is plain.

A chariot fails that test. Take away the wheels, the axle, the pole, and no chariot is left over among them; nor is it anywhere else. Take apart a moment of anger and something is left that cannot be taken further: ferocity, the same wherever it occurs. That is a dhammadhamma an ultimate reality, bearing its own nature; not a mere name or concept.

Four kinds of reality

The Abhidhamma’s catalogue of what remains is short. There are four kinds of ultimate reality.

Cittacitta · consciousness one moment of knowing an object; 89 kinds, or 121 counted in detail, consciousness, is the bare knowing of an object, one moment at a time. The Abhidhamma counts 89 kinds of it, or 121 when the higher states are counted in detail.

Cetasikacetasika · mental factor a mental factor: arises and ceases with a citta, sharing its object and bases, mental factors, are what colour each knowing: feeling, perception, volition, attention, and fifty-two in all, from greed to compassion. They arise and cease together with the citta, sharing its object and its base.

Rūparūpa · matter matter: the third reality, 28 kinds; as an aggregate it is called form, matter, comes in twenty-eight kinds: the four great elements and what is derived from them, from hardness and heat to the eye’s sensitivity and the nourishment in food.

Nibbānanibbāna the unconditioned: the ending of craving; object of path and fruition is the fourth, and it is unlike the other three. It does not arise and does not pass away. It is not made of anything, and nothing is made from it.

The aggregates, counted again

The suttas sort experience into five khandhakhandha · aggregate one of the five heaps into which the suttas sort all experiences: form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. The Abhidhamma does not replace this list; it recounts it. Form is matter. Consciousness is citta. Feeling and perception are two of the cetasikas, important enough to stand alone. Formations are the other fifty mental factors.

Nibbāna appears in none of the aggregates. The aggregates are what clinging takes up; Nibbāna is where clinging ends.

In one sutta the Buddha compares the aggregates to a lump of foam on a river, bubbles in the rain, a mirage, the trunk of a plantain tree, which has no heartwood, and a conjuror’s illusion (SN 22.95). Each simile points to the same absence: when you look for the thing itself, the core is not there.

Why begin here

A manual could begin with ethics or with meditation. Anuruddha begins with this list, and every later chapter assumes it. It changes what the rest of the book is about. Processes of mind will be sequences of cittas. Kamma will be a cetasika, volition. Rebirth will be a relation between one citta and the next. At no point will the book need a self to hold the story together.

That is the claim the Abhidhamma makes, and the reason for its patience with lists. If experience can be described completely without an owner, the owner was never needed. Vajirā was sitting in meditation when she said it, and she spoke from what she had seen.

If “chariot” is not a thing, what is the word? The Manual answers at the very end of its account of conditions: see Names and the named.

The ideas in this reading

  • Two truths conventional, ultimateTwo ways of speaking: of persons and things, and of the ultimate dhammas they resolve into. The starting point of the Abhidhamma.
  • Citta consciousnessConsciousness: one moment of knowing an object. The Abhidhamma counts 89 kinds.
  • Cetasika mental factorsThe mental factors that colour each citta.
  • Rūpa matterMatter: 28 kinds, always arising in groups of eight or more.
  • Nibbāna unconditionedThe one unconditioned reality, known in the moments of awakening called path and fruition.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Pick something you call "mine", a hand or a habit or a name. Taken apart into the four realities, where does the "mine" go, and what still works without it?

One way to see it, after you have tried

The hand is matter of several kinds, renewed moment by moment. Feeling it is a citta with its factors. "Mine" appears in neither. It is a concept that later cittas apply. Nothing practical is lost: the hand still lifts, the habit still runs, the name still answers. What goes is the owner standing behind them, and with it a reason for much of grasping.
