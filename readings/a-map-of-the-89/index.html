A map of the eighty-nine

Every kind of consciousness the Abhidhamma knows, sorted by plane, by root and by feeling, and what the sorting reveals.

About 8 minutesManual I §§3–32 (all 89/121 cittas)

In brief

  • The Abhidhamma counts eighty-nine kinds of consciousness (citta), each fixed by its roots, feeling, plane, and whether it is wholesome, unwholesome, resultant or functional.
  • Fifty-four belong to the sense sphere, fifteen to the fine-material sphere, twelve to the immaterial sphere, and eight are supramundane: path and fruition.
  • Mental displeasure occurs in only two kinds, both rooted in aversion, so in this analysis grief and sadness are forms of aversion.
  • Eighteen kinds have no root such as greed or its opposite; most are the given results of past action, like bare seeing and hearing.
  • Joy occurs in good and bad minds alike, and neutral feeling in the dullest mind and the arahant's, so only the roots tell such states apart.
  • The count replaces one continuous, owned mind with many brief, conditioned kinds of knowing, meant to be recognised one moment at a time.

If you were asked how many kinds of mind there are, you might answer: one, mine. The Abhidhamma answers eighty-nine. It does not mean eighty-nine minds sitting side by side. It means that every moment of knowing, every cittacitta · consciousness one moment of knowing an object; 89 kinds, or 121 counted in detail, is of one kind or another, and that the kinds can be counted.

Each citta knows one object and lasts one moment. What makes it the kind it is: whether greed or hatred or delusion is present as a root, or their opposites; whether the moment is pleasant, painful or neither; whether it makes kamma, is the result of kamma, or merely functions; and in what plane of existence it belongs. Fix those, and you have fixed the citta.

Four planes of mind

The first sorting is by plane. Fifty-four cittas belong to the sense sphere, the ordinary mind of seeing, hearing, wanting, giving, worrying. Fifteen belong to the fine-material sphere: the minds of the jhānas, the meditative absorptions (four in the suttas; the Dhammasaṅgaṇī gives both a fourfold and a fivefold scheme, and the Manual follows the fivefold), absorbed in a meditation object. Twelve belong to the immaterial sphere, the formless attainments, where the object is boundless space, the consciousness that knew it, nothingness, or the consciousness that knew that. Eight are supramundane: the moments of path and fruition that take Nibbāna as their object.noteCounted in finer detail, each supramundane citta can occur at any of five jhāna levels, which gives forty instead of eight, and a total of 121. The Manual gives both counts.

The plane of a citta and the plane of a world are linked. A human being lives in the sense sphere but can, in deep meditation, have fine-material cittas. A being reborn in a fine-material world is reborn by such a citta.

Twelve that are unwholesome

The sense-sphere cittas begin with twelve that are unwholesome, akusalaakusala · unwholesome unhealthy, blameworthy, bringing painful results; rooted in greed, hatred or delusion. Eight are rooted in greed, lobhalobha · greed grasping at an object; roots eight cittas; as craving, a root of the wheel, and differ by three switches: with joy or with neutral feeling, with wrong view or without it, spontaneous or prompted (needing a push, from oneself or another). Two are rooted in hatred, dosadosa · hatred aversion, from irritation to rage; root of two cittas, always with displeasure, and both carry displeasure. Two are rooted in delusion alone: one with doubt, one with restlessness.

That sorting has a quiet consequence, which the figure below shows by colour. Domanassadomanassa · displeasure painful mental feeling; only in the two hate-rooted cittas, mental displeasure, occurs in only those two hate-rooted cittas, nowhere else among the eighty-nine. Grief, sadness, dejection are all, in this analysis, forms of aversion.noteThe Dhammapada commentary tells the story of Kisāgotamī, who carried her dead child from house to house looking for medicine. She sought a mustard seed from a house where no one had died, found none, and understood. Her grief was aversion toward loss; the search turned it into a fact about the world.

Rootless, and beautiful

Eighteen cittas have no roots at all. Most of them are results: seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting and touching, each in a wholesome-result and an unwholesome-result version, and the cittas that receive and investigate what the senses bring. They are the passive, given part of experience. Three are functional: two adverting cittas, which turn the mind toward an object, and the smile-producing citta, a rootless moment of joy found only in arahants (I §10).

Then twenty-four “beautiful” (sobhanasobhana · beautiful the 59 cittas other than unwholesome and rootless ones, and their 25 factors) sense-sphere cittas: eight wholesome, eight resultant, eight functional. Twelve, eighteen and twenty-four make the fifty-four of the sense sphere. The wholesome eight are the ordinary good mind, generous, kind, clear, and differ by the same switches as the greed-rooted eight: with joy or neutral feeling, with knowledge or without, spontaneous or prompted. Their functional twins are the same states in an arahant, who still acts with kindness and understanding but makes no kamma by it.

What the count is for

There is a temptation to treat the eighty-nine as trivia. The list does something else. It replaces the single, continuous mind that we assume is doing our knowing with many kinds of knowing, each brief, each conditioned, none of them owned.

It also makes the mind legible. Painful bodily feeling occurs in just one citta, body-consciousness that is the result of unwholesome kamma; pleasant bodily feeling in one, its wholesome counterpart. Every other painful feeling is mental: displeasure, in the two hate-rooted cittas. Joy occurs in wholesome and unwholesome minds alike, so joy alone is no test of a good state. Neutral feeling, equanimity, occurs in the dullest mind and in the arahant’s, and only the roots tell them apart.

None of this is meant to be memorised as a table first. It is meant to be met, one citta at a time, in the moments when you notice which kind you are in.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Displeasure occurs in only two of the eighty-nine cittas, both rooted in aversion. Think of a recent sadness. Can you find the aversion in it?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Often it is aversion toward a loss: the mind pushing against what has happened. That is why the Abhidhamma counts grief as unwholesome, not as a sin but as a state that cannot coexist with letting go. It does not mean sadness is wrong to feel; it means that where there is grief, something is being resisted, and that is worth seeing.
