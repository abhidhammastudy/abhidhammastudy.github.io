The All, counted twice

Twelve bases and eighteen elements: the Buddha's "all" as six doors and what comes through them, and why the same ground was counted three ways.

About 6 minutes

In brief

  • The Buddha defined "the All" as six doors, eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind, together with what comes through each of them.
  • The twelve bases (āyatana) count both sides of each door: six organs, the mind among them, and their six kinds of object.
  • The eighteen elements (dhātu) add the consciousness that arises at each door, so every experience is an organ, an object and a knowing.
  • The commentaries match the aggregates, bases and elements to different temperaments; all three cover the same ground, and none leaves room for a self.
  • The bases are also a practice: guarding the six doors, so that, as the Buddha told Bāhiya, in the seen there is only the seen.

“What is the All?” the Buddha asks in a short sutta, and answers: the eye and forms, the ear and sounds, the nose and smells, the tongue and tastes, the body and tangibles, the mind and mental objects. Anyone who claims to describe some other “all”, he says, would be speaking of what is outside their range (SN 35.23).

It is a startling definition. The All is not a world out there, nor a world in here. It is six doors and what comes through them.

Twelve bases

The twelve āyatanaāyatana · base (the twelve) one of twelve bases: six internal (five sense organs and the mind base) and their six objects, bases, count the two sides of each door. Six are internal: the eye, ear, nose, tongue and body, which are sensitive matter, and the mind. Six are external: visible forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles, and mental objects.

The mind base is the whole of consciousness, all eighty-nine kinds. The mental-object base is everything else that the mind can know and the senses cannot: the fifty-two mental factors, the sixteen subtle kinds of matter that no sense can touch, and Nibbāna. Concepts are left out, though the mind knows them: a base must be something real in its own right, and a concept is only constructed (VII §36).

Eighteen elements

The eighteen dhātudhātu · element (the eighteen) one of eighteen elements: the twelve bases, with the mind base split into seven, elements, take the same doors and add what arises at each: the consciousness. “Dependent on the eye and forms, eye-consciousness arises; the meeting of the three is contact” (MN 18). So for each door there are three elements: the organ, the object, and the knowing.

In doing so the elements split the mind base into seven. Five are the sense consciousnesses. The sixth is the mind element, the cittas that turn toward a sense object and receive it. The seventh is the mind-consciousness element: every other citta, from investigating up to the moments of awakening.The division follows function. The mind element only adverts and receives; it does no more. Everything that examines, judges, wants, gives or understands is mind-consciousness.

Why three lists for one thing

The aggregates, the bases and the elements cover the same ground. The commentaries give a reason for having all three: the Buddha taught the aggregates to those confused mainly about mind, the bases to those confused mainly about matter, and the elements to those confused about both. Each list suits a temperament.

What the three have in common is that none leaves a remainder. Everything that can be experienced is an aggregate, a base or an element. Look for a self among them and it is not there; look for it outside them and there is no outside to look in.

The Manual has a fifth way of cutting the whole, after the aggregates, the aggregates of clinging, the bases and the elements: the four noble truths (VII §§34, 38–40). It sorts the same realities by the task each calls for, and it leaves some of them outside: the path and fruition cittas and their factors, all but the path’s eight (VII §40). It has its own reading, The four noble truths.

Practising at the doors

This is also why the bases are a practice and not only a list. Sense restraint, in the suttas, means guarding the six doors: seeing what is seen without grasping at its signs. The reading The mango and the moment, later on, shows where that guarding works. Seeing itself is given; greed or calm arises only in the moments that follow, the javanas, where the mind acts.

The Buddha’s instruction to the ascetic Bāhiya is the shortest form of this teaching: in the seen there will be only the seen, in the heard only the heard. When that is so, he said, there is no “you” in it, and that is the end of suffering (Ud 1.10). Bāhiya was liberated on the spot. The eighteen elements are the same instruction worked out in full.

The ideas in this reading

  • Two truths conventional, ultimateTwo ways of speaking: of persons and things, and of the ultimate dhammas they resolve into. The starting point of the Abhidhamma.
  • Rūpa matterMatter: 28 kinds, always arising in groups of eight or more.
  • Citta consciousnessConsciousness: one moment of knowing an object. The Abhidhamma counts 89 kinds.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

For the next sound you hear, try to find something in the experience that is neither the ear, nor the sound, nor the hearing. What do you find?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Usually a sense of "me, listening". Looked at closely, that turns out to be a later thought, itself a mental object known at the mind door. The eighteen elements leave no remainder for it: the ear, the sound and the hearing, then the mind, the thought and the knowing of the thought. That is the point of the count.
