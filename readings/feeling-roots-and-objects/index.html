Feeling, roots and objects

Three more ways to sort the eighty-nine cittas: by the feeling each one carries, by the roots that make it firm, and by what it is able to know.

About 10 minutes

In brief

  • Beyond its main list, the Manual sorts the eighty-nine kinds of consciousness by feeling, by roots and by what each can know, each a different lens.
  • Every moment of consciousness has a feeling; bodily pleasure and bodily pain occur in just one kind each, and mental displeasure in only two.
  • The commentary explains that bare seeing, hearing, smelling and tasting are always neutral; the pleasure in a lovely sight comes a moment later, added by the mind.
  • Six roots, greed, hatred, delusion and their opposites, make a mind firm, and the roots present at rebirth limit what that life can reach.
  • A mind never knows itself; eye-door cittas know only present colour, while mind-door cittas can take objects past, present, future or timeless.
  • Each kind of consciousness has a set range of objects: only the paths and fruitions take Nibbāna and nothing else, a few sense-sphere cittas with knowledge can also turn to it, and none can know an attainment not yet reached.

Chapter III of the Manual is called the compendium of the miscellaneous. Having listed the cittas and their factors, it sorts the cittas six more ways: by feeling, by roots, by function, by door, by object and by base. What each citta does takes up functions, doors and bases. This reading takes the other three. Each is a lens: the same eighty-nine cittas, seen from a different side.

Three feelings, or five

Every citta has a feeling, because vedanāvedanā · feeling feeling: the pleasant, painful or neutral tone of a moment; not emotion is one of the seven universal factors, the mental factors present in every citta (see An ensemble of fifty-two): it is how the object is tasted. Counted simply, feeling is threefold: pleasant, painful, and neither (III §2). Counted by the faculty that governs it, it is fivefold, because pleasant and painful each divide into a bodily and a mental kind: bodily pleasure, sukhasukha · bodily pleasure pleasant bodily feeling: only in wholesome-resultant body-consciousness, and mental joy, somanassasomanassa · joy pleasant mental feeling; it accompanies 62 of the 121 cittas; bodily pain, dukkhadukkha · bodily pain painful bodily feeling: only in unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness, and mental displeasure, domanassadomanassa · displeasure painful mental feeling; only in the two hate-rooted cittas; and equanimity, upekkhāupekkhā · equanimity (feeling) neutral feeling, neither pleasant nor painful; in 55 of the 121 cittas.

The counts are lopsided (III §3–4). Bodily pleasure and bodily pain occur in one citta each, the two kinds of body-consciousness. Displeasure occurs in only two, the cittas rooted in hatred. Joy occurs in sixty-two, and equanimity in the remaining fifty-five.noteThe Manual’s sixty-two and fifty-five count the supramundane cittas by jhāna, as in the reckoning of 121: eight paths and fruitions in each of five jhānas. Among the eighty-nine, the lens above counts thirty-eight with joy and fifty-five with equanimity, the eight paths and fruitions being in both.

Why should only touch be pleasant or painful? The Guide gives the commentary’s image. In seeing, hearing, smelling and tasting, a derived kind of matter strikes another derived kind of matter, like balls of cotton striking balls of cotton on an anvil: the impact is soft, and the feeling neutral. In touch, the object is the great elements themselves, three of them, earth, fire and air, and they strike the great elements of the body, like a hammer through cotton onto the anvil. So the bare moment of seeing a lovely sight is neutral. The pleasure comes after, in the javanas, as mental joy: the liking is added.

The suttas sometimes speak of feeling as only two, pleasant and painful, and the Buddha once said that whatever is felt is included in suffering (SN 36.11). The Guide reads that in the wide sense: not painful feeling, but the unsatisfactoriness of everything that passes.

Six roots

A root, hetuhetu · root one of six factors that steady a citta: greed, hatred, delusion, or their opposites, is a mental factor that makes the citta it joins firm and stable. Cittas with roots are compared to trees; those without are like moss (III §5). There are six: greed, hatred and delusion, always unwholesome; and non-greed, non-hatred and non-delusion, which are wholesome in wholesome cittas and neither good nor bad in resultant and functional ones.

Eighteen cittas have no root at all: the sense consciousnesses, receiving, investigating, adverting and the arahant’s smile (III §6). Two have one root, the cittas of sheer delusion. Twenty-two have two: the other ten unwholesome cittas, with greed or hatred together with delusion, and the twelve sense-sphere beautiful cittas without knowledge, with non-greed and non-hatred. Forty-seven have all three beautiful roots: the twelve sense-sphere beautiful cittas with knowledge, and every jhāna, path and fruition.

The count matters later. The roots of the rebirth-linking citta decide what a life can reach: a being reborn with a rootless or two-rooted citta cannot attain jhāna or the path in that life, however hard they practise. That is taken up in From kamma to its results.

Six kinds of object

Every citta knows something. The Manual names six kinds of object (III §16): visible form, sound, smell, taste, the tangible, and the mental object. The first five are matter. The tangible is three of the great elements felt directly, earth as hard or soft, fire as hot or cold, air as pressure; the fourth, water, cannot be touched and is known only by the mind. The mental object is itself sixfold: the five kinds of sensitive matter, the sixteen kinds of subtle matter, cittas, mental factors, Nibbāna, and concepts, paññattipaññatti · concept a concept: a name, or what a name refers to, such as “chariot”.

A citta can know an earlier citta, in its own stream or another’s, but never itself in the same moment: the knower does not know itself.

Doors also limit time (III §17). The cittas of an eye-door process know only a visible form, and only a present one. The cittas of a mind-door process can know any of the six, past, present or future, or timeless: Nibbāna and concepts are outside time, Nibbāna because it does not arise or pass, concepts because they have no being of their own. And the door-freed cittas of a life, in their three jobs of rebirth-linking, life-continuum and death, all keep one object: the one the last process of the previous life held, as a kamma, a sign of kamma, or a sign of destiny.

Who can know what

Last, the Manual sorts the cittas by the range of objects each can take, in seven groups (III §18–19, Table 3.5):

  • Sense-sphere objects only: twenty-five. The sense-sphere resultants, five-door adverting, and the smile.
  • Anything but the supramundane: twenty. The unwholesome cittas, and the wholesome and functional beautiful ones without knowledge. The paths, their fruitions and Nibbāna are too pure and deep for them.
  • Anything but the arahant’s path and fruition: five, Nibbāna included. The wholesome cittas with knowledge, and the wholesome direct knowledge. An ordinary person, or a trainee (a noble disciple short of arahantship), cannot know an attainment they have not reached.
  • Anything at all: six, Nibbāna included. The functional cittas with knowledge, the functional direct knowledge, both arahants’ only, and mind-door adverting, the citta that also does the job of determining, which turns to every kind of object.
  • Sublime cittas: six. The second and fourth immaterial jhānas, which take the first and third as their objects.
  • Concepts: twenty-one. The fine-material jhānas and the first and third immaterial. Their object is a counterpart sign, beings for the illimitables, infinite space, or nothingness (Table 3.6).
  • Nibbāna only: eight. The paths and fruitions. The sense-sphere cittas with knowledge above turn to Nibbāna only at change-of-lineage and in reviewing (Guide to III §18; IX §34).

That makes ninety-one, because the two cittas of direct knowledge are fifth-jhāna cittas as well, and so they are counted twice.

Taken together, the three lenses show that no citta is simply “a mind”. Each has a tone, a footing or none, and a range, and each of these sets limits on what that moment can do.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The Abhidhamma says seeing itself is always neutral in feeling, and that pleasure in a beautiful sight comes a moment later, in the javanas. Next time you enjoy something you see, can you find the gap between the seeing and the liking?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Usually not at first: the two run together. But slowing down, it becomes plausible. The colour registers before the pleasure does, and sometimes the pleasure does not come at all, or comes as something else, such as wanting or comparing. That is the point of the teaching: the liking is not in the object or in the eye; it is added by the mind, as javana, and it is there that the training works.
