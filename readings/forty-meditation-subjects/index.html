Forty meditation subjects

The *Manual*'s catalogue of calm, after the *Visuddhimagga*: subjects matched to temperaments, the signs that arise on the way, and how far each subject can take the mind.

About 8 minutesManual IX §§2–21 (calm: subjects, temperaments, stages of development, signs, jhānas, direct knowledges)

In brief

  • Calm meditation (samatha) unifies the mind, and the Manual, following the Visuddhimagga, lists forty subjects for developing it.
  • They include ten kasiṇa devices such as a disc of earth, ten stages of a decaying corpse, ten recollections, four illimitables and four immaterial states.
  • Subjects are matched to six temperaments by contrast, foulness for the greedy and loving-kindness for the hating, though the matching is a guide, not a rule.
  • As practice deepens, an inner image of the subject grows into a bright, stable sign, and the mind comes near to, then enters, absorption (jhāna).
  • Not every subject reaches absorption: ten stop just short of it, while the devices and mindfulness of breathing can reach every jhāna.
  • Absorption suppresses the hindrances but uproots nothing; its value is a unified, workable mind that insight can then use, though calm first is one route among several.

The Manual’s last chapter turns from analysis to practice. It describes two kinds of meditation: calm, samathasamatha · calm meditation that develops concentration, up to the jhānas, which unifies the mind, and insight, vipassanāvipassanā · insight seeing mind and matter as impermanent, suffering and non-self, which sees its nature. Calm comes first in the order of the chapter, and the Manual, following the Visuddhimagga, gives it a catalogue: forty subjects of meditation, with notes on who should use which, and how far each can go.

The forty

Ten are kasiṇakasiṇa a device for concentration, such as a disc of earth or a colours, devices: earth, water, fire, air, blue, yellow, red, white, light and limited space. Earth is often a prepared disc of clay; air is the wind seen moving in leaves. Each is attended to until its image is held with the eyes closed. Ten are kinds of foulness: ten stages of a decaying corpse. Ten are recollections: of the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Saṅgha, of virtue, generosity and the qualities of the gods, of peace, of death, of the body’s parts, and of breathing.

Then four illimitables, appamaññāappamaññā · illimitables (factors) compassion and appreciative joy as mental factors, taking suffering or happy beings as object: loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy and equanimity. One perception, of the repulsiveness of food. One analysis, of the four elements in the body. And four immaterial states, the formless attainments, reached by going beyond a kasiṇa.

Six temperaments

The Manual matches subjects to people (IX §13). It describes six temperaments: greedy, hating, deluded, faithful, intelligent and speculative. A greedy temperament is given the kinds of foulness and the body’s parts, which cool desire. A hating temperament is given the four illimitables and the four colour kasiṇas. A deluded or speculative one is given mindfulness of breathing. A faithful one, the first six recollections. An intelligent one, the recollection of death and of peace, the perception of food, the analysis of elements.The matching is a guide. The Visuddhimagga presents it as what most directly counters each temperament, and adds that every wholesome development suppresses greed and the rest. Teachers in practice often give loving-kindness and mindfulness of breathing to almost everyone.

Three signs, three stages

As a subject is developed, a sign, nimittanimitta · meditation sign the preliminary, learning or counterpart image of the meditation object, arises. First the preliminary sign, the object as it is attended to. Then the learning sign, the object held in mind with the eyes closed, still with its flaws. Then the counterpart sign: a purified, stable image, bright and without blemish, like a mirror just taken out of its case.

The stages of development run in parallel. Preliminary development is the effort to attend. Access concentration, upacāraupacāra · access concentration just short of jhāna; also a javana in the run-up to absorption, arises, in the subjects that have one, with the counterpart sign: the hindrances are suppressed and the mind is near absorption. Absorption, appanāappanā · absorption the jhāna or path javana itself, after access; the third stage of concentration, is the jhānajhāna · absorption deep concentration fixed on one object: five fine-material levels, then four immaterial itself: the mind fully merged with its object.

How far each can go

This is the most practical part of the chart. Not every subject reaches absorption. Ten subjects reach only access: the eight recollections other than the body’s parts and breathing, the perception of repulsiveness in food, and the analysis of the four elements. They call for so much reflection that the mind cannot settle into jhāna. The kinds of foulness and the body’s parts reach the first jhāna only, since they need initial application to keep them vivid. Loving-kindness, compassion and appreciative joy reach the jhānas up to the one that still has happiness; equanimity reaches only the last, which has equanimity in place of happiness. The kasiṇas and mindfulness of breathing reach all the jhānas, and the kasiṇas open the way to the formless attainments.

What calm is for

A jhāna is a wholesome citta of the fine-material sphere (or, for the immaterial attainments, of the immaterial sphere), as the map of the eighty-nine showed. It is powerful kamma: weighty, as the reading on kamma said, and able to lead to rebirth in the Brahmā worlds. But it does not uproot anything. The hindrances are held down, so that for a while even before and after it they do not overwhelm the mind, but they can return, because nothing has uprooted them (Vism XXII §848; §837).

Its value on the path is the mind it leaves behind: unified, bright and workable, able to look at itself without flinching. For the meditator who goes by the vehicle of calm, that is the basis for insight: calm makes the instrument, and insight uses it. It is not the only route. The suttas name four ways to arahantship, among them insight before calm and the two yoked together (AN 4.170), and the Guide describes a vehicle of pure insight, whose concentration is momentary, not jhāna (Guide to IX §29; see Ten knowledges, one by one).

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The tradition matches subjects to temperaments: the reflection on foulness for a greedy one, loving-kindness for a hating one. Which subject would your own temperament call for, and which would you rather avoid?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Often the one you would rather avoid. The matching works by contrast: the subject is chosen to counter what dominates. A teacher in the tradition would also say that mindfulness of breathing and loving-kindness suit almost everyone, and that the matching is a guide, not a sentence.
