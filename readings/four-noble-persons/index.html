Readings › The way out

Four noble persons

Stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, arahant: what each of the four paths cuts away for good, the kinds of person on each step, and the two attainments that only the noble know.

About 10 minutesManual IX §§38–44 with the Guides and Table 9.3 (the four paths, what each abandons, attainments)

In brief

  • The moment of awakening, a path taking Nibbāna as its object, happens four times, and each time something is cut away that never grows back.
  • The stream-enterer has ended self-view, doubt, and clinging to rules and rituals, and will be reborn at most seven times, never in a woeful world.
  • The once-returner has thinned greed, hatred and delusion without uprooting them, and comes back to this world only once more.
  • The non-returner has ended sensual lust and ill will and, if not freed in this life, is reborn in a fine-material world, never returning here.
  • The arahant has ended conceit, restlessness, ignorance and every remaining defilement, so nothing unwholesome can arise again and their actions make no kamma.
  • Two attainments belong only to the noble: fruition, which experiences Nibbāna here and now, and cessation, in which the stream of consciousness stops for a time.

The tree of purification ends at a single process: change-of-lineage, then a path citta taking Nibbāna as its object, then two or three moments of fruition. That process happens four times, and each time something is cut that never grows back. The Manual closes its account of insight by naming the four kinds of person this makes, and what each has left behind (IX §38–41).

Entering the stream

The first path is stream-entry, sotāpattisotāpatti · stream-entry the first path, entering the stream of the noble path; it ends identity view, doubt and clinging to rules and rituals. The stream is the noble eightfold path itself, which once entered leads only one way. The stream-enterer has cut the three coarsest fetters: identity view, the belief that there is a self in or behind the aggregates; doubt about the Buddha, the Dhamma, the Saṅgha and the training; and clinging to rules and rituals as if they could free by themselves. With them goes any defilement strong enough to lead to a woeful rebirth. The Guide describes the result in human terms: unshakable confidence in the three jewels, and the five precepts scrupulously kept (Guide to IX §38).

Of the fourteen unwholesome mental factors, stream-entry ends four: wrong view and doubt, and, the commentaries add, envy and avarice.

What each path cuts

The Guide to these sections and Table 9.3 track the paths against three lists at once: the unwholesome factors of chapter II, the ten fetters of the suttas, and the four taints. The pattern is the same in all three. Stream-entry clears what is wrong in understanding; non-returning clears what binds to the sense world; arahantship clears everything that is left.

The second path ends no factor outright. It thins greed, hatred and delusion instead, as the once-returner’s section below explains.

The stream-enterer

A stream-enterer will be reborn at most seven more times, and only in the human and heavenly worlds (IX §38). The Guide names three kinds by how many births remain: the “seven-times-at-most”, sattakkhattuparamasattakkhattuparama · seven times at most a stream-enterer with at most seven more births, in the human and heavenly worlds; the one who goes “from family to family” two or three times; and the “one-seed”, reborn just once more before the goal.

The once-returner

The path of once-returning does not uproot greed, hatred and delusion, but it thins them: they still arise, but rarely and without their old grip (IX §39). In terms of the fetters, the gross forms of sensual lust and ill will fall away (Paṭisambhidāmagga; Vism XXII §830), which is why the figure hatches those two rows at the second path. The once-returner comes back to “this world” only once more. Which world that is, the commentaries do not agree. On one reading it is the human world, returned to from a heaven; on the other it is the sense-sphere world, returned to from a Brahmā world. Ledi Sayadaw, cited in the Guide, thinks the canon favours the second.

The commentary to the Puggalapaññatti counts five kinds of once-returner, by where the fruition is reached, where the next birth falls, and where final Nibbāna comes. One of them is born twice more, once in a heaven and once as a human, yet keeps the name: the human world is returned to only once (Guide to IX §39).

The non-returner

The third path ends sensual lust and ill will completely (IX §40). With them go the taint of sensual desire, the factors hatred and worry, and every form of greed that takes a sense object. The five fetters cut so far are called the lower fetters, because they bind to the lower, sensuous world. A non-returner who does not reach arahantship in this life is reborn in a fine-material world and reaches Nibbāna there, never returning to the sense sphere.

The Pure Abodes, the five highest fine-material planes of Thirty-one planes, are only for non-returners. But the Guide is careful: not every non-returner is reborn there. The texts name five kinds, by when and how the final path comes: before the midpoint of that heavenly life, after it, without exertion, with exertion, or after passing upward from realm to realm until the highest, Akaniṭṭha.

The arahant

The fourth path ends the five higher fetters: lust for fine-material and for immaterial existence, conceit, restlessness, and ignorance (IX §41). It destroys the last two taints, attachment to existence and ignorance, which is why the arahant is called khīṇāsavakhīṇāsava · one whose taints are destroyed the arahant, in whom the four taints have ended, one whose taints are destroyed. And it ends the last eight unwholesome factors, the eight still standing: delusion, shamelessness, fearlessness of wrongdoing, restlessness, the rest of greed, conceit, sloth and torpor. Nothing unwholesome can arise again. The arahant’s javanas are functional cittas, which act without making kamma; they have their own place in A map of the eighty-nine.

Fruition and cessation

Two attainments belong only to the noble (IX §42–44).

Fruition, phalasamāpattiphalasamāpatti · attainment of fruition a noble one's absorption in their own fruition, with Nibbāna as object, is open to all four, each to their own fruition. A noble person resolves to attain it and develops insight again from the knowledge of rise and fall; then fruition cittas run, taking Nibbāna as object. It is, the Guide says, for experiencing the bliss of Nibbāna here and now.

Cessation, nirodha-samāpattinirodha-samāpatti · attainment of cessation the temporary stopping of citta and mental factors, open only to non-returners and arahants with all eight jhānas, is open only to non-returners and arahants who have mastered all eight jhānas, and only in the sense or fine-material worlds, since the four fine-material jhānas it requires cannot be entered in the immaterial ones. The meditator enters each jhāna in turn and, emerging, sees its factors as impermanent, suffering and non-self, up to the base of nothingness. Then come four preliminary resolutions: that requisites belonging to others, kept with the meditator, come to no harm; that the meditator emerge if the Saṅgha needs them; that they emerge if the Buddha calls; and that they first check death is not due within seven days. Then the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception runs for two javanas, and the stream of consciousness stops.

On emerging, a single fruition citta arises, of non-returning or of arahantship, and the mind sinks into the life-continuum; then reviewing knowledge looks back on what happened. It is the one place in the Abhidhamma where, for a time, there is matter without mind in a being who has both.Cessation is sometimes compared with the non-percipient beings, who also have matter without mind. The difference is that cessation is entered by choice, for a set time, by one who has insight; the non-percipient birth is a result, and ends in a return to ordinary existence.

The ideas in this reading

  • Path and fruition four pathsThe four moments of awakening that cut defilements for good, each followed by its fruition.
  • Insight vipassanāThe knowledges that see mind and matter rise and fall.
  • Nibbāna unconditionedThe one unconditioned reality, known in the moments of awakening called path and fruition.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Stream-entry is said to end identity view, doubt and clinging to rules and rituals, yet it leaves greed, hatred and conceit in place, except in the grade that could lead to a woeful rebirth. Why might the tradition put the end of a view before the end of a passion?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Because the passions feed on the view. As long as there seems to be a self that owns this body and this feeling, greed has someone to serve and conceit has someone to measure. Once that view is seen through, greed and hatred still arise, but they no longer have a permanent home; they are known as passing states, and they can be worn down. The later paths do the wearing down. The order is the order of the work: first stop believing in the one who suffers, then stop the suffering.
