From kamma to its results

How each kind of kamma is matched to the rebirth it can give, why the quality of a good deed matters as much as the deed, and what ripens within a life.

About 6 minutes

In brief

  • A volition ceases as soon as it arises, yet it can ripen later, as the first moment of a new life or as experiences within a life.
  • Unwholesome kamma leads to rebirth in the woeful worlds; wholesome kamma, weak, middling or excellent, gives correspondingly better human or divine births.
  • Only the best wholesome kamma gives a rebirth able to reach meditative absorption and the path in that very life.
  • How a deed is done matters as much as the deed: a gift given gladly and with understanding is stronger kamma than the same gift given grudgingly.
  • Within a life, kamma ripens as the pleasant or unpleasant sights, sounds and sensations that come unchosen; teachers disagreed on how many results each grade gives.
  • Since the kind of rebirth decides what the next life can reach, the tradition draws a lesson of urgency: each moment of action prepares what follows.

A volition ceases as soon as it arises, and yet it can ripen much later. The Manual traces two ways it does so. It can ripen as the first citta of a new life, the rebirth-linking consciousness. And it can ripen within a life, as the resultant cittas of seeing, hearing and the rest: the pleasant or unpleasant sights and sounds that come to a being without being chosen.

Matching kamma to rebirth

The Manual matches each kind of kamma to the rebirths it can give. Twenty kinds of rebirth-linking cover all thirty-one planes.Nineteen kinds of citta can link a new life; the twentieth is the purely material rebirth of the beings without perception, who have no citta at all at rebirth.

Wholesome kamma comes in grades, weak, middling and excellent, by its roots and by how it was done (next section). Unwholesome kamma gives rebirth in the four woeful planes, by the unwholesome-resultant investigating citta. Weak wholesome kamma, two-rooted and done half-heartedly, gives a rootless rebirth as a human or as one of the lesser spirits: a life with serious disadvantages from the start. Middling wholesome kamma gives a human or divine rebirth with two roots, without understanding. Excellent wholesome kamma, three-rooted and done well, gives a three-rooted rebirth, with the capacity for jhāna and for the path in that very life.

How the deed is done

What makes kamma weak, middling or excellent is not only what is done but how. The Manual weighs the roots of the citta, whether understanding was present, and the manner: whether the act was done gladly, with a clear mind before and after, or reluctantly and with regret. A gift given grudgingly and a gift given with joy and understanding are different kamma, though the gift is the same.

Jhāna and the higher planes

The jhānas ripen in their own planes. In each of the first three jhāna levels, a jhāna developed weakly, moderately or fully leads to the lowest, middle or highest plane of that level. The highest jhāna leads to the plane of great fruit, or, with a turning away from perception, to the plane of beings without perception; the Pure Abodes receive only non-returners. The immaterial attainments likewise lead to the immaterial planes. This is weighty kamma: if a jhāna is maintained to the end of life, it takes precedence over everything else.

Results within a life

Within a life, results arrive constantly. Unwholesome kamma ripens as the seven unwholesome-resultant cittas: unpleasant sights, sounds, smells, tastes, the pain of body-consciousness, and the receiving and investigating of such objects. One unwholesome citta, the restlessness citta, gives results in life but can never produce a rebirth.

Wholesome kamma of the sense sphere ripens too, and here the Manual records a disagreement between teachers (V §§29–30). On the usual view, a wholesome citta of the best kind, with three roots and done well, can give all sixteen sense-sphere wholesome results in the course of life: the eight rootless ones and the eight great resultants. A weaker kind gives twelve, and the weakest only the eight rootless. Some teachers held a stricter rule: an unprompted deed gives only unprompted results, and a prompted deed only prompted ones. On their count the three grades give twelve, ten and eight.The Guide names the two schools: the usual view goes back to the elder Tipiṭaka Cūḷanāga, the stricter one to the school of Mahā Dhammarakkhita at the Moravāpi monastery in ancient Sri Lanka. Table 5.5 of the Manual sets the two side by side. The Atthasālinī gives this view to Mahādatta of Moravāpi, and Ledi Sayadaw corrects the name accordingly (As on Dhs §498; Ledi, Paramatthadīpanī §153).

The practical lesson the tradition draws is about urgency. The kind of rebirth decides what is possible in the life that follows: only a three-rooted being can attain the path in that life. Each life is the field prepared by the last, and each moment of javana is preparing the next.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

A generous act done grudgingly gives weaker results than the same act done gladly and with understanding. Think of something kind you did recently: what were its roots, and how was it done?

One way to see it, after you have tried

The Manual weighs two things: the roots of the citta (whether understanding was present) and the manner of doing (whether it was done gladly, with clarity before and after, or reluctantly). The same outward act can come from very different cittas. That is no reason to stop doing a kind thing when it comes grudgingly; it is a reason to attend to how it is done.
