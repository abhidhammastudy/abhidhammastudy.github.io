How insight is practised

What the texts tell a meditator to do: the ground to stand on, what to watch and how, how to keep the balance, what begins to show, what can go wrong, and what the texts leave to teachers.

About 15 minutesManual IX §§22–37 with the Guides and Table 9.2 (the marks, purification of view and of overcoming doubt, the ten insight knowledges, the imperfections, insight leading to emergence, the three doors)

In brief

  • Insight (vipassanā) is watching body and mind as they actually happen, until it is seen directly that they arise and pass, cannot satisfy, and have no owner inside.
  • It rests on keeping the precepts and on a mind steady enough to watch: some build that steadiness through deep calm first, others inside the watching itself.
  • The texts say to begin with what is plain to you, such as the postures, the body's hardness and warmth, or whether a feeling is pleasant or painful, and the mind will show itself in time.
  • What one does is simple to say: know walking as walking and a mind with greed as a mind with greed, and look for who is doing it; the commentary says one finds only conditions.
  • Lights, joy and calm that come along the way are neither a goal nor a measure; the Visuddhimagga gives one test for them: this has arisen, it will pass, it is not mine.
  • The texts give a method but no timetable; lay people in the canon practised it in ordinary lives, and today's retreats and noting techniques are later ways of applying it.

The tree of purification drew the path as a whole, and Ten knowledges, one by one follows what is seen along it. This reading asks the question in between: what does a meditator actually do? It keeps to the texts and marks which layer says what: the suttas, the Buddha’s discourses; the commentaries, put into their Pāli form in the fifth century CE, chiefly by Buddhaghosa from older commentaries, with his own Visuddhimagga, the Path of Purification; their sub-commentaries, a later layer; the Manual, Anuruddha’s Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha, in Bhikkhu Bodhi’s translation (A Comprehensive Manual of Abhidhamma), with Bodhi’s Guide; and a few modern teachers, named where they come in. Most sections carry a small exercise, “Try this”, for an ordinary day.

What insight practice is

Insight, vipassanāvipassanā · insight seeing mind and matter as impermanent, suffering and non-self, is seeing for oneself what body and mind are and how they behave. In the Manual’s terms it looks at mind and matter and sees three marks in them, tilakkhaṇatilakkhaṇa · the three marks impermanence, suffering and non-self: the marks that insight sees: they are impermanent; they are suffering, unable to satisfy for long; and they are non-self, with no owner or core inside (IX §§23–24). Insight is not a belief about the marks; it is the marks seen in what is happening now.

A plain point first. The texts describe a method, but no timetable for lay people: no sitting lengths, no hours of walking, no retreat schedule. The daily routine the suttas do give is for monks: walking and sitting by day and through the night, sleeping in the middle watch (MN 39; MN 107). Today’s meditation centres fill that gap in their own ways; the reading How insight is taught today sets the main methods side by side.

The ground: conduct and a steady mind

The Manual sets insight inside seven purifications, and the first is purification of virtue (IX §22, §28); the Guide adds that each supports the next (Guide to IX §22). For a lay person the Visuddhimagga names a householder’s virtue: the five precepts kept always, ten if one is willing, eight on the observance days (new moon, full moon and half-moons; Vism I §13). The Manual counts restraint of the senses as one of the four kinds of virtue (IX §28), and the Guide explains it: being mindful when meeting sights and sounds, so that the mind is not swept toward what is pleasant or against what is not (Guide to IX §28). On this site’s reading, that is the part of virtue a lay meditator can practise all day. The canon pictures it as six animals on ropes: a snake, a crocodile (or dolphin; the Pāli susumāra is read both ways), a bird, a dog, a jackal and a monkey. Knotted only to one another, they drag each other toward their own haunts; tied to a strong post, they pull until they tire and then lie down beside it. The post, the Buddha says, is mindfulness of the body (SN 35.247, sn35.247:3.1–6.1).

The second, purification of mind, is concentration: access or absorption (IX §29). The texts know two kinds of meditator. One goes by the vehicle of calm, samathasamatha · calm meditation that develops concentration, up to the jhānas: he first develops concentration to the edge of absorption (access) or into a jhānajhāna · absorption deep concentration fixed on one object: five fine-material levels, then four immaterial (Guide to IX §29; Vism XVIII §662), then emerges and examines the states of that jhāna (Vism XVIII §663; see Forty meditation subjects). The other goes by pure insight, and the Visuddhimagga itself names him: he starts straight from the four elements of the body (Vism XVIII §664), and his path can arise with no jhāna beneath it, which makes him a “dry-insight worker” (Vism XXI §798; XXIII §868). His steadiness is called momentary concentration, khaṇika-samādhikhaṇika-samādhi · momentary concentration concentration that holds each passing event in turn; the term is in the Visuddhimagga; the Guide reckons it as purification of mind for the pure-insight meditator, renewed moment by moment inside the watching. The term is in the Visuddhimagga (IV §72; VIII §235); the sub-commentary makes it this meditator’s own concentration (on Vism I §§3–4), and the Guide counts it as his purification of mind (Guide to IX §29). The canon has neither the term nor the dry-insight worker.

The suttas are wider still. Ānanda lists four routes to arahantship: calm then insight, insight then calm, the two yoked together, and a mind gripped by restlessness about the Dhamma that later settles (AN 4.170; the commentary takes that restlessness as the imperfections of insight described below, Mp, the Manorathapūraṇī, the commentary on the Numbered Discourses, on AN 4.170). On this site’s reading, some stillness is present on every route; what differs is how much, and whether it comes first.

The five hindrances, sensual desire, ill will, dullness, restlessness and doubt, weaken wisdom (AN 5.51). The Buddha’s picture: no one can see his face in a bowl of water that is dyed, boiling, overgrown with moss, rippled by the wind, or muddy and set in the dark; so with each hindrance in turn (SN 46.55, sn46.55:4.1–12.3). The Buddha’s formula has the meditator watch having put away longing and discontent for the world (mn10:3.2), which the commentary takes as the two chief hindrances (Ps, the Papañcasūdanī, Buddhaghosa’s commentary on the Middle Discourses, on MN 10 §106). Yet the same sutta makes the hindrances something to watch (mn10:36.4–36.8). On this site’s reading: do not wait for a perfect mind, but when a hindrance is running the show, notice the hindrance first.

What is watched, and where to start

What insight looks at is mundane mind and matter, the conditioned things of any world (the Manual’s “formations of the three planes”, IX §32; Vism XVIII §671). Nibbāna, the unconditioned peace that is the goal, is not in it, nor are the minds of the paths; the Visuddhimagga leaves them out as not to be comprehended (Vism XX §694). Nor are concepts such as “a person”, “my knee” or “the breath as a stream of air”: the texts imply this without saying it in so many words, since concepts are not formations. In practice it means attending to hardness, warmth, movement, a pleasant feeling, a mind with greed, rather than to the names and shapes laid over them.

Where to begin? The Visuddhimagga says: with whatever is evident to you and easy to take up (Vism XX §694). For the pure-insight meditator its starting point is the body, as the four elements: earth (hardness), water (cohesion), fire (heat) and air (support and motion). As matter becomes clear, mind becomes evident through the doors, as the seeing, hearing and thinking that take it as object (Vism XVIII §664). If mind does not show, he is not to give up but to keep cleaning his view of matter, like wiping a mirror until the face appears (§669).

Try this The four elements, briefly

Sitting quietly, turn your attention to the whole body. Notice hardness or softness (earth), warmth or coolness (both are fire), pressure or movement (air), and name them quietly as elements, not as “me”. Cohesion, the water element, is known by the mind, not felt by touch: the Manual counts only the other three as touchable (VI §3 with the Guide). The Visuddhimagga gives this method to one of keen wisdom, in solitude, to be repeated over time (Vism XI §308); a few minutes is a taste of it. (MN 10, mn10:12.1–12.2.)

The commentary adds that the mind side is easiest to see through feeling. Contact and consciousness are hard to make out, as if in darkness, but pleasure and pain are unmistakable; neutral feeling is known by inference, like a hunter reading a deer’s tracks on either side of a bare rock (Ps on MN 10 §113). It also matches the four foundations of mindfulness to people: body or feeling for those led by craving, mind or mental objects for those led by views (Ps §106). The Guide calls the four one practice with four objects (Guide to VII §24).

Knowing what is present

The Buddha’s instruction is short. Walking, one knows “I am walking”; standing, sitting or lying down, one knows that (mn10:6.1–6.2). The commentary asks what knowing is meant. Dogs and jackals also know they are walking, and that does not remove the idea of a being. The knowing meant asks: who walks? whose walking is it? why? Its answer: no being walks. The thought “I’ll go” arises and produces the air element, which moves the body forward. The body is like a cart, the mind-born motion its oxen, the mind its driver; “the cart goes” is only a way of speaking (Ps on MN 10 §108), and so is “I walk”.

Try this Know the posture

A few times today, as you stand, sit or lie down, simply know it. Then ask the commentary’s question: who is standing? Look for the intention just before the movement, then the movement itself. (MN 10, mn10:6.1–6.2; Ps on MN 10 §108.)

The same goes for feeling, of which the Buddha names three kinds: pleasant, painful and neither (mn10:32.2–32.4). An infant at the breast knows “I feel pleasure”; the meditator knows that it is feeling that feels, taking its object, and that “I feel” is a way of speaking (Ps §113).

Try this Feeling-tone

For one meal, or ten minutes, notice whether each moment feels pleasant, painful or neither. Notice too that when one is present, the other two are not, a point the Buddha makes in MN 74 (mn74:10.3–10.8), which the commentary quotes here (Ps on MN 10 §113). The meal and the ten minutes are this site’s suggestion.

Mind is known the same way: a mind with greed as a mind with greed (mn10:34.2). A hindrance is known as present or absent, how it arose, how it goes, and how it will not come back (mn10:36.4–36.8).

Try this A hindrance check

At the start of a sitting, ask which of the five is here, and know it simply as present. If it is dullness, try the commentary’s remedies: notice if you have eaten too much, change posture, attend to light, sit in the open air (Ps on MN 10 §115). Opening your eyes to daylight or sitting by an open window is this site’s adaptation of the last two.

To Bāhiya of the Bark Cloth, an ascetic, the Buddha gave the briefest form: in the seen let there be only the seen, in the heard only the heard, and so for what is sensed and cognised (Ud 1.10, ud1.10:8.2); he gave the same to the monk Māluṅkyaputta (SN 35.95). Bāhiya, whom the Buddha names foremost in swift understanding (AN 1.216), was freed on the spot (ud1.10:9.1).

Try this Only the seen

On a short walk, let seeing be seeing and hearing be hearing. Notice when the mind adds a story to what it sees, and come back. “Adding a story” is this site’s gloss, not the sutta’s. (Ud 1.10, ud1.10:8.2.)

Every section of the sutta ends with one refrain: see things arise and pass; know “there is a body” just enough for knowing; cling to nothing (mn10:5.1–5.3). The commentary reads it: only a body, not a being, not a self (Ps §107).

Walking and daily acts

The commentaries divide a step into six parts: lifting, moving forward, shifting sideways (to avoid a thorn, say), lowering, placing and pressing (Vism XX §710; Ps on MN 10 §109). In the first three, the commentary says, earth and water are weak and fire and air strong; in the last three, the reverse. What arose in one part does not reach the next; it breaks up right there, joint by joint, crackling like sesame seeds in a hot pan; seen so, the Visuddhimagga adds, comprehension of matter becomes subtle. The texts divide the step for contemplation; they do not say to walk slowly, which is a modern way of applying them. The Buddha praises walking meditation in general: among its five benefits, the concentration it brings is lasting (AN 5.29).

Try this The parts of a step

On a level floor, walk ten paces at whatever pace lets you follow them. Feel the lifting, moving forward, lowering, placing and pressing, and notice each part ending before the next begins. Stop if you feel dizzy. When you stop, stand a moment and reflect, as an elder in the commentary advised after a long walk: what happened in the walking has ended there (Ps on MN 10 §109). The ten paces are this site’s suggestion.

The sutta asks for clear comprehension, sampajañña, in every daily act, going out and coming back, looking, bending, eating, speaking (mn10:8.1); the commentary divides it into four kinds (Ps on MN 10 §109; the same in its commentary on DN 2, §214). Before an act, ask whether it has a benefit (purpose) and whether it suits the time and place (suitability). While doing it, keep one’s meditation subject (resort). Throughout, know that no self goes forward or looks around (non-delusion): the eyelids open by the mind-born air element, not because someone inside opens them. For the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, the commentary says, it is chiefly non-delusion that is meant. Its examples for the first two are a monk’s: whether to visit a shrine or an elder, whether to join a festival crowd.

The commentary makes non-delusion concrete for the plainest acts. Dressing: the cloth does not know it covers a body, nor the body that it is covered; elements cover elements, and a shrine feels nothing whether it is garlanded or smeared with dirt, so fine cloth is no cause for pleasure nor poor cloth for dismay. Eating: no one opens the jaw with a key; the teeth do the work of pestle and mortar, the tongue that of a hand, and the elements carry the food in, hold it and digest it. Even the toilet: go when it is time, since holding back brings sweat, swimming eyes and a scattered mind; what leaves the body leaves unbidden, like water from an over-full jar, and is no one’s (Ps on MN 10 §109). For the monk’s robe, read clothes (this site’s adaptation).

Try this Purpose and suitability

Before one ordinary act today, such as picking up your phone or going into a shop, pause and ask: is there benefit in this? Is it suitable now? These examples are this site’s lay adaptation of a monk’s practice (Ps on MN 10 §109).

Keeping the balance

The Visuddhimagga asks for balance, not force. Faith must match wisdom: strong faith with weak wisdom believes without ground, and strong wisdom with weak faith slides into craftiness. Energy must match concentration, or the mind slides into idleness or into restlessness. For insight, strong wisdom is right, to penetrate the marks; mindfulness is wanted everywhere, like salt in every dish (Vism IV §62). The Buddha told the over-strenuous monk Soṇa the same: a lute plays only when its strings are neither too tight nor too slack (AN 6.55).

For a sluggish mind, the Buddha says, rouse investigation, energy and rapture, like dry fuel on a small fire; for a restless one, tranquillity, concentration and equanimity; mindfulness is needed in every case (SN 46.53). The Visuddhimagga makes these two of its ten skills for reaching absorption (IV §§64–65) and adds two more: stir a flat, joyless mind by reflecting on birth, ageing, sickness, death and the like, and gladden it by recollecting the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Saṅgha; and when the mind runs evenly, look on without interfering, like a charioteer whose horses are going well (IV §66; said there of calm).

Try this Sluggish or restless?

When you sit, notice which way the mind leans. If it is sluggish, look more closely and rouse a little effort. If it is restless, let it calm and look on. In both cases keep mindfulness. (SN 46.53; Vism IV §§64–66.)

Conditions matter too: seven suitable things, from lodging and food to company and posture, chosen for whatever lets the mind settle (Vism IV §59; for insight, XX §699). Even cleanliness matters: a dirty body or a cluttered room dims knowledge as a dirty wick dims a lamp (IV §61).

Above all, a good friend. The Buddha calls good friendship the whole of the spiritual life (SN 45.2) and the first condition for the rest (AN 9.1); the friend to keep will admonish and accept admonition (AN 7.37). The Visuddhimagga adds: short of the Buddha or an awakened disciple, find a learned, careful teacher who follows the tradition rather than his own opinion, and tell him about your health (Vism III §42).

Learning to see the marks

Seeing mind and matter clearly is the third purification in the Manual’s scheme, the first of insight proper, and seeing their conditions, as a doctor looks for the cause of a disease, is the fourth (IX §§30–31; Vism XIX §678).

Then the three marks are applied to what has been seen. The Manual gives a word for each: formations are impermanent because they are destroyed, suffering because they are fearful, non-self because they have no core (IX §32). The Visuddhimagga trains this by narrowing the span: the matter of a hundred-year life is impermanent; then of a decade, a year, a day, one movement, one part of a step (Vism XX §§706–711). Then the meditator turns to the mind that saw this and sees it too as impermanent, with a later mind, and so on up to a tenth (§§718–720). And the meditator guards against thinking “I see” or “I can do insight”: it is formations that see formations (§721). Buddhaghosa follows here another exposition, that of the Ariyavaṃsa, which he judges more fitting than his own first version (§717; both are given at §721).

Why are the marks hidden? Continuity hides impermanence, the shifting of posture hides suffering, and the look of a solid mass hides non-self (Vism XXI §739). Watching rise and fall breaks the continuity, attending to constant oppression uncovers what posture hides, and resolving things into elements breaks the mass.

Try this The end of each breath

After a few minutes with the breath, attend to the end of each in-breath and each out-breath: each one finishes. The Buddha’s refrain is to see the body’s arising and vanishing (mn10:5.2), and the commentary says the breath arises dependent on the body, the nostrils and the mind, like air in a smith’s bellows, and stops when they stop (Ps on MN 10 §107). The texts take this further, to the impermanence of all of body and mind (the five aggregates), after the stages of breathing that include calm (MN 118, mn118:21.1; Vism VIII §236). This site’s modern advice: if attending to the breath makes you tense, let it breathe by itself, or go back to the posture.

If the breath grows so fine that it seems to have gone, the Visuddhimagga says, do not get up to go and ask the teacher, thinking “my subject is lost”. Reflect instead: breath is absent in the unborn, the drowned, the dead and a few others; you are none of these, so it is there, only too fine for your wisdom to catch yet. Then wait where it touches, at nostril or upper lip, as a skilled ploughman whose oxen have wandered into the forest does not track them but waits at the ford where they come down to drink (Vism VIII §§229–230). The passage is written for the breath as a subject of calm.

What begins to show

Ten knowledges, one by one follows these stages in detail. First rise and fall: each present formation arises and changes, coming from no store and going to none, like the sound of a lute, which depends on the lute, its neck and the player (Vism XX §723). Ledi Sayadaw (1846–1923), a Burmese monk who wrote a commentary on the Manual, holds that a disciple’s knowledge cannot reach the arising and passing of single moments of mind and matter; for a disciple, “by moment” includes short runs of them (Paramatthadīpanī, CS §171).

As knowledge grows keen, attention settles on the ceasing alone, like bubbles bursting on a pond in heavy rain (Vism XXI §§741, 748). Then formations appear as fearful, a danger, nothing to delight in (§§750–754). The Visuddhimagga is careful here. The knowledge itself does not fear; it judges, like a man looking at pits of glowing embers who knows that whoever falls in will burn (§751). But the text knows the meditator can be alarmed: its image for danger is a frightened man with his hair on end, and it pairs “arising is fearful” with “non-arising is safe”, partly to comfort an alarmed mind (§§752–753). What it does not give is modern mental-health advice.

The mind then wants out, like a fish in a net (§756), looks again at formations by the marks, and settles into equanimity, dropping both fear and delight (§§757–766). If it does not yet see peace, it returns to formations again and again, like a ship’s crow that flies out looking for land and comes back to the mast (§767). The last steps, conformity, change-of-lineage (the moment the mind turns from formations to Nibbāna) and the path, the texts describe as moments of mind in a single process (IX §34); on this site’s reading, there is no separate technique for them.

When it goes wrong

At the early stage of rise and fall come ten experiences the Visuddhimagga calls imperfections of insight: light, knowledge, zest, tranquillity, happiness, strong faith, balanced energy, firm mindfulness, equanimity, and a subtle attachment hard to recognise as a defilement (Vism XX §§732–734). They come to one who is practising rightly (§732), at a stage well along the way, and in very different strengths (§733); the text makes them neither a goal nor a sign of attainment. This site’s advice: do not look for them, and do not measure yourself by them. The mistake it warns of is thinking “I have reached the path” and sitting enjoying them (§733). It tells of an elder who thought himself an arahant, one fully awakened, until his former pupil asked him to conjure an elephant charging at him; when he started to flee, he saw he was still a worldling, not yet awakened at all, and took a meditation subject from that pupil (§733). A modern teacher tells the same lesson against himself: Mahasi Sayadaw let his first village meditators enjoy the light and calm they reached within three days, without telling them to note the joy, and in four more days they went no further (Fundamentals of Vipassanā Meditation, “Rediscovery”). The test is to see each as arisen, impermanent and conditioned, “not mine, not I, not my self”, and to know that insight free of them, on its course, is the path (§736).

Try this When something special happens

If a light, a rush of joy, an unusual calm or clarity arises, note: “this has arisen; it is impermanent; not mine, not I, not my self”, and return to your subject. Do not take it for the path or its fruit; that is the mistake the Visuddhimagga warns about (Vism XX §§733, 736; Guide to IX §32). This site’s modern advice: lights or sensations that persist outside meditation are a matter for a doctor.

Hindrances come back; know each, and how it arose and goes (mn10:36.4–36.8). The Buddha says what feeds each and what starves it (SN 46.51). For a persistent unwholesome thought he gives five steps: replace it with a wholesome one, see its danger, leave it unattended, let its drive calm, and only as a last resort hold it down by force (MN 20).

Hard going is one of the recognised ways. The canon names four modes of progress, painful or pleasant, slow or quick, set by the strength of one’s defilements and faculties and by the kind of practice (AN 4.162–163); the Visuddhimagga sets them by how hard the defilements were to suppress and how slowly the path then came (Vism XXI §801). Moggallāna was freed by painful practice, Sāriputta by pleasant, both with swift insight (AN 4.167–168). The commentaries disagree about the details: the Visuddhimagga even makes Moggallāna’s last path slow, where the sutta and its commentary have it swift (Vism XXI §801; Mp on AN 4.167–168). Going round again is in the method too (Vism XXI §§738, 767).

In the Therīgāthā, the early nuns’ verses, it went slowly for some. Sāmā and Uttamā each left their dwelling four or five times, finding no peace of heart and no control over the mind (Thig 2.10; Thig 3.2); another Sāmā went twenty-five years without once finding her mind settled (Thig 3.1). Uttamā went to a nun she trusted, who taught her the aggregates, sense bases and elements; the commentary names that nun as Paṭācārā, who saw what her pupil’s mind needed (ThigA on Thig 3.2). All three were freed in the end.

In a household life

The texts do not keep insight for monks. The commentary says that in the Kuru country all four assemblies, monks, nuns, lay men and lay women, practised the four foundations of mindfulness as a matter of course (Ps on MN 10 §106), and it reads the sutta’s “whoever” as any of the four (§137). In the canon the layman Pessa tells the Buddha that householders too, from time to time, dwell with the four foundations well set up (MN 51). Many more than five hundred celibate lay followers are non-returners, at the third of four stages of awakening, and many more than five hundred who still enjoy sensual pleasures have gone beyond doubt (mn73:9.3–12.3). Dīghāvu, gravely ill, already contemplated impermanence, suffering and non-self, and died a non-returner (SN 55.3); the householder Citta, by his own account, had no fetter left that would bring him back (SN 41.9; see Four noble persons). The lay follower rich in wisdom, the Buddha says, sees rise and fall (SN 55.37).

On his deathbed Anāthapiṇḍika heard a teaching on not grasping at anything and wept: he had never heard such a talk. Told that it was not usually given to lay people, he asked that it should be (MN 143). The Buddha had once told him that perceiving impermanence for a finger-snap bears more fruit than vast gifts (AN 9.20).

On this site’s reading, the texts assume seclusion and continuous practice for the deeper stages (mn10:4.2; Ps on MN 10 §107; MN 39). The promise of results within seven years, or even seven days, is for one who develops the four foundations in this way (mn10:46.1–46.23; the whole scale, down to seven days, is meant for a person of middling ability, the commentary says, and one of keen wisdom may attain between morning and evening, Ps §137). The lay examples mostly practise from time to time (MN 51) or meet the teaching at the end of life (SN 55.3; MN 143).

What the texts leave open

Much that is taught today is not in the texts. There is no retreat format: the nearest precedent is fifty monks who agreed not to speak to one another until they reached arahantship (Ps on MN 10 §109). There is no technique of noting each event with a word. The sutta gives the content of knowing in direct speech, “I am walking”, “a mind with greed” (mn10:6.1, 34.2); the commentary does not discuss words, and says the knowing that counts is the one that removes the notion of a being (Ps §108). On this site’s reading, the closest model for repeated mental naming is the brief elements method, which names elements, not activities (Vism XI §308). Labelling each event, as in the school of Mahasi Sayadaw in Burma, sweeping attention through the body, as in the line of U Ba Khin and S. N. Goenka, and analysing matter into its smallest groups (kalāpas), as taught by Pa-Auk Sayadaw, are, in this site’s summary, modern methods that draw on these texts in different ways. The texts know no sixteen knowledges and no “dark night”, and the canon does not name momentary concentration (see Ten knowledges).

The safeguards the texts give are a good friend, balanced faculties, suitable conditions and the test for the imperfections. What follows is this site’s own modern advice, not the texts’. For intensive practice, work with a qualified teacher. If practice brings distress that does not settle, stop, and seek help. If you have a history of trauma or mental illness, talk to a professional before intensive practice. None of the exercises here asks you to endure pain, go without food or sleep, or hold down strong feelings, and none should.

To see the whole road at once, with what each stage looks like, a picture from the texts and a life that shows it, open the path of insight, where the same road can also be opened in 3D, as a path climbing a hill.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The commentary on the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta says that dogs and jackals also know when they are walking. The next time you walk across a room, what would have to be different about your knowing for it to count as the knowing the Buddha meant?

One way to see it, after you have tried

The dog knows it is walking, and the sense of a walker stays untouched. The commentary's knowing asks who walks, whose walking it is, and why. The commentary's answer: an intention arises, the movement follows from it, and no one walks over and above them; the <i>Visuddhimagga</i> adds that each part of a step ends where it is. 'I walk' stays useful as a way of speaking. On this reading the difference is not in walking slowly or saying words to oneself, but in what the knowing removes: the sense of a walker.
