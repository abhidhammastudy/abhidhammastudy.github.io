How insight is taught today

The living methods side by side: what a beginner is asked to do in each, how much calm comes first, which map of stages is used, how closely each follows the texts it cites, and how to choose and practise safely.

About 16 minutesManual IX §§22–37 with the Guides and Table 9.2 (the marks, purification of view and of overcoming doubt, the ten insight knowledges, the imperfections, insight leading to emergence, the three doors)

In brief

  • Every method described here is modern. The suttas and the Manual say what to know and give a map of stages; the Visuddhimagga adds some technique, such as counting the breath and the touch-point; none gives a timetable or a retreat.
  • Mahāsi Sayadaw teaches noting: a light mental label for the rising and falling of the abdomen and for whatever else appears, with walking in parts, from the first hour and without calm first.
  • In the line of U Ba Khin and S. N. Goenka, a ten-day course spends three and a half days on the breath, then moves attention through the body, observing sensation without reacting.
  • Pa-Auk Sayadaw follows the Visuddhimagga step by step: jhāna through the breath, or access through the four elements, then a detailed analysis of matter and mind before insight proper.
  • Each method reads the texts in its own way. Some readings are close (the four elements), some go beyond them (stored reactions surfacing as sensation, seeing past lives); this page says which is which.
  • Intensive practice helps many people and troubles some. This site's advice: a qualified teacher, honesty about your health, and stopping to seek help if distress does not settle.

How insight is practised kept to the texts. This reading describes what is taught now: the main living methods, side by side. For each it asks what a beginner is asked to do, how much calm comes first, which map of stages is used, and how closely the method follows the texts it cites. Every method here is modern. Each is described from its teachers’ own books where we could read them, otherwise from Jack Kornfield’s Living Buddhist Masters, with criticisms named and the tradition’s answers given where they exist.

Why there are many methods

The texts give a method but no technique. The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta says what to know: walking as walking, a pleasant feeling as pleasant, a mind with greed as a mind with greed. The Manual gives the forty subjects of calm (IX §§1–21) and the map of purifications and knowledges (IX §§22–34). The sutta says only where and how to sit: cross-legged, body upright, in a quiet place (MN 10, mn10:4.2). Neither it nor the Manual says what to attend to in the first hour or how many hours to practise. Every living method fills that gap from somewhere: the sutta and its commentary, the Visuddhimagga’s meditation chapters, and the experience of teachers.

Most of the methods below come from Burma. Ledi Sayadaw, known on this site for his Pāli commentary on the Manual, also wrote practice manuals in Burmese for monks and lay people alike: one on mindfulness of breathing in 1904, and a manual of insight finished in 1915 for Buddhists in Europe (Ānāpāna Dīpanī, pp. 655–660; Manual of Insight, p. 44). His line of practice passed, Anālayo writes (2020, p. 195, citing Solé-Leris), through a follower of his, the lay teacher Saya Thetgyi, to U Ba Khin, a government accounts officer and later Accountant General of Burma, and from him to S. N. Goenka (Essentials, biographical sketch). In Rangoon a lay society to support meditation, the Buddhasāsana Nuggaha Organization, was founded in 1947; Mahāsi Sayadaw came to its new centre in November 1949 and began teaching there on 4 December (Criticisms and Replies, Foreword; Practical Vipassanā Exercises, biography). In 1955 his method reached Sri Lanka, where a centre was opened at Kanduboda near Colombo (Criticisms and Replies, Foreword).

Mahāsi Sayadaw: noting

Mahāsi Sayadaw (1904–1982) trained under Mingun Jetavana Sayadaw at Thaton and was a final editor and questioner at the Sixth Buddhist Council (Satipaṭṭhāna Vipassanā Meditation, preface). Page numbers below are those of his Practical Insight Meditation.

The first day. A lay meditator takes the eight precepts and reflects briefly on the Buddha, goodwill, the body’s unattractiveness and death (pp. 1–3). Then he sits with his mind, not his eyes, on the abdomen, resting both hands on it if the movement is unclear. He notes “rising” and “falling” silently, without treating them as words and without deepening the breath (pp. 3–4). Wandering, pain, itching, hearing and thinking are noted as they come; then he returns to the abdomen. Before moving, he notes the intention to move (pp. 4–7). Walking, which alternates with sitting (Kornfield, ch. 5, p. 54), is noted first as “left, right”, later in two and then three parts: lifting, pushing forward, putting down (p. 9). Eating and dressing are noted in detail, from waking until sleep (pp. 9–15).

The format. Jack Kornfield’s Living Buddhist Masters (1977) describes sitting and walking for about sixteen hours a day (Living Buddhist Masters, ch. 5, p. 54); Mahāsi’s own booklet sets about four hours’ sleep for a serious meditator, five or six being acceptable (Practical Vipassanā Exercises). The length of periods and the timing of interviews vary between centres; his books do not fix them.

Calm. No jhāna comes first. Mahāsi argues that the concentration built up moment by moment in noting, khaṇika-samādhikhaṇika-samādhi · momentary concentration concentration that holds each passing event in turn; the term is in the Visuddhimagga; the Guide reckons it as purification of mind for the pure-insight meditator, counts as access and as purification of mind, and cites the Visuddhimagga’s sub-commentary for it (Progress of Insight, ch. II(b)). The Visuddhimagga itself names the dry-insight worker, whose path has no jhāna beneath it (Vism XXI §798).

The map. The Progress of Insight numbers seventeen knowledges: the usual sixteen, plus insight leading to emergence. The names and order are the Visuddhimagga’s. What Mahāsi adds is how each shows itself to a noting meditator: many pains and mental images at the knowledge of conditionality, lights and rapture at rise and fall, the shapes of things fading at dissolution, pains and restlessness of posture peaking at re-observation, and noting that runs without effort at equanimity (pp. 16–32). A pupil with a teacher, he says, should not read the stages beforehand (pp. 34–35).

Against the texts. The abdomen: the canon counts the winds in the belly as internal air (MN 28, mn28:21.5), but does not name the abdomen as a meditation subject, and nor does the Visuddhimagga. Mahāsi treats its movement as the air element (Progress of Insight, ch. II(a)). His school adds that it supplements breathing rather than replacing it, and that it falls under the sutta’s clause on knowing the body however it is placed, read after Ledi Sayadaw as covering everything (Criticisms and Replies, Part Two); the commentary on DN 22 calls the clause all-inclusive but explains it by the four postures (Sv §375). Walking: the Visuddhimagga divides a step into six parts as a reflection within the knowledge of comprehension (Vism XX §710). Mahāsi’s own texts use up to three parts; on this site’s reading, slow walking in parts applies that idea rather than repeating the passage. The labels: an Abhidhamma student will ask how words, which are concepts, can serve insight, whose field is formations (IX §32). Mahāsi’s answer is that the note is not a word repeated, and that it thins out: as insight quickens, a general note is enough, and at dissolution names fade (pp. 4, 23, 27).

His assurances. Mahāsi tells his pupils that the pains of practice are not disease and that one who persists will come to no harm, and that lost sleep at dissolution will not make one ill (pp. 7–8, 28–29). He also writes that path and fruit may come within a month, or within seven days for one of exceptional maturity (Practical Vipassanā Exercises). These are a teacher’s assurances, not medical findings.

A criticism. In the 1960s Kheminda Thera of Sri Lanka argued in print that momentary concentration is not purification of mind. U Ñāṇuttara replied, and the exchange is collected in Criticisms and Replies (Part One). The tradition replies from the canon’s insight-first route (AN 4.170), the commentaries’ pure-insight vehicle and Dhammapāla’s sub-commentary; Bhikkhu Bodhi’s Guide to the Manual gives the same reading (CMA IX, Guide to §29).

Ledi Sayadaw: manuals for everyone

Ledi left manuals rather than a retreat method. His Ānāpāna Dīpanī puts mindfulness of the body, through the breath, before both calm and insight: without it, he says, a meditator is like a madman with no control of his mind (pp. 655–660). The ideal order is the four jhānas first. But one may turn to insight from any jhāna, from access, or even from the early stages of counting and following the breath, once the mind has stopped wandering (section XVII, pp. 687–688). His Manual of Insight sets out matter, mind and their conditions in Abhidhamma terms, then the three marks. Insight, he writes there, can be practised anywhere, even in a household life, by one whose knowledge is mature; the immature need calm exercises first (p. 40).

A beginner on Ledi’s plan, then, steadies the mind on the breath, studies mind, matter and their conditions in the Abhidhamma’s terms, and contemplates the marks. In his commentary on the Manual he also holds that a disciple cannot see mind and matter arise and cease at single moments: “by moment” must include short runs of them (Paramatthadīpanī, CS §171). His practice manuals are modern Burmese works, not part of the commentarial layer.

U Ba Khin and S. N. Goenka: the body sweep

U Ba Khin first tried mindfulness of breathing on 1 January 1937 and soon took a course with Saya Thetgyi, a farmer and meditation teacher. Webu Sayadaw was the first to urge him to teach, and in 1952 he opened the International Meditation Centre in Rangoon (Essentials, pp. v–viii). Goenka trained with him for fourteen years and was authorised to teach in 1969 (dhamma.org).

The first day. The course is residential: ten days, twelve with arrival and departure, free, and paid for by donations from those who have finished one. Students agree to stay to the end, keep silence until the last full day, keep five precepts (old students eight) and practise no other technique. The day runs from a four o’clock bell through about ten hours of sitting, with interviews at noon and a recorded discourse in the evening (Code of Discipline; Questions and answers). The first instruction is to feel the natural breath at the nostrils, then its sensations within a small area there (Discourse Summaries, Day 2).

The rest of the course. The breath work fills the first three and a half days (Hart, The Art of Living, p. 7). On the fourth, attention moves through the body a few inches at a time, from head to feet, observing each sensation without reacting, as impermanent (Day 4). From then on the three group sittings are hours without moving (Day 9). Later the body may be felt as a free flow of subtle vibration; gross sensations that return are explained as deep reactions surfacing, not a step back (Day 7). Goodwill, mettāmettā · loving-kindness the wish that beings be well; in the Abhidhamma, the mental factor non-hatred taking beings as object, is taught on the last full day. There is no walking meditation. At home the student is asked to sit an hour morning and evening, and to take a course each year (Day 11).

Calm and map. The breath days are a foundation; Anālayo notes that Goenka holds formal calm to be a necessary basis for insight (2011). Goenka says progress is measured only by equanimity towards every sensation (Day 7), though the tenth-day discourse names rise and fall, dissolution and equanimity about formations. U Ba Khin lists ten knowledges; those who progress quickly, he says, may recognise them only in retrospect, and no one should look forward to them (Essentials, pp. 17–18).

Against the texts. Sensation: Goenka renders the line that all things converge on feeling, vedanāvedanā · feeling feeling: the pleasant, painful or neutral tone of a moment; not emotion (AN 8.83), as everything being accompanied by sensation (Discourse Summaries, p. 98). The commentary takes the feeling there as the three feelings (Mp on AN 9.14). In the Manual’s terms a tingle or a pressure is three things at once: the tangible, which is matter (VI §3); body-consciousness; and its feeling, which in body-consciousness is always bodily pleasure or pain, never neutral (III §§2–3; Guide to I §8). Goenka can point to a sutta where neutral feelings too arise in the body (SN 36.12); the Manual confines body-consciousness itself to pleasure or pain. On this site’s reading the method watches the tangible and its feeling-tone together, but its “sensation” is not the Manual’s feeling. Saṅkhāra: for U Ba Khin every action leaves a force that accumulates and is worn away by seeing impermanence (Essentials, p. 13). For Goenka it is a reaction; when no new one is made, an old one surfaces with a sensation and, met with equanimity, is removed (Days 4–6). Hart glosses the word as volition, close to the Manual’s twenty-nine mundane volitions (Guide to VIII §3). But stored reactions used up by equanimity have no warrant in the Manual, where it is the paths that cut the defilements (IX §§38–41). Kalāpas: U Ba Khin calls the tiny groups of matter, kalāpakalāpa the smallest group of matter: eight or more kinds arising and ceasing together, the Buddha’s own teaching (Essentials, p. 15); Bhikkhu Bodhi says they are first spelled out in the commentaries, though implied by the canon’s division of matter (CMA, Introduction). The sweep: the Visuddhimagga reads the sutta’s “experiencing the whole body” as the whole of each breath, its beginning, middle and end (Vism VIII §220). Anālayo traces the scan to an old alternative reading of that step, found in a meditation manual compiled in Chinese by Kumārajīva and in three Indian monastic codes, where the breath pervades the whole body; using the scan to watch the groups of matter seems to have arisen somewhere between Ledi and U Ba Khin, perhaps as U Ba Khin’s own contribution (2020, pp. 196, 198–199). Anālayo himself reads the step as the physical body, not the breath alone (p. 200).

The lineage claim. The tradition holds that the technique was kept in its original form by an unbroken chain of teachers since the Buddha (Hart, Foreword; dhamma.org). Anālayo cites the historian Erik Braun: as far as Braun knows, Ledi made no such claim, and neither Saya Thetgyi nor U Ba Khin laid such stress on a perfectly preserved technique. Anālayo suggests the claim may be meant of contemplating impermanence in general, which the early discourses do teach (2020, p. 196).

Pa-Auk Sayadaw: the Visuddhimagga step by step

At Pa-Auk Sayadaw’s own wish, his books carry no biography (Knowing and Seeing, imprint).

The first day. The meditator attends to the breath where it touches, at the nostril or the upper lip, without following it into the body, counting from five to ten if that helps (Knowing and Seeing, Talk 1, pp. 33–35; compare Vism VIII §229). The aim is the sign, nimittanimitta · meditation sign the preliminary, learning or counterpart image of the meditation object, and then the jhānas. The other entry is four-elements meditation, which reaches access (Introduction, p. 12). Every meditator, with jhāna or without, completes four-elements meditation before insight, discerning twelve characteristics in the body, from pushing and hardness to cohesion and flowing, which the Sayadaw takes from the Dhammasaṅgaṇī. One with jhāna enters the fourth jhāna at each sitting first (Talk 4, pp. 116–117).

After that. The body is seen as tiny groups of matter, which are analysed into their kinds (Talk 4, pp. 122–124); then the cittas and their factors; then dependent origination, traced into past and future lives (Introduction, pp. 24–25; Talk 6, p. 183); and only then the insight knowledges, in the Visuddhimagga’s order. The compiler of one of his books warns that readers new to the Visuddhimagga and the Abhidhamma will find the method hard (The Practice Which Leads to Nibbāna). How long retreats last and how often students see the teacher could not be checked for this page.

Against the texts. Closely, in most places. The Sayadaw cites Vism XVIII §664, where the pure-insight meditator, or one who goes by calm, discerns the four elements, the groups of matter become evident, and then mind through the doors: close to his sequence. The Visuddhimagga and the Manual agree that the elements give access only (Vism XI §308; CMA IX §14). On past lives he goes further. Vism XIX §679 says that, having seen present mind and matter arise from conditions, the meditator sees that as now, so in the past and future; on this site’s reading, an inference from the present. The Sayadaw reads it as seeing, and his editors say this seeing is the power of insight, not the higher knowledge of past lives (Talk 6, n. 351). The groups of matter are commentarial (CMA, Introduction). On this site’s reading, Ledi’s view that a disciple cannot follow single moments of mind and matter (Paramatthadīpanī, CS §171) sits uneasily with seeing them group by group. The tradition answers that concentration brings a light of wisdom, citing AN 4.141–145 and SN 56.11, and that the light is not itself an imperfection, only a basis for one (Introduction, p. 13 and n. 43; compare Vism XX §§732–736).

Mogok Sayadaw: conditions first

Mogok Sayadaw (1900–1962) was a scholar for over thirty years and followed in Ledi Sayadaw’s footsteps in stressing dependent origination (Kornfield, ch. 12, p. 215). In Kornfield’s account his students first learn the ultimate realities and dependent origination. Practice then begins with mindfulness of breathing to concentrate the mind, and turns to consciousness or feeling, where change must be experienced, not merely noted (pp. 216–218). Its frame is dependent origination; Kornfield gives only a three-stage outline, not the list of knowledges (pp. 216, 226–227). The commentary on MN 10 already pairs contemplation of mind with people led by views and with the insight vehicle (Ps on MN 10 §106); that link is this site’s comparison, not one Mogok is known to make.

Briefly: Sunlun, Webu, the Thai forest, Ajahn Naeb, Sri Lanka

Sunlun Sayadaw (1878–1952) taught about forty-five minutes of vigorous breathing with attention at the nostrils, then mindfulness of sensation, sitting without moving for two or three hours. Pain is a central object and effort is strong; the lineage criticises noting methods for working through concepts (Kornfield, ch. 6, pp. 87–90). This site’s note: vigorous breathing and long motionless sitting are for a teacher’s guidance, not for trying from a page.

Webu Sayadaw (1896–1977) taught attention to the touch of the breath at the nostrils, held with determination. One need not master all the scriptures, he said: take one object and persist (The Way to Ultimate Calm, Introduction; Discourse V, pp. 39–40).

The Thai forest. Ajahn Chah told Kornfield that the Dhamma is found not in books but in watching one’s own mind; books can come later. He held no daily interviews, though questions were welcome at any time, so that students would learn to examine their own doubt (ch. 4, pp. 39, 50). Ajahn Mahā Boowa allowed either order: some calm the mind with the word buddho and let concentration support wisdom; others, whose minds will not settle, must use wisdom first (ch. 9, pp. 176–178). On this site’s reading, that echoes the canon’s several routes (AN 4.170).

Ajahn Naeb (1897–1983), a Thai woman teacher in Bangkok, not of the forest tradition, studied Abhidhamma and insight under Ajahn Pathunta U Vilāsa. She taught that a right understanding of mind and matter must come before practice, and that developing calm is no part of insight (Kornfield, ch. 8, pp. 137–138).

Sri Lanka. Besides the Mahāsi centres there is Matara Sri Ñāṇārāma, meditation master of Mitirigala Nissaraṇa Vanaya. He trained in the Mahāsi system under U Javana in 1958 (author’s biography) and specialised in the Burmese methods, but did not keep to pure insight: his Seven Stages of Purification walks the whole map of the purifications and stresses both calm and insight (translator’s preface).

What they share, and where they differ

On this site’s reading they share a good deal. All begin with precepts, the first purification (IX §28). All point the mind at body and mind as they happen now, not at ideas about them; the study in Pa-Auk and Mogok is preparation for seeing. All take the three marks as the content of insight, and all value continuity. The two maps checked here, Mahāsi’s and Pa-Auk’s, treat lights and rapture as things to see through, not as attainment, as the Visuddhimagga does (Vism XX §§732–736).

They differ in five ways that matter to a beginner. First, how much calm, and when: none first (Mahāsi), a few days (Goenka), jhāna where possible, access at least (Pa-Auk), the jhānas ideally (Ledi), or by temperament (Mahā Boowa). This is the texts’ own question of the two vehicles (Vism XVIII §§663–664; AN 4.170), not a modern invention. Second, what the mind does with an object: notes it, feels it with equanimity, analyses it, relates it to its conditions, or simply knows it. Third, how much Abhidhamma is put to work, from Pa-Auk’s full analysis to the forest’s distance from books. Fourth, how much is said about stages, from Mahāsi’s signposts to Ajahn Chah’s silence. Fifth, the form: a long intensive retreat, a fixed ten-day course, or a monastic life. The teacher’s part varies too: Mahāsi writes that his treatise is enough for a person of fair intelligence, though without a teacher the paths are hard to reach, so one should find a teacher qualified by his own attainments (pp. 46–47), while Ajahn Chah avoided daily interviews.

None of these methods is the Buddha’s method, or the Manual’s, as such. Each is a way of applying the texts, read closely in some places and freely in others.

Choosing and practising safely

What follows is this site’s own advice, not the texts’ and not any tradition’s.

Choose by what you would actually be asked to do. A ten-day course asks you to stay the whole time, in silence, sitting about ten hours a day. A Mahāsi retreat asks for noting from waking to sleep. Pa-Auk asks for a long, step-by-step training that, its compiler warns, is hard to follow without some knowledge of the Visuddhimagga and the Abhidhamma. The commentary on MN 10 matches the four foundations of mindfulness to different temperaments (Ps on MN 10 §106); on this reading, a method that suits one person may not suit another, and that is no failure.

Many people find practice helpful, but intensive practice can also be hard. One study of Western Buddhist meditators mapped fifty-nine kinds of meditation-related experience in seven domains, ranging from very positive to very negative; it was not designed to count how common they are. Most of those with difficulties had no psychiatric history, and for nearly three in four of them the difficulties began during or just after a retreat (Lindahl et al. 2017). In a survey of 1,232 regular meditators, about a quarter reported a particularly unpleasant experience they put down to meditation, more often among those who had ever been on retreat or practised only “deconstructive” methods such as vipassanā. It asked a single question and did not assess earlier mental health, and its links are associations, not causes (Schlosser et al. 2019). In eight-week mindfulness courses (not retreats) for 96 adults with depression, lasting bad effects affected 6 to 14 per cent, depending on whether “lasting” meant more than a month, a week or a day: rates like those of other psychological treatments, alongside overall benefit (Britton et al. 2021).

The canon knows this too. When monks practising the meditation on the body’s unattractiveness turned against their own bodies, and many took their lives, the Buddha taught mindfulness of breathing as a peaceful practice that settles unwholesome states (SN 54.9, sn54.9:3.3–3.4, 6.3). Anālayo, who takes this as his example, argues that the insight knowledges, with their hard stages, are a historical development specific to Theravāda vipassanā, not early Buddhism’s picture of the path (he notes that a shorter list is already in the Paṭisambhidāmagga, which Theravāda counts as canonical); and that mindfulness is no cure-all, needing professional help beside it in trauma and mental illness (2019).

The traditions have safeguards of their own. Goenka centres ask applicants to disclose their mental-health history and medication, warn that old symptoms may return during or after a course, and do not recommend it for people with significant mental-health issues; it is, they say, no substitute for treatment (Code of Discipline; Questions and answers). Mahāsi asks pupils not to read the stages in advance, to entrust themselves to the Buddha and the teacher against frightening visions, and to lean on a wall or lie down if swaying grows (Practical Insight Meditation, pp. 1, 8).

So, as this site’s advice: for intensive practice, work with a qualified teacher you can speak to. Tell the centre honestly about your health, medication and any history of trauma or mental illness, and if you have such a history, talk to a doctor or therapist first. Ask before you go what happens if you need to stop. If things become too much, ease off: walk, eat, rest. If sleep breaks down for more than a night or two, if fear, unreality or unusual energy persists outside sitting, or if you cannot manage daily life, stop, tell the teacher and seek medical help; thoughts of harming yourself need help at once. Treat the stages in books as maps for teachers, not targets. The texts’ own safeguards, a good friend, balanced faculties and suitable conditions, are in How insight is practised.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta says only that one walking knows "I am walking". Mahāsi Sayadaw has the meditator note the parts of each step; Goenka's courses teach no walking meditation at all; Pa-Auk has the meditator analyse the body's matter into its smallest groups. All three say they follow the sutta. What does each add to it, and which of those additions would you want a teacher to explain before you began?

One way to see it, after you have tried

The sutta gives the content of knowing, not a technique. Mahāsi adds a word, a step noted in parts (an idea the <i>Visuddhimagga</i> also uses, with six parts), and a whole day of continuous noting. Goenka leaves posture aside and adds a sequence through the body and a teaching about stored reactions. Pa-Auk adds the commentaries' analysis of matter and a long training in concentration first. None of this is wrong for being added: every practice needs some form. What a careful student wants is to see where the text ends and the method begins, and to have the teacher say why the method takes that step, what it expects to happen, and what to do if something goes wrong.
