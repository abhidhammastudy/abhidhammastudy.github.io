Kamma, death and rebirth

Four ways to sort kamma, the signs that appear to a dying mind, and the hinge between two lives, where nothing passes over and yet nothing is lost.

About 9 minutesManual V §§18–33 (kamma: four ways, unwholesome and wholesome kamma, results), §§34–41 (death and rebirth)

In brief

  • Kamma is volition, the willing behind a deed, and it leaves a potency that can ripen later as an experience or as a new birth.
  • Weighty kamma ripens first, then kamma done or recalled near death, then habitual kamma, what one has done again and again, then anything else strong enough.
  • Death comes when the life-span runs out, or the sustaining kamma, or both, or when a destructive kamma cuts life short, like a lamp blown out.
  • A dying mind takes as object the deed, a sign of it, or a sign of the world to come, and the new life begins with that object.
  • Nothing passes from one life to the next, yet the new consciousness is conditioned by the old, as a seal's impression is by the seal.
  • The first moment of a life sets a background that runs until death, while each new choice made against it makes fresh kamma.

“It is volition, monks, that I call kamma. Having willed, one acts by body, speech and mind” (AN 6.63). In the Abhidhamma this sentence becomes exact. Kammakamma wholesome or unwholesome volition, which later ripens as results is the volition, cetanācetanā · volition the willing that organises the other factors; as kamma, it bears results, that arises in wholesome and unwholesome javanas. The act itself, the gift or the blow, is the expression. The kamma is the willing.

A volition does not vanish without trace. It leaves a potency that can ripen, later, as a resultant citta: as a pleasant or unpleasant sight, as a sound, as a rebirth. The Manual sorts this ripening four ways.

Four ways to sort kamma

By function: some kamma produces results, giving rise to resultant cittas and kamma-born matter. Some supports kamma that is already ripening, some obstructs it, and some destroys it, cutting off a weaker kamma and taking its place.

By order of ripening: weighty kamma ripens first. On the unwholesome side this means the five heinous deeds, such as killing a parent, and wrong views that deny the results of deeds, held firmly; on the wholesome side, the jhānas. Next comes death-proximate kamma, an act done or remembered just before death. Then habitual kamma, āciṇṇaāciṇṇa · habitual kamma what one does again and again; next in line after death-proximate kamma, what one has done again and again. Last is reserve kamma: anything else done that is strong enough to ripen.

By time: some kamma ripens in this life, some in the next, some in any later life, and some lapses without ripening at all.

By place of result: unwholesome kamma ripens in the sense sphere; wholesome sense-sphere kamma ripens there too; the jhānas ripen in the fine-material worlds; the immaterial attainments in the immaterial ones.

Four ways a life ends

Death, in the Abhidhamma’s terms, is the cutting off of the life faculty that belongs to one existence. The Manual names four ways it comes (V §34). The life-span may run out, as it does in worlds with a fixed span, or in old age. (Then, if the kamma that made the life still has force, it can bring a new birth on the same level or higher.) The kamma may run out while the life-span still has years to give. Both may run out together. Or a destructive kamma may cut the life short, overriding the kamma that sustained it.

The first three are timely deaths; the fourth is untimely. The Guide’s image is an oil lamp, which goes out when the wick is spent, or the oil, or both, or when a gust of wind blows it out.

The last moments

As death approaches, the Manual says (V §35), one of three kinds of object presents itself to the mind. It may be the kamma itself, the deed recalled or re-lived (Guide to V §36). It may be a sign of the kamma, kamma-nimittakamma-nimitta · kamma sign at death, an object linked with a past deed, such as offered flowers, an image connected with the act. Or it may be a sign of destiny, gati-nimittagati-nimitta · destiny sign at death, an image of the coming birth, an image of the world about to be entered. The last process of the life takes this object, and its javanas, weak now, run five times instead of seven (IV §21; V §38).

Counted out, the last process is short. The life-continuum vibrates and is arrested. Adverting turns to the object: at the mind door if it is a kamma recalled, and at any of the six doors if it is a sign of kamma. Where a sign of destiny appears is read two ways (V §38 and its Guide; see the note). The five javanas run. Then death may come at once, or after two moments of registration, or after a few more moments of life-continuum; the figure below shows the shortest case.noteThe Manual’s wording, as the Burmese teacher Ledi Sayadaw reads it, lets a sign of destiny appear at any of the six doors; the Visuddhimagga (XVII) has it appear at the mind door, its sub-commentary says only there, and the Vibhāvinī follows them (Guide to V §38). This is a difference among commentators. The principle is the same for both: the last process carries the object that the next life will begin with.

The hinge

Then comes the death-consciousness, cuticuti · death consciousness the last citta of a life, of the same kind as its life-continuum: the last citta of a life, the same kind as the life-continuum and with the same object as it. It ceases. Immediately after it, with no gap at all, the rebirth-linking consciousness, paṭisandhipaṭisandhi · rebirth-linking the first citta of a life: a resultant that links it to the last, arises in a new life, taking as its object the sign that the dying process presented.

Nothing, the Abhidhamma holds, passes across. No soul migrates, no consciousness is carried over. The new citta is a different citta. But it is conditioned by the old: by the kamma that ripens in it, and by the dying citta as the proximity condition that makes way for it. The commentaries compare this to a seal pressed into wax, to an echo answering a voice, to a flame lit from another flame. The impression is not the seal. It is not unrelated to the seal either.

A life of one kind

The rebirth-linking citta sets the tone of the new life. The same kind of citta then runs as the bhavaṅgabhavaṅga · life-continuum the resultant citta, of the same kind as rebirth-linking, that runs between processes, the life-continuum, whenever no process is active, and at the end it will be the death-consciousness. One kamma, ripening as one kind of resultant citta, holds a whole life’s background together.

The foreground is different. Every process in the new life has its own javanas, making new kamma, which will ripen in their turn. The wheel turns on these two at once: the given background of a life, and the choices made against it, moment by moment.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Habitual kamma ripens at death when nothing weightier or nearer takes precedence. What would your habits bring to mind if you had to die today?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Whatever the mind returns to most often. That is the practical teaching of habitual kamma: what we repeat shapes not only character but the last moments that condition rebirth. It is also why the tradition values small, daily wholesome acts. They become the default.
