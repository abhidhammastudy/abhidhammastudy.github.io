Names and the named

If a chariot is only a name, what is a name? The Manual's last word on what there is: concepts, the things they make known, and the six ways of naming, from "feeling" to "king's son".

About 8 minutes

In brief

  • After counting every reality, the Manual turns to what is left over: concepts (paññatti), which have no nature of their own yet are met constantly.
  • Every concept has two sides: the meaning made known, and the name that makes it known, as the word "dog" calls up a dog.
  • Meanings such as mountain, house, person or month are built on real things, and reach the mind like shadows that cannot be found when looked for.
  • Names come in six kinds, by whether they name something real or unreal, directly or through something else, as in "woman's voice" or "king's son".
  • Ordinary speech mixes the two, and the mix is where trouble starts: a real feeling, named through an unreal owner, seems to belong to someone.
  • Concepts are real objects of real minds and are not dismissed; insight leaves the net of names in place and looks through it to the realities beneath.

A chariot of parts takes the chariot away and leaves its parts. But the word “chariot” did not vanish. People still say it, understand it, and steer by it. The Manual waits until the end of its chapter on conditions to say what such words and their meanings are (VIII §§29–32). Having counted every reality, it turns to what is left over.

What is left over

The realities fall into two classes. Matter is one: the twenty-eight kinds, a single aggregate. The other is called nāma, name, or the immaterial: citta and the mental factors, which make up the four mental aggregates, and Nibbāna. The Guide explains the word. Mind is nāma because it bends toward its object; Nibbāna is nāma only because it makes something else bend, drawing the cittas of awakening, called path and fruition, onto itself as their object.

Everything else that the mind can take up is paññattipaññatti · concept a concept: a name, or what a name refers to, such as “chariot”, concept. A concept has no nature of its own, and yet the mind meets concepts constantly: in every word, every plan, every person thought of.

Two sides of a concept

Every concept has two sides (§29). One is what is made known, the meaning: attha-paññattiattha-paññatti · meaning-concept a concept as what is made known: the meaning a name conveys, such as the idea of a chariot. The other is what makes it known, the name: nāma-paññattināma-paññatti · name-concept a concept as what makes known: the name or designation itself, the word “chariot”. The Guide’s example is a dog. The idea of a four-legged, furry, domestic animal of a certain kind is the meaning; the word “dog” is the name that calls it up.

What is made known

The Manual lists the meanings by what each is built on (§30):

  • land, mountain, built on the elements in their changing shapes; the Guide calls these formal concepts;
  • house, chariot, built on materials put together: collective concepts;
  • person, individual, built on the five aggregates;
  • east, morning, month, built on the course of the moon and sun: concepts of place and of time;
  • well, cave, built on a place where nothing strikes: concepts of space;
  • the kasiṇa sign, the image that arises in meditation on a device such as a coloured disk, built on an element and on the work of meditation.

None of these exists in the ultimate sense. The Manual’s image is that they reach the mind as shadows of real things: real enough to be objects of consciousness, never real enough to be found when looked for.

What makes known

Names come in six kinds (§31). Two are simple: a name for something real, as “matter” or “feeling”; and a name for something unreal, as “land” or “mountain”. The other four are compounds that name one thing by means of another, real or unreal. The Manual gives one example of each:

  • “possessor of six direct knowledges”: the knowledges are real, the possessor is not;
  • “woman’s voice”: the sound is real, the woman, as a whole, is not;
  • “eye-consciousness”: both the eye-sensitivity and the consciousness that depends on it are real;
  • “king’s son”: neither the king nor the son is real in the ultimate sense.

The grid is less a puzzle than a habit worth learning. Ordinary speech mixes the two registers in nearly every phrase, and the mix is where the trouble starts: a real feeling, named through an unreal owner, seems to belong to someone.

How a word is understood

The chapter ends with how a name works at all (§32). A spoken word is a sound, and it is first known like any other sound, by a run of cittas at the ear. Then further runs of cittas follow at the mind alone. They take up the concept the sound stands for, and only then is the meaning understood. Concepts, the Manual says, are made by the world’s agreement.noteSo the same sound can carry different concepts in different languages, or none, to someone who does not know the language. The sound is real; the meaning rides on convention.

Why the Abhidhamma bothers

Concepts are not dismissed. They are real objects of real cittas. The kasiṇa sign is the object of the fine-material jhānas, and beings are the object of loving-kindness (see Feeling, roots and objects). A person remembered can inspire faith years later; the Paṭṭhāna counts that as a strong form of support, called decisive support (VIII Table 8.3). What concepts cannot be is a base or an element: those lists hold only things with a nature of their own (VII §36).

So the two truths are not two worlds. There is one world of realities, and over it a net of names that lets beings speak, plan and practise. Insight does not tear the net. It looks through it to the realities it was laid over.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

"Woman's voice" names something real by means of something that is not. Pick a sentence you said today and sort its words: which name realities, which name constructions, and which do both at once?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Almost every sentence mixes them. "My back hurts" names a pain that is real, a back that is a concept built on matter, and an owner, "my", that is a concept built on the aggregates. The exercise is not meant to make you stop speaking that way. It shows how quickly the constructions carry the realities along with them, so that the pain seems to belong to someone. Insight works by noticing the real part, the painful feeling, without the concept of an owner fused to it.
