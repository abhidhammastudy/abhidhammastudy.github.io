Nibbāna, the unconditioned

The fourth ultimate reality, the one the analysis does not take apart: what the suttas and the Manual say it is, why the commentaries deny that it is mere absence, and which few moments of mind ever take it as their object.

About 12 minutesManual VI §§30–32 with the Guide (Nibbāna: definition, analysis, summary)

In brief

  • Nibbāna is the one unconditioned reality: the suttas call it unborn and unmade, and the Dhammasaṅgaṇī counts a single unconditioned element, not several.
  • It is one by its own nature, and is spoken of as two only by reference to the aggregates: with residue while an arahant lives, without residue when the aggregates end.
  • Its names void, signless and desireless describe Nibbāna itself; the three doors of insight with the same names are ways of contemplating the conditioned that lead there.
  • The Visuddhimagga argues that it is not mere absence or mere destruction: the suttas' "destruction of greed" is a figure for that on which the destruction depends.
  • Only a few moments of mind take it as object: change-of-lineage at the threshold, the four paths and four fruitions, and the reviewing of the noble ones (the Manual's rule would also allow the direct knowledges); worldlings know of it only by inference.
  • Nibbāna produces nothing in the way causes produce, but it conditions those cittas in three ways: as object, as predominant object and as object decisive support.

The analysis so far has taken things apart: a person into mind and matter, and what was left over into names. One ultimate reality remains that it does not take apart, because it has no parts and no conditions: nibbānanibbāna the unconditioned: the ending of craving; object of path and fruition. The Manual gives it three short paragraphs at the end of the chapter on matter (VI §§30–32). This reading opens them out, with the suttas behind them and the commentaries that argued over them.

One, and unmade

In the Udāna the Buddha describes a sphere in which there is no earth, water, fire or air, none of the formless spheres, neither this world nor another; no coming or going, no arising or passing away; without support, not continuing; and that, he says, is the end of suffering (Ud 8.1). A little later comes the passage most quoted: there is something not born, not become, not made, not conditioned; if there were not, no escape from what is born, become, made and conditioned would be known (Ud 8.3, repeated in It 43). The Itivuttaka’s verse adds that it is peaceful, stable, and beyond the reach of reasoning.

Elsewhere the definition is short. Asked what Nibbāna is, Sāriputta answers: the destruction of greed, of hatred, of delusion (SN 38.1). A whole collection of short suttas asks what the unconditioned is and gives the same answer, then repeats it under thirty-two other names: the far shore, the island, the refuge, the deathless, the peaceful, the subtle, and more (SN 43).

The Abhidhamma takes all of this as one thing. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī, sorting every dhamma into the conditioned and the unconditioned, puts on the second side a single entry, the unconditioned element, asaṅkhata-dhātuasaṅkhata-dhātu · unconditioned element Nibbāna, the one element not made by conditions (Dhs §§1090, 1092). It is indeterminate, neither wholesome nor unwholesome (Dhs §583). The Manual states it in a line: Nibbāna is supramundane, to be realised by the knowledge of the four paths, and the object of paths and fruits (VI §30). Of the four ultimate realities it is the only one not made by conditions; citta, its factors and matter all are (Guide to I §2).

The word itself has two explanations. Its plain sense is a fire going out, the fires of greed, hatred and delusion. The Manual gives another, which the commentators prefer: Nibbāna is a departure (nikkhanta) from craving, which entangles (vāna) and binds one life to the next (VI §30 with the Guide).

What it is like, if it is like anything

The commentaries describe every dhamma by four marks: characteristic, function, manifestation and proximate cause. For Nibbāna the Visuddhimagga gives the first three and names no proximate cause; nothing causes it. Its characteristic is peace, santisanti · peace peace; the characteristic of Nibbāna (Vism XVI §556). Its function is not to pass away, or, in a second reading, to comfort. It shows itself as the signless, or as freedom from proliferation, nippapañcanippapañca · non-proliferation freedom from proliferation, the spreading out of craving, conceit and views (the commentaries' gloss); a manifestation of Nibbāna (Vism XVI §556) and a sutta name of it (SN 43.23) (Vism XVI §556).

The Manual’s closing verse gathers its names: a footing (pada), not passing away (accuta), endless, unconditioned, unsurpassed (VI §32). Most are negations.

Two elements, one Nibbāna

The Itivuttaka speaks of two Nibbāna elements (It 44). One is the element with residue, sa-upādisesasa-upādisesa · with residue the Nibbāna element with residue: the defilements ended while an arahant's aggregates remain (It 44; VI §31): in a living arahant, greed, hatred and delusion are destroyed, yet the five faculties remain, and he still meets the agreeable and the disagreeable. The other is the element without residue, anupādisesaanupādisesa · without residue the Nibbāna element without residue: named by the absence of aggregates after an arahant's last citta (It 44; VI §31 with the Guide; Vism XVI §561): for that same arahant, everything felt, no longer delighted in, will grow cool right here. The verse calls the first visible in this life and the second what comes after, where all becoming stops.

The Manual keeps the two but insists they are one thing seen two ways. By its own nature Nibbāna is single; it is twofold only by way of a ground for naming it (kāraṇapariyāyena, VI §31). The basis, the Guide explains, is whether the aggregates are still there. The Visuddhimagga says the same: the element with residue is named by reference to the residue still present, and the element without residue by reference to its absence once the last citta has passed (Vism XVI §561). Nibbāna does not change when an arahant dies; only the aggregates to name it by are gone.

Three names, and three doors

By aspect, the Manual says, Nibbāna is threefold: void, suññatasuññata · void void; an aspect of Nibbāna itself (VI §31), and, with signless and desireless, one of the three emancipations named after the insight that leads to the path, signless, animittaanimitta · signless without sign; an aspect of Nibbāna (VI §31), and the emancipation reached through the contemplation of impermanence, and desireless, appaṇihitaappaṇihita · desireless without hankering; an aspect of Nibbāna (VI §31), and the emancipation reached through the contemplation of suffering (VI §31). The Vibhāvinī gives two reasons for each name. Nibbāna is empty of greed, hatred and delusion, has none of their marks and none of their longing; and, read more widely, it is empty of every formation, bears none of their marks, and is nothing craving can long for (Vibhāvinī, ch. 6, CS §64; Guide to VI §31).

The same three words return in the chapter on insight with a different job, and the two uses are easy to blur. There they name doors: ways of contemplating the conditioned that lead out of it. Contemplating non-self is the door of the void, impermanence the door of the signless, suffering the door of the desireless (IX §35). A path is named after the door by which it was reached (IX §36). But by its object and its own quality every path and every fruition has all three names, because the Nibbāna they take is void, signless and desireless at once (IX §37 with the Guide). The doors are three; what they open on is one. Ten knowledges follows the doors further.

Not nothing, and not mere ending

Perhaps Nibbāna is just the absence of everything conditioned. The Visuddhimagga answers this in a short debate, the tradition’s main defence of Nibbāna as a reality (Vism XVI §§557–561).

It cannot be found, says the objector, like the horn of a hare. It can, comes the reply, by the right means, the practice that fits it, just as another person’s supramundane mind is found by the knowledge that reads minds. What the foolish cannot find is not therefore absent (§557). If it did not exist, right practice would bear no fruit (§558). Nor is it the absence of the aggregates. Past and future aggregates are already absent, yet that is not Nibbāna; present ones cannot simply be absent; and the element with residue is reached while the aggregates are present (§558).

Then the strongest objection: the sutta itself calls Nibbāna the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion. The reply is that arahantship is described with the same words (SN 38.2), and arahantship is not mere destruction either. Worse, a destruction is an event. If Nibbāna were one, it would last only a while, would bear the mark of the conditioned, and could be reached without right effort. The path, which does the destroying, would itself be Nibbāna. The sutta’s word is therefore a figure: Nibbāna is called destruction because it is the decisive support for the destruction, the ceasing by which defilements never arise again (§559). Last, the path reaches Nibbāna but does not make it. What the path does not make has no origin, and what has no origin does not age or die (§560).

Later manuals add an argument from the mind that knows it. Buddhadatta’s Abhidhammāvatāra asks what change-of-lineage, cleansing and the path would take as object if Nibbāna were the destruction of defilements. At change-of-lineage they are yet to be destroyed; at the path they are being destroyed; only at fruition have they been destroyed. On that view only fruition would have an object (Abhidhammāvatāra, ch. 11, after v. 770). The Vibhāvinī, the standard commentary on the Manual, puts the point as an inference. Knowledge that takes conditioned things or concepts as object cannot cut off defilements; yet defilements are cut off; so there is a dhamma unlike both, the object of paths and fruits, and that is Nibbāna (Vibhāvinī on VI §30).

The Milindapañha makes the same case by simile. Space and Nibbāna, Nāgasena says, are the two things not born of kamma, of a cause or of season. A path to realise Nibbāna can be taught, but no cause to produce it, as a man can walk to the Himalaya but cannot carry it home. And it exists though it cannot be shown, like the wind, which no one can show by colour or shape (Milindapañha, Akammajādipañha).noteWhere teachers differ. How many unconditioneds? The Theravāda counts one. The Kathāvatthu rejects those who would add others, among them space, dependent arising, the attainment of cessation and two kinds of cessation: a second unconditioned would mean two deathless refuges with a boundary between them (Kv II.11, VI.1–6, XIX.3–5). It names no school; its commentary does. Another school entirely, the Sarvāstivāda, counted three unconditioned dharmas: space, cessation through discernment, which is nirvāṇa, and cessation without discernment, the mere non-arising of what lacks its conditions. Destruction itself, or what destruction depends on? SN 38.1 says plainly that Nibbāna is the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion; the Visuddhimagga reads this as a figure (Vism XVI §559). Read literally, the sutta would make Nibbāna the extinction of the fires in a living person, a state of a life, not a further element. The commentaries’ answer is the debate above. The Udāna’s “without object” (anārammaṇa, Ud 8.1) is read in a similar split: its commentary takes it to mean that, unlike feeling and the other mental states, Nibbāna takes no object (Udāna commentary, CS §71; so too Kv XIX.6). Read the other way, as “not an object for the mind”, it would contradict the Abhidhamma, for which Nibbāna is the object of path and fruit.

Outside time, outside the aggregates

Every conditioned dhamma has a moment of arising, a moment of standing and a moment of passing. Nibbāna has none of these, and so it is not past, present or future. The Manual allows mind-door cittas objects that are free of time (kālavimutta, III §17), and the Guide names them: Nibbāna and concepts. Nibbāna stands outside time because what it is has no arising, change or ceasing; a concept stands outside time because it has no nature of its own to arise (Guide to III §17).

For the same reason it stands outside the five aggregates, which are sorted as past, present and future, near and far, and the rest (VII §40). Among the twelve bases it belongs to the base of mental objects (VII §39). It is not a place: the Milindapañha says there is no spot where it is stored (Nibbānasannihitapañha). And it is not a consciousness, for it is not citta.

The moments that take it

Most cittas can never take Nibbāna as object: not the unwholesome, not the beautiful sense-sphere cittas without knowledge, for which it is too pure and too deep (III §18 with the Guide), not the sublime cittas of the jhānas (Table 3.5), and not insight, which comprehends only the formations of the three planes (IX §32). The moments that can are few (the Manual’s rule for the direct knowledges, III §18, would add them; the Visuddhimagga’s list of their objects, XIII §§416–419, does not), and each has its place in a process.

Change-of-lineage, gotrabhūgotrabhū · change-of-lineage the javana that leaves the sense-sphere lineage for jhāna, or turns to Nibbāna, is the first. It is a sense-sphere wholesome citta with knowledge, at the end of the insight before the first path (Guide to III §18). It is the first moment to advert to Nibbāna, and it removes nothing (IX §34 with the Guide). Before the three higher paths the same moment is called cleansing, vodānavodāna · cleansing the name of the change-of-lineage javana in the processes of the three higher paths, because the person already belongs to the noble lineage.

The four paths and four fruitions come next: the supramundane cittas, whose only object is Nibbāna (III §18; VI §30). Counted by jhāna, they are forty (I §§30–32).

Reviewing, paccavekkhaṇapaccavekkhaṇa · reviewing after attainment, knowledge looking back on path, fruition and Nibbāna, and usually, not always, on the defilements abandoned and those that remain, follows each path, in ordinary mind-door processes. The disciple reviews the path, the fruition and Nibbāna, and usually the defilements abandoned and those that remain (IX §34 with the Guide). Each process opens with mind-door adverting and runs on sense-sphere javanas with knowledge: wholesome ones for trainees, functional ones for the arahant (Guide to III §18). The Paṭṭhāna names each of these moments as taking Nibbāna as object (Paṭṭhāna, Kusalattika, question chapter, CS §§410–411).

Last, the attainment of fruition, phalasamāpattiphalasamāpatti · attainment of fruition a noble one's absorption in their own fruition, with Nibbāna as object, in which a noble person enters his own fruition again, with Nibbāna as object, to dwell in the happiness of Nibbāna in this very life (IX §42 with the Guide). Here no change-of-lineage comes before the fruition: the moments just before it are conformity, still taking formations, and the Visuddhimagga says that in this process “change-of-lineage” is to be understood as conformity (Vism XXIII §§863, 866). The process of the path is described in Other processes, and who reaches which fruition in Four noble persons.

What about everyone else? The Vibhāvinī draws the line. That Nibbāna is to be realised by the four paths shows it is known by direct perception to the noble ones, each at their level; that it is the object of paths and fruits shows that the good worldling, who has not reached a path, can establish it only by inference, by the argument given above (Vibhāvinī on VI §30). A worldling can still think about Nibbāna, but outside change-of-lineage that thought cannot have Nibbāna itself as its object; presumably it takes the idea of it. That is our inference from III §18 and its Guide, not something the Manual says.

What it gives

Nibbāna is a condition, and never conditioned (Guide to VIII §1). It produces nothing, as a seed produces a sprout; it conditions only by being taken as object, in three forms. It is an object condition for every citta that takes it (VIII §17). For the cittas that take it with weight, those of change-of-lineage and cleansing, path, fruition and reviewing, it is also object predominance, ārammaṇādhipatiadhipati · predominance the one factor leading a citta: desire, energy, citta or investigation; or, as object predominance, an object held in high regard, the object that dominates the mind attending to it (VIII §19), and object decisive support, the object that, prominent, gives strong support to the mind that takes it (VIII §17; Table 8.3). Mind-door adverting takes it only as plain object (Paṭṭhāna CS §§410, 416). It is not prenascent like a visible form, nor a natural decisive support like past faith (Table 8.3).

This is also why the Abhidhamma counts Nibbāna as nāma, though it is no mind. Citta and its factors are nāma because they bend toward an object; Nibbāna is nāma only because it makes the supramundane cittas bend toward itself, as their predominant object (Guide to VIII §29).

The Manual’s account ends there. It says what Nibbāna is, how it differs from everything else, and which minds can know it; it does not describe what it is like to know it, and makes no promise about who will. How insight ripens toward it is the subject of The tree of purification.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The suttas say that Nibbāna is the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion; the Visuddhimagga says that this is a figure of speech, and that Nibbāna is rather that on account of which they are destroyed. What would be lost if the sutta phrase were taken literally?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Taken literally, the phrase makes Nibbāna an event: something that happens at a moment and is over. The Visuddhimagga draws out what follows. Arahantship is described with the same words, so it too would be only destruction; the path, which does the destroying, would itself be Nibbāna; and Nibbāna would last only a while and bear the mark of the conditioned. The commentators keep the sutta phrase, since a destruction does happen at the path, but they read it as naming Nibbāna by what it makes possible. Whether that reading is needed is a question the commentaries settle and the suttas leave open. On either reading, the ending of greed, hatred and delusion is what the word points to in a life.
