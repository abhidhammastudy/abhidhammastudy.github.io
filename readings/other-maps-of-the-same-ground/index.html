Other maps of the same ground

Other Indian schools also took experience apart into elements, and reached different counts. Comparing the maps shows which choices were open, and why the Theravāda made the ones it did.

About 7 minutesManual Introduction (other schools)

In brief

  • Other Indian Buddhist schools also took experience apart into ultimate elements, and their lists reached different counts from the Theravāda's.
  • Counted the same way, the Theravāda has eighty-two elements, the northern Sarvāstivāda seventy-five, and the later Yogācāra a hundred.
  • The northern lists add formations apart from mind, such as possession and words; the Theravāda treats these as concepts, as aspects of other things, or as unneeded.
  • The Sarvāstivāda held that things exist in past, present and future; for the Theravāda only present dhammas exist, and a past deed conditions without existing now.
  • Yogācāra placed a storehouse consciousness beneath the mind; the Theravāda's life-continuum (bhavaṅga) stores nothing, and continuity is carried by conditions alone.
  • The Theravāda list is the most economical, with a single unconditioned element, Nibbāna, and the comparison shows each of its choices as a choice.

The Theravāda was not the only school to build an Abhidhamma. In northern India the Sarvāstivāda developed its own seven books, and later Vasubandhu summarised its system, often critically, in the Abhidharmakośa, a handbook much as Anuruddha’s Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha, the text of the Manual, would later be for the Theravāda. The Yogācāra school took the method further still. All three began from the same early lists and the same aim. They reached different maps.

Counting the same way

To compare the maps fairly, count the same way. If citta is counted as one element, the Theravāda list has eighty-two items in four categories: one citta, fifty-two mental factors, twenty-eight kinds of matter, and Nibbāna.

The Sarvāstivāda counted seventy-five: eleven kinds of matter, one mind, forty-six mental factors, fourteen “formations dissociated from mind”, and three unconditioned dharmas. The Yogācāra counted a hundred: eight consciousnesses, fifty-one mental factors, eleven kinds of matter, twenty-four dissociated formations, and six unconditioned.noteThese counts are the classical summaries. Within each school, earlier texts give variant lists; the plate notes where a figure rests on secondary sources.

The differences are not arbitrary. Each marks a decision about what is real.

A fifth category

The northern lists have a category the Theravāda lacks: formations dissociated from mind. They include possession, the force that makes a dharma belong to a particular stream; the life force; the marks of arising, enduring, decaying and ceasing; and words, phrases and syllables.

The Theravāda treats most of these differently. Words and names are concepts, paññattipaññatti · concept a concept: a name, or what a name refers to, such as “chariot”, not realities. The marks of arising and decay are aspects of matter and mind, not separate things. Life is a faculty, one in matter and one in mind. Possession it does not need, because a stream is held together by conditions alone.

Time

The Sarvāstivāda’s name means “the doctrine that all exists”: its teachers held that dharmas exist in the past, present and future, and that only their activity is present. This explained how a past deed could bring a result, and how memory could know the past.

The Kathāvatthu argues against this at length. For the Theravāda, of conditioned dhammas only present ones exist. Past ones have ceased and future ones have not arisen (Kv I.6). Yet both can be objects of a present citta (Kv IX.6), and a past volition still conditions its result, as asynchronous kamma condition (VIII §14, Guide). A past kamma conditions its result without existing now, as a kamma condition across time; a memory is a present citta taking a past dhamma as its object.

The base of mind

Yogācāra added two consciousnesses below the six of the senses: a storehouse consciousness, ālaya-vijñāna, which carries the seeds of past action from moment to moment and life to life, and a defiled mind, manas, which mistakes the storehouse for a self.

The Theravāda’s bhavaṅgabhavaṅga · life-continuum the resultant citta, of the same kind as rebirth-linking, that runs between processes is often compared to it. Both explain continuity when no active process is running. The difference is instructive. For the Theravāda the bhavaṅga is a series of momentary resultant cittas and stores nothing; continuity is carried by conditions, not by a container.

The unconditioned

The Sarvāstivāda counted three unconditioned dharmas: space, and two kinds of cessation. Yogācāra counted six. The Theravāda counts one: nibbānanibbāna the unconditioned: the ending of craving; object of path and fruition. Space, for it, is in one sense a feature of matter, the gap that delimits groups (VI §4); the space left when a kasiṇa is removed, and “unentangled” space, are mere concepts (Kathāvatthu commentary on Kv VI.6). The Milindapañha pairs space with Nibbāna as the two things not born of kamma, cause or season, but the Kathāvatthu rejects the view that space is unconditioned (Kv VI.6).

That single entry is characteristic. The Theravāda list is the most economical of the three. It admits as real only what it cannot reduce further, and it reduces almost everything. Laid beside the others, its choices become visible as choices, and the reader can ask of each one what it was meant to protect.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The Sarvāstivāda held that dhammas exist in past, present and future; the Theravāda held that only present dhammas exist. Which view fits better with your experience of memory?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Memory seems to reach the past directly, which is what the Sarvāstivāda argued. The Theravāda answers that a memory is a present citta taking a past dhamma as its object; the past dhamma does not exist, but it can still be taken as an object, just as a concept, which has no existence of its own, can be. Both views were trying to explain how the past can condition the present without making it permanent.
