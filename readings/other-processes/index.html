Other processes

The mind door, the last process of a life, absorption, the path, fruition and cessation: how the building blocks of the mango moment make every other kind of process.

About 13 minutes

In brief

  • The same few kinds of mind-moment that make up one act of seeing also make up remembering, dying, deep meditation, awakening and the stopping of mind.
  • Remembering needs no sense organ: at the mind door the mind turns to the object, seven moments of action (javana) run, and two may register it.
  • Following Ledi Sayadaw, seeing delivers only a colour; later mind-door processes add the thing and then its name, so the "I" in "I see" comes late.
  • A life ends with a weak process of five javanas and death consciousness; on the Abhidhamma's account rebirth-linking follows with no gap, except for an arahant.
  • In absorption and on the path, a few preparatory moments give way to deep concentration, or to the path moment that the tradition holds irreversible.
  • The order of moments stays the same; what changes is which kinds of mind fill the places of action, and how many times they run.

The mango reading followed one process: a strong visible object at the eye door, seventeen moments, seven javanas. The same few building blocks make every other process the Abhidhamma describes: adverting, javana, registration, and the life-continuum before and after. This reading counts the others out one by one. In every strip below, one cell is one mind-moment, except the dotted gap in the last.

At the mind door

The next day the man remembers the mango. Nothing strikes his eye. The object is the mango as remembered: a past thing, now present to the mind. It arrives at the sixth door, the mind door. There is no sense organ for it. On the Theravāda account, the mind door is the resting stream itself, the life-continuum, at the moment an object disturbs it.noteThe commentaries do give this mind a physical base, the heart-base, which the canonical Abhidhamma does not name (Guide to III §20). Which moment of the life-continuum counts as the door is disputed: the Vibhāvinī says the arrest, Ledi Sayadaw the whole stream (Guide to III §12). What follows is a mind-door process, manodvāra-vīthimanodvāra-vīthi · mind-door process a process at the mind door alone: adverting, seven javanas, and two registrations when the object is clear and the conditions for registration hold. Counted out, it goes like this.

  • Moment 1. The life-continuum vibrates (bhavaṅga-calanabhavaṅga-calana · vibration of the life-continuum the life-continuum moment that is shaken when an object comes into range; the next is the arrest). The remembered mango has come into range.
  • Moment 2. The life-continuum is arrested (bhavaṅgupacchedabhavaṅgupaccheda · arrest of the life-continuum the last life-continuum moment before a process; after it the stream stops and adverting arises). This is the last moment of the stream.
  • Moment 3. Mind-door adverting, manodvārāvajjanamanodvārāvajjana · mind-door adverting citta 29: opens mind-door processes, and does determining in five-door ones, turns to the object. It is the same functional citta that did the determining in the mango process. Here it has the job of adverting, and it makes no kamma.
  • Moments 4 to 10. Seven javanas run. “Javana” names a place in the process, the one after adverting. Usually kamma is made there, and so it is here: the seven are cittas of one kind, wholesome or unwholesome. He may want another mango, or be glad he shared it. In an arahant the same places hold functional cittas, which make no kamma (Guide to III §13). And the four fruitions, met below, are the only results that ever stand in a javana place (III §9).
  • Moments 11 and 12. Two moments of registration, tadārammaṇatadārammaṇa · registration resultant cittas that hold the javanas' object after a strong object, hold the object once more.

Then the life-continuum flows again. Counting from the vibration, that is twelve moments. Two are the life-continuum being disturbed; the other ten are the process proper, ten cittas doing three kinds of work: one adverting, seven javanas, two registrations (IV §12–13).

Three things are missing compared with the mango. There is no sense consciousness, because nothing is seen or heard. There is no receiving, investigating or determining, because those cittas handle what a sense has delivered. And there is no past moment counted before the vibration. At a sense door the object is matter, and the commentaries explain the past moment by saying matter needs one moment to reach full strength. A memory, a plan or a concept is not present matter, so nothing needs to be waited for.noteThe reason is Ledi Sayadaw’s, in his Paramatthadīpanī: objects of any time, or of no time, come into range at the mind door, so no rule about moments already past applies there.

A mind-door object can be anything: a sight or sound just past, something that happened years ago, something expected, a concept, a name, another citta. A sense object is graded by how many of its seventeen moments are left when it strikes, as When the object is weak tells. A mind-door object is graded only by how plainly it appears. A clear object, vibhūtavibhūta · avibhūta · clear and obscure an object's strength at the mind door; only a clear one gets registration, gets the full course above. With an obscure one, avibhūtavibhūta · avibhūta · clear and obscure an object's strength at the mind door; only a clear one gets registration, the javanas still run seven times, but no registration follows and the mind sinks straight back into the life-continuum (IV §12).

Registration has conditions of its own, whatever the door. In plain words: it belongs to everyday experience of everyday things, and nowhere else. Exactly: it follows only sense-sphere javanas, only in beings of the sense sphere, only when the object is itself a sense-sphere thing, and only with a clear or very great object (IV §19–20). When one of these fails, the process ends at the last javana.noteThe Manual fixes registration at two moments or none. The Visuddhimagga (XIV) says “twice or once”, and reports elsewhere that the Majjhima commentary allowed a single moment. The later tradition settled on two.

How a colour becomes a mango

The eye-door process of the mango reading knew a colour and nothing else. It did not know “mango”. That is added afterwards, at the mind door, by processes of exactly the kind just counted.

The Manual’s own text does not describe them. The Guide does: Bhikkhu Bodhi’s explanatory guide, printed with the Manual in the same book (Guide to IV §12). It follows the Burmese teacher Ledi Sayadaw. After the eye-door process ends, seven mind-door processes follow it in this order:

  1. One repeats the colour just past. The eye-door process has dropped it; the mind door takes it up again.
  2. One grasps it as a whole. The separate glimpses are put together, the way a torch whirled in the dark looks like a circle of fire.
  3. One recognises the colour.
  4. One grasps the thing: a shape, an object.
  5. One recognises the thing.
  6. One grasps the name.
  7. One recognises the name: “mango”.

Together they may run to many thousands of processes. Ledi Sayadaw’s image for this is a bell. Struck once, it goes on sounding; in the same way one touch of a colour at the eye sets off process after process at the mind door.noteLedi Sayadaw’s own Paramatthadīpanī gives both accounts: these seven processes, and also, more briefly, four kinds of grasping that follow each sense door: of the past object, of the whole, of the thing and of the name. The Guide follows the fuller list.

Only then is a mango known. Only in the recognising processes, Ledi Sayadaw holds, does one know “I see” anything: first the colour, then the thing, then its name. This is an analysis of recognition, not a description of what it feels like. On this account the “I” in “I see” is a name put together late in the chain, not a seer standing behind the seeing.noteA comparison with another school: the Buddhist logicians Dignāga and Dharmakīrti also held that perception proper is free of conceptual construction, and that names and kinds are added afterwards by thought. Ledi Sayadaw’s chain draws a similar line in the Abhidhamma’s own terms, with the name coming last.

The last process

Every life has one process of its own kind, the last. At the end of a life an object appears to the dying mind: the deed itself, recalled or re-lived, or a sign of it, or a sign of the world to come (V §35–36). The last process takes that object. It may run at the mind door or at a sense door, and it is weak. Its javanas run five times, not seven (IV §21; V §38). They have no rebirth-producing power of their own; they are the channel for the past kamma now set to produce the next birth (Guide to V §37). If that kamma is a death-proximate one, it was done or recalled just before this process, which is why, in Buddhist countries, the dying are customarily reminded of their good deeds (Guide to V §19).

After the javanas, the last citta of the life arises: death consciousness, cuticuti · death consciousness the last citta of a life, of the same kind as its life-continuum. It is not a process citta. It is the life-continuum’s own kind of citta, doing its final job. It may follow the fifth javana directly, or two moments of registration, or some moments of life-continuum (Guide to V §37). On the Abhidhamma’s account, when it ceases, rebirth-linking, paṭisandhipaṭisandhi · rebirth-linking the first citta of a life: a resultant that links it to the last, arises with no gap as the first citta of the next life, and takes the object of that last process. The one exception is an arahant: their death consciousness is followed by no further citta at all. This is the final passing away (Guide to V §40; Guide to VIII §13). The story is told in full in Kamma, death and rebirth.

Absorption

The Abhidhamma sorts cittas by sphere. The sense sphere is our everyday world and the cittas that belong to it: seeing, wanting, remembering a mango. The fine-material and immaterial spheres are the levels of deep meditation, and the worlds that match them. So far every javana in this reading has been a citta of the sense sphere. In a meditator the mind-door process can carry something more: jhānajhāna · absorption deep concentration fixed on one object: five fine-material levels, then four immaterial, absorption.

The process begins as before. The object is a meditation sign, nimittanimitta · meditation sign the preliminary, learning or counterpart image of the meditation object, such as the bright image of a disc that the meditator has been attending to. The life-continuum vibrates and is arrested, and mind-door adverting turns to the sign. Then the javanas run, but they are not seven of one kind. The first few are sense-sphere cittas with knowledge, and each has a name for what it does (IV §14):

  • Preliminary, parikammaparikamma · preliminary the first stage of concentration; also the first javana leading into absorption: it prepares the mind for absorption.
  • Access, upacāraupacāra · access concentration just short of jhāna; also a javana in the run-up to absorption: it comes close to absorption.
  • Conformity, anulomaanuloma · conformity the javana that fits the mind to what follows, just before change-of-lineage: it fits the mind both to the moments before it and to the absorption about to come.
  • Change-of-lineage, gotrabhūgotrabhū · change-of-lineage the javana that leaves the sense-sphere lineage for jhāna, or turns to Nibbāna: the last sense-sphere javana. With it the mind leaves the lineage of the sense sphere and takes on that of the higher sphere.

Then comes the next javana, and it is not a sense-sphere citta at all. It is the first-jhāna citta of the fine-material sphere: absorption itself, appanāappanā · absorption the jhāna or path javana itself, after access; the third stage of concentration.

How many preparatory javanas run depends on the meditator’s faculties; Ledi Sayadaw speaks of dull or keen wisdom. In one whose faculties are slow (the Guide’s “average”), mandatikkha · manda · keen and slow of a meditator's faculties: a keen one needs three preparatory javanas before absorption or path, a slow one four, all four arise, and absorption is the fifth javana. In one whose faculties are keen, tikkhatikkha · manda · keen and slow of a meditator's faculties: a keen one needs three preparatory javanas before absorption or path, a slow one four, the preliminary moment is not needed: access, conformity and change-of-lineage are enough, and absorption is the fourth. It is never earlier, and never later. The commentaries give a reason for each side. It cannot come earlier, say the Vibhāvinī and Ledi Sayadaw, because change-of-lineage needs conformity javanas before it that have been strengthened by repetition. It cannot come later, says the Visuddhimagga (IV), because by the sixth or seventh place a run of javanas is already close to falling back into the life-continuum. It illustrates this, without proving it, with a man who runs to the edge of a cliff and cannot stop there.noteThe Visuddhimagga records the elder Godatta’s view that absorption could come at the sixth or seventh javana, and rejects it.

Three more rules belong here. The preparatory javanas and the absorption share one feeling: joyful javanas lead into a joyful absorption, equanimous ones into an equanimous one (IV §15). No registration follows absorption, and its object is never obscure (IV §14). And the first time a meditator reaches a jhāna, the absorption citta arises once and the mind sinks back into the life-continuum. With practice the same citta can be made to repeat without any fixed limit, for as long as the meditator has resolved, for hours or even days (IV §22 and its Guide).

Direct knowledge runs the same way. The Manual counts five mundane kinds, abhiññāabhiññā · direct knowledge a mundane power worked from the fifth jhāna, such as the divine eye or recalling past lives; its citta runs for one moment: supernormal powers, the divine ear, knowing the minds of others, recalling past lives, and the divine eye (IX §21). All of them are worked from the fifth jhāna (IX §21), by one who, the commentaries add, has mastered it.noteThe Abhidhamma counts five fine-material jhānas where the suttas count four, by taking in two steps what the suttas pass in one. Its fifth jhāna is the suttas’ fourth. After three or four preparatory javanas, one moment of the fifth-jhāna citta does the work of direct knowledge, and only one, however practised the meditator is (IV §14, §22).

The path

The noble ones, ariyaariya · noble one anyone who has reached a path or fruition, from stream-entry up, are those who have seen Nibbāna for themselves. Anyone who has not is a worldling, puthujjanaputhujjana · worldling an ordinary person who has reached no path. On the Theravāda account, Nibbāna is the one unconditioned reality: it does not arise from conditions or pass away. It is an “object” only in the sense that the mind can take it up; it does not strike a door as a colour does. There are four paths, one for each stage of awakening, and here is the first, the moment a worldling becomes a noble one. The tree of purification tells how insight ripens up to this point; here is the process itself. It has the same shape as absorption, and the path is itself an absorption, a supramundane one (IV §14; IX §34).

It runs at the mind door. The preparatory javanas are moments of insight. They take some conditioned thing as their object and see it as impermanent, as suffering or as non-self. They are named as before: preliminary, access, conformity. Then comes change-of-lineage, and here the name means more. It is still a sense-sphere citta, but it drops the conditioned object and, for the first time, takes Nibbāna as its object. With it the lineage of the worldling is left for that of the noble ones.noteIn the processes of the three higher paths the person is already noble, so the same javana is called cleansing, vodānavodāna · cleansing the name of the change-of-lineage javana in the processes of the three higher paths, and not change-of-lineage (Guide to IX §34).

Change-of-lineage sees Nibbāna but removes nothing. The next citta does: the path, maggamagga · path (citta) a supramundane citta that arises once, cutting defilements, with Nibbāna as object. It arises for one moment only, and in that one moment it does four things at once: it fully understands suffering, abandons craving, its origin, realises Nibbāna, its cessation, and develops the path itself (IX §34 and its Guide). It is said to abandon, for good, certain defilements, the states that stain the mind. The first path removes wrong view and doubt, and with them every defilement strong enough to lead to a woeful rebirth (IX §38 and its Guide); what remains of greed, hatred and delusion falls to the later paths. Straight after it comes its result, fruition, phalaphala · fruition the supramundane resultant citta that follows its path, also taking Nibbāna as object, with the same object. So in a path process the javana places hold not only kamma but its result.

Path-kamma differs in kind from all other kamma. Its result is its own fruition (Guide to I §§26–28), and it builds no rebirth. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī calls the path a jhāna that tears down, and its commentary explains: it pulls down the deaths and rebirths that other kamma has built up (Atthasālinī; commentarial). Compare ordinary kamma, whose result may ripen lives later: here the result follows at once, in the same process.noteThe tradition also recognises noble ones who reached the path by insight without jhāna, of “dry insight” (Visuddhimagga XXIII). How much concentration the path needs is a question on which modern teachers differ.

The javana counts here come from the Guide to IV §22 and the Manual’s Table 4.4; that one adverting can carry seven javanas at most is the Visuddhimagga’s rule (XXII). The path process uses all seven. With slow faculties there are four preparatory javanas, one path and two fruitions. With keen faculties there are three preparatory javanas, one path and three fruitions. Then the life-continuum resumes. The process is short, and the tradition holds the change irreversible.

Fruition again

Afterwards come processes of reviewing, paccavekkhaṇapaccavekkhaṇa · reviewing after attainment, knowledge looking back on path, fruition and Nibbāna, and usually, not always, on the defilements abandoned and those that remain (IX §34 and its Guide). Each is an ordinary mind-door process, adverting and then seven sense-sphere javanas with knowledge. One reviews the path, one the fruition, one Nibbāna, and usually, not always, the defilements abandoned and those that remain (an arahant has none remaining). When the object is the path, the fruition or Nibbāna, no registration follows, since none of these is a sense-sphere thing (IV §19–20).

A noble person can also return to the fruition they have reached and stay in it, as a meditator stays in jhāna: the attainment of fruition, phalasamāpattiphalasamāpatti · attainment of fruition a noble one's absorption in their own fruition, with Nibbāna as object (IX §42). Each noble one enters only the fruition of their own stage (IX §42 and its Guide). They resolve on it and develop insight again. A process then runs with a few javanas of insight, all of them called conformity here (Visuddhimagga XXIII). After them come fruition cittas, taking Nibbāna as object, one after another with no fixed number, for as long as was resolved (IV §22).

Cessation

One attainment goes further than any jhāna. The attainment of cessation, nirodha-samāpattinirodha-samāpatti · attainment of cessation the temporary stopping of citta and mental factors, open only to non-returners and arahants with all eight jhānas, is described as one in which the stream of cittas stops for a time (IX §43–44). The Theravāda Abhidhamma holds that in it there is no citta at all, not even the life-continuum.noteThis is not the path-and-fruition moment that some modern insight teachers also call “cessation”. That moment has Nibbāna as its object; in the attainment of cessation there is no citta to take any object.

Who can enter it. Only a non-returner or an arahant who has mastered all eight absorptions, the four fine-material (five in the Abhidhamma’s count) and the four immaterial, and only in the sense sphere or the fine-material worlds: not in the immaterial worlds, since the fine-material jhānas that lead up to it cannot arise there (Guide to IX §43). It needs both calm and insight at full strength. Stream-enterers and once-returners cannot enter it: they have not yet abandoned sensual lust, which obstructs concentration, so their calm is incomplete. Nor can noble ones of dry insight, who lack the absorptions. Calm alone, the Visuddhimagga says, stops at neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and insight alone at the attainment of fruition; only the two together reach cessation (Visuddhimagga XXIII; commentarial).

How it is entered. The meditator enters each absorption in turn, emerges, and sees its factors with insight as impermanent, suffering and non-self. After the third immaterial absorption they make certain resolves, among them how long the attainment is to last. Then they enter the fourth and highest immaterial absorption, the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. Its process begins like any absorption process, but the absorption javana runs just twice (IV §22). After the second, no citta arises.noteThe Visuddhimagga (XXIII) says the last absorption runs “one or two” times; the Manual says two. The detail of the resolves is also from the Visuddhimagga, and is told in Four noble persons.

How it is described. The tradition holds that for the time resolved, up to seven days, there is no citta and no mental factor, and so no matter produced by mind. The body remains, kept going by kamma, temperature and nutriment. The suttas mark the difference from death. In the Mahāvedalla Sutta (MN 43), one who has entered the cessation of perception and feeling differs from a dead body in this: life is not exhausted, heat has not subsided, and the faculties are clear. The Abhidhamma reads this as the stopping of all citta.noteCessation is not Nibbāna. The Visuddhimagga (XXIII) calls it neither conditioned nor unconditioned, since it has no nature of its own, and the Kathāvatthu (VI.5) rejects the view that it is unconditioned.

What emerges. The first citta to arise afterwards is one moment of fruition: the fruition of non-returning for a non-returner, the fruition of arahantship for an arahant (IV §22). No adverting comes before it, the commentators note. Then the life-continuum flows, and reviewing follows (IX §44).

How can a citta arise after days with none? The Paṭṭhāna’s answer, quoted in the Visuddhimagga (XXIII), is that the last citta of neither-perception-nor-non-perception before the gap is the proximity condition for the fruition after it. “Proximity” means that no citta comes between them (Guide to VIII §13), not that they are near in time; the Visuddhimagga (XVII) uses this very case to make that point.noteA comparison with other schools, which asked the same question: in Vasubandhu’s Abhidharmakośa the Vaibhāṣikas, much as the Paṭṭhāna does, let the citta before the gap condition the one after it, while the Sautrāntikas have mind return from seeds kept in the body. The Yogācāra puts a store-consciousness, ālaya-vijñāna, beneath the gap, so that some mind never stops.

One pattern

What these processes share is a rhythm: a disturbance, a turning, a run of javanas, and a return to the life-continuum. Remembering a mango, dying, entering deep concentration and awakening are all made of it. What changes is which cittas fill the javana places, and how many times they run.

  • A sense-sphere process, at any door: seven javanas of one kind, or six when the object is weak (IV §21).
  • The last process of a life: five (IV §21; V §38).
  • A first attainment of jhāna: three or four preparatory javanas, then absorption once (IV §14, §22).
  • Direct knowledge: three or four preparatory javanas, then direct knowledge once (IV §14, §22).
  • The path: three or four preparatory javanas, the path once, then three or two fruitions (Guide to IV §22; Table 4.4).
  • Jhāna or fruition entered as an attainment: no fixed limit (IV §22).
  • Entering cessation: the fourth immaterial absorption twice, then nothing (IV §22).

The Abhidhamma’s claim is not that awakening is ordinary, but that it happens in the same stream of cittas: in moments of the same kind, under the same fixed order.

Which of these processes, and which cittas in them, can arise for a given being in a given world is the subject of Who has which cittas.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Recognising a mango, entering jhāna, awakening and dying are all, on this account, runs of the same few kinds of moment in the same order. What, then, changes when a mind changes?

One way to see it, after you have tried

On this account, what changes is which cittas fill the javana places, and how many times they run. Even ordinary seeing shows it. The five-door process supplies only a present colour; the mind-door processes that follow add the thing and its name out of past perception, so a great deal of what we call seeing is recognition. From this one may draw a lesson the texts leave implicit: perception can be retrained, and can mislead. Training works in the same places: preparatory javanas give way to absorption, insight gives way to the path, and the tradition holds that change irreversible. The order of the moments stays as it was; what runs in it does not.
