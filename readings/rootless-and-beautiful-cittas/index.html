Rootless and beautiful cittas

The eighteen cittas with no root at all, the arahant's smile among them; the twenty-four beautiful cittas of the sense sphere and who has them; and, briefly, the jhāna, formless and supramundane cittas, with how eight became forty.

About 13 minutesManual I §§8–16 with the Guides and Tables 1.3–1.4 (the rootless and the sense-sphere beautiful cittas)

In brief

  • Eighteen cittas have no root, neither greed, hatred and delusion nor their opposites: fifteen are results of past kamma, such as bare seeing, and three are functional: two adverting cittas and the arahant's smile.
  • Sense-sphere results are not tied to the realm one lives in: the Atthasālinī says beings in hell had wholesome-resultant seeing when Mahāmoggallāna appeared there on a lotus.
  • The smile-producing citta is found only in arahants. The Atthasālinī says the Buddha smiled with it when recalling past lives (MN 81, MN 83); a sub-commentary doubted this, and the later teachers defended it.
  • The twenty-four sense-sphere beautiful cittas are eight wholesome, in worldlings and trainees; eight resultant, which carry any sense-sphere being reborn with roots; and eight functional, their counterparts in arahants.
  • The fine-material cittas are five jhānas in the Manual, but the Dhammasaṅgaṇī itself gives both a fourfold and a fivefold scheme; the four immaterial cittas are named by their objects.
  • The totals 89 and 121 are not in the canon: 89 is counted in the commentaries (the Atthasālinī, the Visuddhimagga, the Abhidhammāvatāra), 121 in the Abhidhammāvatāra and the Manual.

The twelve unwholesome cittas took the first part of the sense sphere one citta at a time. This reading goes on through the rest of the eighty-nine: the eighteen rootless cittas (I §§8–11), the twenty-four beautiful cittas of the sense sphere (I §§12–16), and, more briefly, the cittas of jhāna, of the formless attainments, and of the path (I §§18–32).

Eighteen without roots

A rootless citta, ahetukaahetuka · rootless a citta with none of the six roots; there are 18, has none of the six roots: not greed, hatred or delusion, and not their opposites either. Since roots give a citta stability, these are the weaker cittas (Guide to I §8). Ledi Sayadaw adds that the roots are great states, and do not arise in cittas born of weak kamma or with weak bases and functions (Paramatthadīpanī §38). Fifteen of the eighteen are results of kamma; three are functional.

Fifteen results. Seven come from unwholesome kamma and eight from wholesome kamma (I §§8–9). In each set are the five sense consciousnesses, seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting and touching; receiving, which takes up what was sensed; and investigating, which examines it. The word “unwholesome” in “unwholesome-resultant” qualifies the kamma, not the citta: the seeing of an ugly sight is not itself bad (Guide to I §8). Four of the sense consciousnesses always come with neutral feeling. Only touch is felt as pain or pleasure, because only there does the object strike the faculty hard (Guides to I §§8–9). The wholesome set has one more citta than the unwholesome: investigating with joy, which arises when the object is especially desirable (Guide to I §9).

These are the given part of experience, and they are not tied to the realm one lives in. The Atthasālinī gives an example (on Dhs §498). When Mahāmoggallāna made a lotus appear in hell and sat on it teaching, the beings there saw him with wholesome-resultant eye-consciousness and heard him with wholesome-resultant ear-consciousness. They smelled the sandalwood scent of his robe, tasted the rain he made fall on the fires, and felt a gentle breeze. Reborn there by unwholesome kamma, they still had old wholesome kamma to ripen.

One rootless result does more than register a sense. The wholesome-resultant investigating citta with neutral feeling can serve as rebirth-linking, life-continuum and death consciousness: for human beings born with a deep defect already in the kamma that made the birth, such as those born blind or deaf, and for some lower deities (V §11 with the Guide). Its unwholesome counterpart does the same for every being in the four woeful planes (Guide to III §9).

Three functional. A functional citta, kiriyakiriya · functional a citta that neither makes kamma nor results from it, such as adverting, is neither kamma nor its result. The Atthasālinī compares those that never reach the javana stage to wind-flowers, which set no fruit, and those that do to the flowers of a tree whose root has been cut (on Dhs §566). The first of the three is five-door adverting, which turns to an object at a sense door and does no seeing itself (Guide to I §10). The second is mind-door adverting, which does the same at the mind door and, in a five-door process, determines the object. The Atthasālinī calls it the great elephant, since there is no object it cannot take: even the Buddha’s unique knowledges take only what it has turned to first (on Dhs §574).

The arahant’s smile

The third functional citta is the smile-producing one, hasituppādahasituppāda · smile-producing an arahant's rootless functional citta that causes smiling at sense-sphere things, joyful and rootless (I §10). It has no root of any kind: not greed, and not non-greed either. The canon has it as one citta among the indeterminate, without the name (Dhs §568); the name, and the rule that only arahants have it, are the commentaries’ (Atthasālinī on Dhs §568; Vism XIV §454). Buddhas and paccekabuddhas are arahants too, so they have it (Guide to I §10).

What does it smile at? Small things, says the Visuddhimagga (Vism XIV §454); “sense-sphere phenomena”, says the Guide (Guide to I §10). The Atthasālinī gives examples at each door (on Dhs §568). An arahant sees a place fit for meditation; hears the din where goods are handed out and greedy people grab, and knows that craving is gone in him; shares good almsfood and reflects that he has kept the rule of sharing. At the mind door, it says, the Buddha smiled with this citta when he recalled what he had done as the potter’s friend Jotipāla (MN 81) and as King Maghadeva (MN 83): both suttas open with him smiling at a certain place. The recalling was the work of his knowledges; the smile came at their end.

A smile, in the Abhidhamma, is an act of the body made by a citta. Thirteen joyful javana cittas can produce one: a worldling smiles with any of eight, a trainee with six, an arahant with five, the four joyful great functionals and this one (VI §11 with the Guide).Where teachers differ. Did the Buddha smile with it? The root sub-commentary on the Dhammasaṅgaṇī objects: this citta has no wisdom in it, while the canon says all the Buddha’s bodily action follows knowledge (Mahāniddesa; Paṭisambhidāmagga, ps3.1:26.1); so the Atthasālinī’s statement “should be examined” (on Dhs §568). The later teachers answer that the smile does follow knowledge, since it comes at the end of the work of his knowledges; and that if the objection held, the Buddha could not have mind-door adverting either, which also moves the body (second sub-commentary on Dhs §568; sub-commentary on Vism XIV §454; Vibhāvinī on VI §11; Ledi, Paramatthadīpanī §158). Does it smile at the five doors? The second sub-commentary says that there it only gladdens, since five-door cittas make no bodily act; the actual smile comes at the mind door (on Dhs §568). The Manual lists the smiling cittas without such a limit (VI §11).

Twenty-four beautiful

“Beautiful”, sobhanasobhana · beautiful the 59 cittas other than unwholesome and rootless ones, and their 25 factors, is wider than “wholesome”. It covers every citta with beautiful mental factors, so it takes in resultant and functional cittas that make no kamma (Guide to I §12). In the sense sphere there are twenty-four: eight wholesome, eight resultant and eight functional, each set sorted by the same three switches: joy or neutral feeling, with or without knowledge, unprompted or prompted (I §§13–16). With knowledge they have three roots; without it, two, non-greed and non-hatred (Guide to I §13). Without knowledge does not mean deluded: it means only that the factor of wisdom is absent (Guide to I §13).

The great wholesome eight. The Visuddhimagga’s examples run parallel to those for greed (Vism XIV §452). Someone meets a fine gift, a worthy recipient or some other cause for joy, and with the right view that there is fruit in giving, the view of MN 41, gives without hesitation and unurged: that is the first. The same person, hesitating for want of free generosity, or urged by others, gives with the second. Small children who have seen their relatives do it, catching sight of monks, joyfully give whatever is in their hands or bow: the third. When their relatives tell them “give, bow”, the fourth. When the gift or recipient is nothing special, the same four arise with neutral feeling.

The Atthasālinī’s examples are close to home (on Dhs §§146–149). A monk living at the edge of the monastery thinks the shrine terrace, or the elders’ service, or the Dhamma talk is too far, and decides not to go; then reflects that it is unfitting for a monk not to go, and goes. Whether he goes by his own effort, because another admonishes him, or because he is told “come, do this”, the citta is prompted. Small children see a monk, think “this is my elder”, and bow; or their parents take them by the head and make them bow, and, though they did not want to, they bow gladly. The Guide’s examples are of generous deeds done with or without understanding, spontaneously or after deliberation (Guide to I §13).

These eight arise in worldlings and trainees, not in arahants (Guide to I §13). The Guide says that “according to the commentary” one acts unprompted from fitness of body and mind, good food and climate, and past practice; that list is the Vibhāvinī’s (on I §13, CS §15; climate and food at CS §4).

The great resultants. Each wholesome citta has its result of the same shape (I §14). They do not act: they carry a life, as rebirth-linking, life-continuum and death, and register an object after the javanas, all within the sense sphere (Vism XIV §454). Anyone reborn in the happy sense-sphere planes with two or three roots, a human being or a deva, is carried by one of them (V §11), arahants included, whose life-continuum is still a result (Guide to III §9). The Visuddhimagga gives an image: the result is like a face in a mirror, there without effort, while the wholesome citta is like the face (Vism XIV §454). The Atthasālinī adds that every bodhisatta takes his last rebirth with the first of them, joyful, with knowledge and unprompted, as the fruit of loving-kindness; the elder Mahāsīva held it was the neutral one, and the commentary sets that aside (on Dhs §498).Where teachers differ. What makes a result “prompted”? Not effort, since a result makes none. The Atthasālinī reports three elders (on Dhs §498). Tipiṭaka Cūḷanāga let it depend on the conditions present: strong, clear conditions give an unprompted result, weak ones a prompted, whatever the deed. Mahādatta of Moravāpi held a stricter rule: an unprompted deed gives only unprompted results, a prompted deed only prompted ones; Mahādhammarakkhita held the same rule within a different count. The Atthasālinī tells us to take what is sound in each. The Manual gives Cūḷanāga’s view and then the strict rule as “some teachers” (V §§29–30); the Guide, following the Vibhāvinī, ascribes it to Mahā Dhammarakkhita’s school (Guide to V §30), and Ledi Sayadaw corrects the name to Mahādatta (Paramatthadīpanī §153). Ledi also gives a concrete case: a rebirth citta is prompted if, at the previous death, relatives set the object before the dying person, unprompted if kamma brought it up of itself (§44).

The great functionals. In arahants the same eight shapes occur as functional cittas, mahākiriyamahākiriya · great functional the eight beautiful sense-sphere functional cittas: the arahant's counterparts of the great wholesome, which make no kamma: their twins, which act and leave nothing behind as kamma (I §15 with the Guide). An arahant still gives, bows and teaches, with or without the factor of knowledge in a given moment, and the giving makes no kamma. The Atthasālinī says these are the great wholesome cittas become functional because they arise in an arahant (on Dhs §576).

Beyond the sense sphere

Fifteen fine-material. These are the jhāna cittas, each defined by its jhāna factors (I §§18–20). The Manual counts five jhānas: the first with initial and sustained application, zest, happiness and one-pointedness, each later one dropping a factor, the fifth with neutral feeling and one-pointedness. It is often said that the suttas have four jhānas and the Abhidhamma five, but the Dhammasaṅgaṇī itself gives both schemes, fourfold (Dhs §§160–166) and fivefold (§§167–175), and the Manual keeps the fivefold. The Atthasālinī explains the second: for some hearers only initial application seemed gross, and the suttas already know a concentration with sustained application but without initial (on Dhs §167, citing DN 33; the same three concentrations are in MN 128). Wholesome jhānas belong to worldlings and trainees; their results arise only in beings reborn in the fine-material world; the functional jhānas are the arahant’s (Guide to I §§18–20). All have three roots (Guide to I §21).

Twelve immaterial. The four formless attainments have the same two factors as the fifth jhāna and are named by their objects (I §25; Table 1.6). The first takes infinite space; the second, the consciousness that took infinite space; the third, nothingness, the absence of that first consciousness; the fourth, the consciousness that took nothingness. Each is wholesome, resultant or functional, as before (I §§22–24).

Eight, or forty, supramundane. Four paths and four fruitions take Nibbāna as their object (I §§26–28). A path citta arises only once in a person, for one moment; its fruition follows at once and can be entered again later. There is no supramundane functional citta (Guide to I §§26–28). The Manual then counts again (I §§30–32). Every path and fruition has the factors of one jhāna or another, so each of the eight can occur at five levels: eight become forty, and the eighty-nine become a hundred and twenty-one.

Which jhāna a path has, the Guide says, the ancient teachers explained in three ways; a bare-insight meditator’s path is of the first jhāna (Guide to I §§30–31).Where teachers differ. Prompting above the sense sphere. The Manual leaves prompting out of the jhāna, formless and supramundane cittas. The Vibhāvinī gives two reasons: neither word fits, since no jhāna comes without preliminary work, nor from preliminary work without past aptitude; or “unprompted” never fits and “prompted” would add nothing (on I §18, CS §21). The Guide holds that the effort belongs to the approach, not to the attainment, so the distinction does not apply (Guide to I §21). Ledi Sayadaw keeps it, reading prompted and unprompted into the painful and pleasant modes of progress, as the commentaries’ own wording allows (Paramatthadīpanī §§50–51).

Who counted

None of these totals is in the canon. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī describes the cittas one after another, without a sum. The count of eighty-nine is the commentaries’: it stands in the Atthasālinī’s introduction, the Visuddhimagga (Vism XIV §455) and Buddhadatta’s Abhidhammāvatāra (ch. 1 v. 64); the forty supramundane cittas already stand behind the Visuddhimagga’s count of joyful cittas (Vism XIV §456), and the total of a hundred and twenty-one is given in the Abhidhammāvatāra (ch. 3 vv. 90–91), before the Manual (I §§30–32). And it is one count among several: the commentaries also multiply the great wholesome cittas by the bases of merit, the objects and the doors of action into many thousands, and add that their variety by time, place and person is beyond counting (Vibhāvinī on I §13; Abhidhammāvatāra ch. 1 v. 27).

The count is a tool. What it sorts is ordinary: a moment of seeing, a turning toward, a gift that comes of itself and one that needs a nudge, a smile at a small thing.

The ideas in this reading

  • Citta consciousnessConsciousness: one moment of knowing an object. The Abhidhamma counts 89 kinds.
  • Kamma volitionVolition, and the way it ripens.
  • Path and fruition four pathsThe four moments of awakening that cut defilements for good, each followed by its fruition.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The Atthasālinī says that when Mahāmoggallāna sat on a lotus in hell, the beings there saw him with wholesome-resultant eye-consciousness. What does that tell you about the relation between where a being is and what it experiences?

One way to see it, after you have tried

That the realm is fixed by the kamma that gave rebirth, but each moment of experience is fixed by whatever kamma ripens then. A being reborn in hell by unwholesome kamma still carries old wholesome kamma, and when a fit object comes, that kamma can ripen as a moment of pleasant seeing, hearing or touch. The same holds the other way: a deva can see something ugly. In the Abhidhamma's terms rebirth decides the life-continuum, not every citta in the life, so no one's experience is wholly of one colour, and none of it is owned by the place.
