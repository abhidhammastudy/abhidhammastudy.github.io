Taints, floods, bonds, ties

Nine lists of what binds the mind, and why the tradition kept all their names: each name has its own reach, a latent tendency is a defilement not yet abandoned, and the paths do not end every name at the same time.

About 12 minutesManual VII §§3–14 with the Guides and Table 7.1 (the nine lists of the unwholesome and the factors they name)

In brief

  • The nine lists of chapter VII name only fourteen factors, but the names are not idle: the taint of sensual desire, the tie of covetousness and the clinging to sense pleasures are all greed, yet each covers a different range of it, and they do not all end at the same path.
  • The lists are not all the suttas' own: the four main Nikāyas count three taints, never a taint of views; the sixth hindrance, ignorance, is the Dhammasaṅgaṇī's; and the latent tendencies are found in the Vibhaṅga and the Yamaka, not among the Dhammasaṅgaṇī's clusters.
  • In MN 64 a baby has no idea of 'identity' or of 'sensual pleasures', yet the latent tendency to view and to sensual lust lies within it: a latent tendency is a defilement not yet abandoned and gone to strength.
  • In the Kathāvatthu the Theravāda rejects other early schools' view that a latent tendency is dissociated from mind, indeterminate or without object; its commentaries conclude that it is a real, mind-associated unwholesome factor.
  • The commentaries see a defilement at three levels, latent, obsessing the mind, and breaking out in deeds, and match them to virtue, concentration and wisdom; the Paṭisambhidāmagga and a later chapter of the Visuddhimagga give the abandoning by substitution to insight instead.
  • The paths do not fight a defilement present at that moment; they cut off what is liable to arise, and the Visuddhimagga tells, list by list, which name falls at which path.

The seventh chapter of the Manual opens with nine lists of what binds the mind: taints, floods, bonds, ties, clingings, hindrances, latent tendencies, fetters and defilements (VII §§3–12). Then, in two short paragraphs, it reduces them: all their members are a few of the fourteen unwholesome mental factors (VII §§13–14). The lists reduced does that arithmetic. This reading asks what the arithmetic leaves open: why keep the names, what it means for a defilement to lie latent, and what a path abandons.

Nine lists, in brief

Each list has an image behind its name. The images are mostly the commentaries’, and the Visuddhimagga gathers them in one paragraph (Vism XXII §829).

Taints, āsavaāsava · ogha · yoga taints, floods, bonds: sense desire, desire for existence, views, ignorance: sensual desire, existence, views, ignorance (VII §3). Factors: greed twice, wrong view, delusion. The Atthasālinī gives “long fermented”, like old liquor, as the one sense common to every use of the word (on Dhs §1102, and on the mātikā’s cluster of taints); the Vibhāvinī adds that they ooze through the six doors like fluid from a sore (on VII §3). The Visuddhimagga reads the prefix as “up to”: they flow as far as the summit of existence and, as object, as far as change-of-lineage (Vism XXII §829; Guide to VII §3). The suttas usually count three taints, without the taint of views (DN 33; SN 38.8); the fourth appears in the Abhidhamma (Dhs §1102) and in Khuddaka books such as the Paṭisambhidāmagga and the Niddesa.

Floods, oghaogha · flood the four taints seen as floods that drag beings into the sea of becoming: sensual desire, existence, views, ignorance, and bonds, yogayoga · bond the four taints seen as bonds that keep beings from coming unyoked from suffering: sensual desire, existence, views, ignorance: the same four (VII §§4–5). As floods they drag beings down into the sea of becoming, a sea hard to get across; as bonds they never let one come unyoked from the object or from suffering (Vism XXII §829). Here the suttas already count four (SN 45.171; DN 33).

Ties, ganthagantha · tie, knot four ties of the mental and physical body: covetousness, ill will, rites and rituals, the dogma 'this alone is true', which Bodhi calls bodily knots: covetousness, ill will, rites and rituals, and the dogma “this alone is true” (VII §6). Factors: greed, hatred, wrong view twice. They knot both the mental and the physical body (Vism XXII §829); the Vibhāvinī says they tie mind to body, or this body to bodies to come (on VII §6; Guide to VII §6).

Clingings, upādānaupādāna · clinging intense grasping: at sense pleasures, views, rites, a self; link 9 of the wheel: to sense pleasures, to views, to rites, to a doctrine of self (VII §7). Greed once, wrong view three times. The prefix makes the grasping firm (Atthasālinī on Dhs §1219); the Vibhāvinī pictures a snake holding a frog (on VII §7).

Hindrances, nīvaraṇanīvaraṇa · hindrances obstacles to jhāna and to wisdom: sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, doubt; the Abhidhamma adds ignorance: sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, doubt, ignorance (VII §8). Eight factors: greed, hatred, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, doubt, delusion. They obstruct and cover the mind (Vism XXII §829). Six is the Dhammasaṅgaṇī’s count (Dhs §1158); the suttas (SN 45.177), and even the Vibhaṅga (Vibh §836), have five, without ignorance.

Latent tendencies, anusayaanusaya · latent tendencies seven (six factors) that lie latent, unabandoned and gone to strength, ready to arise when conditions meet; only the paths remove them: sensual lust, lust for existence, aversion, conceit, views, doubt, ignorance (VII §9). Seven names, six factors. They “lie along” with the mind because, having gone to strength, they lie in wait as the cause of their own arising again and again (Vism XXII §829); unabandoned as well, the Vibhaṅga commentary adds (on Vibh §949). The Dhammasaṅgaṇī also has nine clusters of defilements, but not these nine: it has none for latent tendencies, and has one the Manual leaves out, misapprehension, which is wrong view. The seven latent tendencies are found in the Vibhaṅga (Vibh §949), in the Yamaka, and in suttas such as AN 7.11–12.

Fetters, saṃyojanasaṃyojana · fetter one of ten defilements that bind to rebirth; the four paths cut them, in two lists of ten (VII §§10–11). The first, found in the suttas and the Abhidhamma alike, splits greed three ways: sensual lust, lust for fine-material and for immaterial existence. The second, the Abhidhamma’s own (Dhs §1118; Vibh §969), adds envy and avarice (Guide to VII §§10–11). Fetters bind aggregate to aggregate, kamma to its fruit, beings to suffering (Vism XXII §829).

Defilements, kilesakilesa · defilement one of ten states that torment the mind, from greed to fearlessness of wrongdoing: greed, hatred, delusion, conceit, views, doubt, sloth, restlessness, shamelessness and fearlessness of wrongdoing (VII §12). Ten names, ten factors. They are defiled and defile what comes with them (Vism XXII §829); they afflict the mind (Guide to VII §12).

Bodhi’s Table 7.1 sums this up, and its last column is easy to misread. Greed’s “9” counts the nine kinds of list it appears in (both lists of fetters counted as one), not its number of names, which is larger.

One factor, different reach

Why keep the names? One might answer that each name catches a different behaviour of the same factor. The commentaries say something sharper: the names cut the factor at different widths. Three names for greed show it.

The taint of sensual desire is narrow. The Atthasālinī says that lust for the mansions, wishing-trees and ornaments of the Brahmā gods is not this taint; nor is lust that comes with a wrong view, which it calls view-lust (on Dhs §1102). The tie of covetousness is wide. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī defines it not with the short formula for sensual desire but with its long string of words for greed, and the Atthasālinī explains why: the tie is every kind of greed, lust for the Brahmā worlds included (on Dhs §1140). The clinging to sense pleasures is wide in another way. “Sense pleasures” here means every object of desire, not only the five strands of sensual pleasure, so that even a non-returner, free of sensual lust, still has this clinging (Atthasālinī on Dhs §1220).

The consequence shows in the Visuddhimagga’s table of what each path abandons (Vism XXII §830). The taint of sensual desire falls at the third path, the path of non-returning. The tie of covetousness and the clinging to sense pleasures last until the fourth, and the Visuddhimagga gives the reason for the clinging: lust for fine-material and immaterial existence also counts as sensual there.

Greed, then, is seen through several windows of different widths, and the paths close them at different times. Knowing only that each name “is greed”, one could not say why a non-returner, free of the taint of sensual desire, still has the tie of covetousness. That is reason enough to keep them. (The image of windows is ours; the widths and the path assignments are the commentaries’.)

What lies latent

In MN 64 the Buddha asks how a young baby lying on its back could have identity view, when it has not even the notion “identity”; or doubt, clinging to precepts, sensual lust or ill will, when it has no notion of teachings, precepts, sensual pleasures or beings. Yet the latent tendency to each, he says, lies within it. The same sutta then describes the ordinary person whose mind is obsessed by identity view, which, having gone to strength and not been removed, is a fetter; and the noble disciple, in whom identity view is abandoned together with its latent tendency (MN 64). Latent, obsessing, gone to strength: the three ideas the commentaries later systematise are already here.

Other suttas tie the tendencies to feeling. In MN 44 the nun Dhammadinnā says that the tendency to lust lies in pleasant feeling, to aversion in painful feeling, to ignorance in neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling; and then that it does not lie in every such feeling, since in the first jhāna lust is abandoned and no tendency to it lies there (MN 44; also SN 36.3). The Yamaka draws a different grid, placing the tendency to sensual lust in neutral as well as pleasant sense-sphere feeling (Yamaka VII §2) (the two feelings, as the commentary reads them).

The Vibhaṅga sets the seven latent tendencies beside seven fetters and seven obsessions, pariyuṭṭhānapariyuṭṭhāna · obsession a defilement risen into the mind and taking it over, but not yet in deed; the middle of the commentaries' three levels, with the same names (Vibh §949): canonical evidence that “obsession” is a category of its own. Its commentary defines a latent tendency as a defilement that lies along because it is unabandoned and gone to strength, thāmagatathāmagata · gone to strength a canonical word (MN 64) that the commentaries make the mark of a latent tendency: a defilement not only unabandoned but grown firm, ready to arise when its cause is met (on Vibh §949). The Vibhāvinī presses the second mark. If being unabandoned were enough, every defilement would be a latent tendency; what makes these seven so is that they have gone to strength (on VII §9). The Guide takes the wider view: any defilement may be called latent, these seven being only the chief (Guide to VII §9). The canon’s own use is wider than seven, as MN 64’s tendencies to identity view, rites and ill will show.

Is a latent tendency a mental factor at all? Other early schools said no. A worldling whose present citta is wholesome is still called “with latent tendencies”, and they cannot be in that citta; so, these schools argued, they are dissociated from mind, without object or root, indeterminate, and a different thing from the obsession. The Kathāvatthu rejects each thesis (Kv IX.4; XI.1–3; XIV.5–6; also III.5), and its commentary names the holders: Andhakas, Sammitiyas, Mahāsaṅghikas and some Uttarāpathakas. The Theravādin’s reply is by parity: the same worldling is also called “with lust” at that moment; is that lust then without object too? The commentary on Kv IX.4 explains: he is “with latent tendencies” only because they are unabandoned, not because they are occurring. The commentary on the Yamaka goes further: the latent tendency is the defilement gone to strength, a real, citta-associated, conditioned, wholly unwholesome dhamma (Pañcappakaraṇa commentary on Anusayayamaka §1).

Why does this matter for the whole Abhidhamma? Because the Manual rests rebirth on it. Rebirth-linking, it says, is produced by a volitional formation wrapped in latent ignorance, with latent craving at its root (V §37). What carries the round on is not only the craving that bends the mind at death (Vism XVII §631), but that craving and ignorance are still unabandoned: the liability the path removes (cf. Vism XXII §835).

That is also what the path removes. The Paṭisambhidāmagga asks whether the path abandons past, future or present defilements, and answers: none of them, for the past are gone, the future not yet arisen, and a path with present defilements would be defiled. Yet abandoning is real, as cutting the root of a young tree means fruit not yet born will never be (ps3.3:17–18). The Visuddhimagga builds on this (Vism XXII §§831–838). The path abandons defilements “arisen as having soil”: liable to arise in aggregates not yet fully understood. The soil is the aggregates as the defilement’s basis, not its object; defilements can take even an arahant’s aggregates as their object, yet those aggregates are not their soil (§834). The roots of the round lie in all five aggregates, as the sap of earth and water runs through a whole tree, not its root only; the path is like a poison thorn driven into that tree, after which it bears no more fruit (§§835–836).Where teachers differ. Past, present or future? The commentary on the Kathāvatthu says an unabandoned defilement is none of the three, and “exists” only as not yet abandoned by the path (on Kv IX.4). The Pañcappakaraṇa commentary says the latent tendency can be past, future or present, and arises (on Anusayayamaka §1). The Vibhāvinī says that strictly they are future defilements, called so in the past and present because their nature is the same, and that they lie along the continuity “as it were” (on VII §9). Ledi Sayadaw answers the Vibhāvinī: they really lie along, are not future, and are present only in a loose sense until they rise to obsession (Paramatthadīpanī, CS §162). All agree that what the path removes is the defilement as unabandoned and liable to arise; they differ on how to place it in the Abhidhamma’s scheme of time. The reach of a name. The Atthasālinī and the Vibhāvinī count lust that comes with the eternalist view as the taint of existence (on Dhs §1102; on VII §3). Ledi disagrees: the Dhammasaṅgaṇī puts the taint of existence only in greed-cittas without view (Dhs §1465), so that lust belongs to the taint of sensual desire (Paramatthadīpanī, CS §162). Each side argues from the Dhammasaṅgaṇī. Which path ends worry? The Atthasālinī, on the hindrance cluster, puts worry with doubt at stream-entry (on Dhs §§1158 ff.); Vism XXII §830 and Table 9.3 put it at non-returning.

Three levels, three abandonings

The commentaries give a defilement three levels. It can lie latent; it can rise into the mind and take it over, as an obsession; and it can break out in body and speech, as a transgression, vītikkamavītikkama · transgression a defilement broken out in body or speech; the coarsest of the commentaries' three levels, opposed by virtue. The Visuddhimagga sets these against the three trainings in its first chapter: virtue opposes the transgression of the defilements, concentration their obsession, wisdom their latent tendency. In the same paragraph virtue abandons by substitution, tadaṅgatadaṅga-pahāna · abandoning by substitution removal of a defilement by its opposite, as a lamp removes darkness, for as long as the opposite lasts; given to virtue in Vism I §5, to insight in Vism XXII, concentration by suppression, vikkhambhanavikkhambhana-pahāna · abandoning by suppression the holding down of defilements by concentration, as a pot pushes moss aside on water; they can return, and wisdom by cutting off, samucchedasamuccheda-pahāna · abandoning by cutting off the path's way of abandoning a defilement: for good, never to arise again (Vism I §5). Its sub-commentary defines the levels: transgression at the doors of body and speech; obsession, defilements that, given an opening, rise and take over the wholesome course of the mind; latent tendency, the seven gone to strength. The wisdom that cuts off, it adds, is the wisdom of the four paths (sub-commentary on Vism I §5). The introductions to Buddhaghosa’s commentaries on the Abhidhamma, the Dīgha Nikāya and the Vinaya give the same three levels to the three baskets: the Vinaya abandons transgression, the Suttanta obsession, the Abhidhamma the latent tendency (Atthasālinī, Sumaṅgalavilāsinī and Samantapāsādikā, introductions).

A caution belongs beside this. The same three words appear elsewhere with a different distribution. The Paṭisambhidāmagga counts five abandonings: suppression of the hindrances by one developing the first jhāna; substitution of views by one developing the concentration that partakes of penetration, that is, insight; cutting off by one developing the supramundane path; tranquillisation at fruition; and escape, which is Nibbāna (ps1.1:125). The Visuddhimagga’s chapter on the paths keeps the first three. Suppression is mundane concentration, like a pot pushing aside moss on water; it holds the hindrances back so that, before and after the jhāna, they do not suddenly overwhelm the mind (Vism XXII §848). Substitution is insight: each knowledge removes its opposite as a lamp removes darkness, as defining mind and matter removes identity view, and seeing rise removes the annihilationist view (§849). Cutting off is the path, like a tree struck by lightning (§851). So substitution is given to virtue in Vism I §5, and to insight in Vism XXII and the Paṭisambhidāmagga. Both uses mean removal by the opposing quality, and both are temporary. Only cutting off is the path’s own; the other two prepare for it (§851).

What each path removes

The Manual itself names little: view and doubt at stream-entry, the attenuation of lust, hatred and delusion at once-returning, sensual lust and ill will at non-returning, and all the rest at arahantship (IX §§38–41). The fuller accounting, list by list, is the Visuddhimagga’s (Vism XXII §830), and Bodhi’s Table 9.3 gives the factor-level result.

  • Stream-entry: the taint, flood and bond of views; the ties of rites and of “this alone is true”; the clingings to views, rites and a doctrine of self; the hindrance of doubt; the latent tendencies to views and doubt; the defilements of view and doubt; the fetters of identity view, doubt and rites; in the Abhidhamma list, envy and avarice as well (Atthasālinī on the fetter cluster).
  • Once-returning ends no list item outright. It ends the gross forms of sensual lust and aversion: as fetters in both the Paṭisambhidāmagga (ps1.1:349) and Vism XXII §830, and as latent tendencies in the Paṭisambhidāmagga only; the Visuddhimagga gives the second path no latent tendency.
  • Non-returning: the taint (with the flood and bond) of sensual desire, the tie of ill will, the hindrances of sensual desire, ill will and worry, the latent tendencies to sensual lust and aversion, the defilement of hatred, and the subtle forms of sensual lust and aversion as fetters.
  • Arahantship: the taints (floods, bonds) of existence and ignorance, the tie of covetousness, the clinging to sense pleasures, the hindrances of sloth and torpor and restlessness (the Visuddhimagga counts five hindrances), the latent tendencies to conceit, existence-lust and ignorance, the other seven defilements, and the five higher fetters.

The Visuddhimagga adds a rule (Vism XXII §830): whatever is assigned to a higher path has already, through the lower ones, lost its power to lead to the woeful states. Stream-entry does not leave sensual lust and aversion untouched; it removes the grade of them that leads to a woeful rebirth. Who these four persons are, and what else marks them, is told in Four noble persons.

The reduction to factors says what the defilements are; the names say how far each reaches and when it ends.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

In the Majjhima Nikāya a baby lying on its back has no idea of "identity" or of "sensual pleasures", yet the latent tendencies to identity view and to sensual lust are said to lie within it. What is being claimed about the baby, if nothing in its mind at that moment is greedy or deluded?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Not that a hidden lust is going on in it, but that it is liable to lust: nothing has removed the conditions under which, given the object, greed and view will arise. The Theravāda commentaries put it in their own terms. A latent tendency is the defilement itself, considered as not yet abandoned and gone to strength, ready to arise when its cause is met; the Kathāvatthu's commentary says a person is "with latent tendencies" because they are unabandoned, not because they are occurring. On this account what the path removes is that liability, not a present state, which is why the <i>Paṭisambhidāmagga</i> can say (ps3.3:17–18; quoted in Vism XXII §831) the path abandons neither past, future nor present defilements, and still abandons something real.
