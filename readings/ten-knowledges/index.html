Ten knowledges, one by one

What insight looks at, the groundwork it needs, and the ten knowledges the Manual lists, from comprehension to conformity, each with what it sees and why it gives way to the next.

About 11 minutesManual IX §§22–37 with the Guides and Table 9.2 (the marks, purification of view and of overcoming doubt, the ten insight knowledges, the imperfections, insight leading to emergence, the three doors)

In brief

  • Insight (vipassanā) looks only at mind and matter, the conditioned dhammas, and sees them by three marks: impermanence, suffering and non-self.
  • Before the knowledges come two purifications: seeing mind and matter with no self behind them, and seeing their conditions in past, present and future.
  • The ten knowledges run from comprehension, which works on formations in groups, through rise and fall and dissolution, to equanimity about formations and conformity.
  • Bright experiences arise with the early knowledge of rise and fall; the Visuddhimagga calls them imperfections of insight, and seeing them as not the path is a purification of its own.
  • Fear, danger and disenchantment are knowledges, judgements about formations, not moods: the Visuddhimagga says the knowledge of fear does not itself fear.
  • The canon counts some of the ten as one knowledge with several names, while the sixteen stages and the 'dark night' are modern labels, not the texts' own.

The tree of purification gave the frame: seven purifications, and within them ten insight knowledges. This reading takes the knowledges one at a time: what each looks at, what it sees, and why it gives way to the next. The Manual lists them in a single line (IX §25) and opens them out in a few paragraphs (IX §§32–34); the Guide and the Visuddhimagga fill in the rest.

What insight looks at

Insight, vipassanāvipassanā · insight seeing mind and matter as impermanent, suffering and non-self, is wisdom that sees. Its field is narrow by design. It looks only at formations, the conditioned dhammas of the three planes: mind and matter, citta, its factors and rūpa. It does not take concepts as its object, and it does not take Nibbāna (IX §32; Guide to III §18). What it sees in formations is their three marks, tilakkhaṇatilakkhaṇa · the three marks impermanence, suffering and non-self: the marks that insight sees: impermanence, suffering and non-self (IX §23). For each mark there is a contemplation (IX §24), and each knowledge below is a stage in how clearly the marks are seen.

The Manual gives each mark a single word. Formations are impermanent because they are destroyed (khaya), suffering because they are a source of fear (bhaya), non-self because they have no core (asāraka) (IX §32). The Guide spells out each word.

Groundwork: view and doubt

Two purifications come before any of the ten. Under them lie purification of virtue and purification of mind, the roots of the tree, which the tree describes.

Purification of view discerns mind and matter by their characteristic, function, manifestation and proximate cause (IX §30). It takes its name from the view it clears away, the view of a self (Guide to IX §30). The Visuddhimagga sums up what is seen: only mind and matter, no being and no person (Vism XVIII §672). The Visuddhimagga gives two ways in. A meditator who already has jhāna starts from the factors of the jhāna; one who goes by pure insight starts from the four elements (Vism XVIII §§663–664). Either way the result is the same: only mind and matter, no being.noteWhere teachers differ: calm first, or pure insight. The canon knows more than one route: Ānanda lists calm before insight, insight before calm, the two yoked together, and a mind that settles after restlessness about the Dhamma (AN 4.170). The Visuddhimagga defines purification of mind as the attainments with their access (Vism XVIII §662), and names a pure-insight vehicle that begins from the four elements (§664). That the pure-insight meditator’s concentration is momentary concentration, khaṇika-samādhikhaṇika-samādhi · momentary concentration concentration that holds each passing event in turn; the term is in the Visuddhimagga; the Guide reckons it as purification of mind for the pure-insight meditator, is said in Dhammapāla’s sub-commentary (on Vism I §§3–4); the term itself is in the Visuddhimagga (Vism IV §72, VIII §235). The Guide reckons it, being as steady as access, as purification of mind for one who goes by dry insight (Guide to IX §29). The Mahasi school in Burma teaches insight in this way (Mahasi Sayadaw, The Progress of Insight).

Purification by overcoming doubt discerns the conditions of that same mind and matter (IX §31). Here the meditator brings dependent origination to what was just seen. Mind and matter are seen to have causes, neither none nor a lord’s; the body is produced by ignorance, craving, clinging and kamma and sustained by nutriment; and as now, so in the past and the future (Vism XIX §§678–679; Guide to IX §31). Doubts about the self in the three times fall away (Vism XIX §§678–691).noteThe Visuddhimagga calls a meditator who has reached this point a “lesser stream-enterer”, with a fixed destiny, which the sub-commentary reads as a good one so long as this knowledge is not lost (Vism XIX §691). The name is commentarial; it is not a path, and the person is still a worldling.

These two are not counted among the ten. They are the ground the ten stand on.

The ten in order

The Manual names ten knowledges: comprehension, rise and fall, dissolution, fear, danger, disenchantment, desire for deliverance, reflection, equanimity about formations, and conformity (IX §25). The first two belong to the fifth purification, of what is and is not the path; the rest, beginning with rise and fall again, to the sixth, the purification of the way (IX §§32–33; Table 9.2). Rise and fall is counted once but appears twice, first tender and then mature.noteWhere teachers differ: ten, or sixteen. The Manual counts ten knowledges, and the Visuddhimagga has the same knowledges: comprehension and rise and fall in XX, then eight knowledges and conformity as the nine of the way (Vism XXI §737). The “sixteen knowledges” familiar from modern teaching add defining mind and matter and discerning conditions before the ten, and change-of-lineage, path, fruition and reviewing after. Every item is in Vism XVIII–XXII; the count of sixteen is a later arrangement used in modern teaching, for example in Mahasi Sayadaw’s The Progress of Insight, not a number found in the Visuddhimagga. The “sixteen kinds of knowledge-conduct” named for the attainment of cessation are a different list (Vism XXIII §868).

1. Comprehension

The knowledge of comprehension, sammasana-ñāṇasammasana-ñāṇa · comprehension the first insight knowledge, assigning the three marks to formations gathered in groups, still works with groups. The meditator gathers the formations already discerned into the five aggregates, all materiality whether past, future or present, inner or outer, gross or subtle, and so on, and the same for feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. Then each group is assigned the three marks (IX §32 with the Guide).

It works by duration, continuity and moment. By duration, one starts with a whole lifetime and shortens the span step by step, to a decade, a year, a day, an hour, until even the formations of a single step are seen as impermanent, suffering and non-self (Guide to IX §32; Vism XX §§692–722). It works on formations in groups, by spans of time, before the knowledges that watch present formations arise and pass.

2. Rise and fall, tender

The knowledge of rise and fall, udayabbaya-ñāṇaudayabbaya-ñāṇa · rise and fall knowledge seeing dhammas arise and pass; first tender, then mature, turns to formations in the present. It sees them arise and pass in two ways: by condition, as each arises with the arising of its conditions and ceases with their ceasing; and by moment, as they are generated and break up now (IX §32; Vism XX §§723–731).noteLedi Sayadaw, in his commentary on the Manual (Paramatthadīpanī, CS §171), holds that a disciple cannot see the rise and fall of single moments of mind and matter; “by moment” must include short runs of them.

It is in this first, tender phase that the imperfections arise.

The ten imperfections

As rise and fall gains momentum, ten experiences arise that the Visuddhimagga calls the imperfections of insight, vipassanupakkilesavipassanupakkilesa · imperfections of insight ten bright experiences, such as light and zest, that arise with early rise and fall and can be clung to (Vism XX §§732–736). In its order they are: light, knowledge, zest, tranquillity, happiness, resolution, exertion, mindfulness, equanimity, and attachment. The Manual’s verse lists the same ten in a different order (IX §32).

What is strange about the list is that most of it is good. Zest, tranquillity, mindfulness and equanimity are wholesome factors; knowledge is wisdom itself. The Visuddhimagga says plainly that the first nine are imperfections not because they are unwholesome but because they are a basis for defilement: each can be taken up by view (“my light has arisen”), by conceit (“what a fine light”) or by craving, relishing it, so that ten become thirty (Vism XX §735). Only the tenth, attachment, nikantinikanti · attachment the subtle liking for insight's bright experiences; the tenth imperfection, is both a defilement and a basis for defilement, and so subtle that it is hard to recognise as one (Vism XX §§734–735).

They come, the Visuddhimagga adds, only to one who is practising rightly; not to one who has given up, nor to one who has already reached a path (Vism XX §732). The danger is a simple misreading: taking the light and the calm for the goal, and stopping there. The Visuddhimagga describes the meditator who decides “I have reached path and fruit”, leaves the meditation subject and sits relishing the light (Vism XX §733). The canon knows the problem under another name: restlessness about the Dhamma, a mind stirred up by its own good states, which the Paṭisambhidāmagga works out as these ten (AN 4.170; Paṭis, Yuganaddhakathā, quoted at Vism XX §732).

Purification by knowledge and vision of what is and is not the path is the answer (IX §32). The meditator sees the ten for what they are, impermanent, suffering and non-self, not mine, not I, not my self, and so knows: this is not the path; insight free of these is. The Visuddhimagga makes both steps explicit, seeing them by the marks and then marking path from not-path (Vism XX §736).

The way: from mature rise and fall to conformity

With the imperfections seen through, the sixth purification begins: nine knowledges, from rise and fall to conformity (IX §33).

2 again. Rise and fall, mature

The same knowledge, practised again, now without the imperfections, and stronger and clearer (Guide to IX §33). The Visuddhimagga says what had been hiding the marks: continuity hid impermanence, the shifting of postures hid suffering, and the appearance of a solid mass hid non-self (Vism XXI §§737–740).

3. Dissolution

As the knowledge grows keen, formations show up quickly, and mindfulness comes to rest on their destruction and cessation alone. This is the knowledge of dissolution, bhaṅga-ñāṇabhaṅga-ñāṇa · dissolution insight knowledge that attends only to the ending of formations (Vism XXI §741; Guide to IX §33). The meditator sees not only the object dissolving but also the knowing of it, and infers that it was so in the past and will be so in the future (Vism XXI §§741–745). Among its benefits the Visuddhimagga lists the giving up of attachment to life and, notably, fearlessness (Vism XXI §749).

4–6. Fear, danger, disenchantment

Then three knowledges that sound like feelings and are not.

The knowledge of fear, or of appearance as fearful, finds every dissolving formation, of whatever realm and time, a thing to be feared (Vism XXI §750; Guide to IX §33). The knowledge of danger finds no shelter or refuge anywhere in becoming: whatever arises is a danger, and safety is in non-arising, which is Nibbāna (Vism XXI §§752–753). The knowledge of disenchantment, nibbidānibbidā · disenchantment turning away from formations, seen as fearful and dangerous; an insight knowledge, follows from that: the mind turns away from formations of every kind and finds no pleasure in them (Vism XXI §754; Guide to IX §33).

The Visuddhimagga is careful here. The knowledge of fear does not itself fear. It only judges, as a man standing by three pits of glowing embers judges that whoever falls in will burn, without being in them or afraid of them (Vism XXI §751). The fear is in what is seen, the fearfulness of formations, not in the seer. These are judgements, knowledges, and the texts treat them that way.noteWhere teachers differ: the “dark night”. Some modern teachers group the knowledges from dissolution to reflection as a hard passage. The name “dark night” for it is best known from Daniel Ingram (Mastering the Core Teachings of the Buddha, 2008; 2nd ed. 2018), who takes it from the Christian mystics. It is not a Pāli term; the Visuddhimagga and its commentaries give these knowledges no name for hardship. The Visuddhimagga’s reading is that they are judgements, and that dissolution brings fearlessness (Vism XXI §§749, 751).

The Paṭisambhidāmagga counts the three as one knowledge with three names, and the Visuddhimagga agrees (Vism XXI §755).

7–9. Desire for deliverance, reflection, equanimity

Seeing formations so, the mind clings to none of them and wants to be free of the whole field: the knowledge of desire for deliverance, muñcitukamyatā-ñāṇamuñcitukamyatā-ñāṇa · desire for deliverance insight knowledge that wants release from the whole field of formations. The Visuddhimagga compares it with a fish in a net, or a frog in a snake’s mouth (Vism XXI §756).

To get free, the meditator looks again at the same formations and lays the three marks on them in many ways. This is the knowledge of reflection, paṭisaṅkhā-ñāṇapaṭisaṅkhā-ñāṇa · reflection insight knowledge that re-examines formations by the three marks, as the means of release: re-examination as the means of release (Guide to IX §33; Vism XXI §§757–759).

Formations are now seen as void of self and of anything belonging to a self (Vism XXI §760), and the mind, dropping both fear and delight, looks on them evenly, neither drawn nor repelled. This is equanimity about formations, saṅkhārupekkhāsaṅkhārupekkhā · equanimity about formations insight knowledge that looks on all formations evenly, with neither fear nor delight (Vism XXI §767; Guide to IX §33). The Visuddhimagga gives it a long treatment, with the many ways in which formations are seen as void (Vism XXI §§760–781). It also says something useful about its shape. If this knowledge sees Nibbāna as peace, it moves on toward the path; if not, it turns back to formations, again and again, like a ship’s crow released to find land that returns to the mast when it sees none (Vism XXI §767).

Again the Paṭisambhidāmagga counts these three as one knowledge, and again the Visuddhimagga follows it (Vism XXI §§778–781). The Paṭisambhidāmagga’s own list is shorter still, with no separate conformity; the ten unfold it.

10. Conformity

The last is conformity, anulomaanuloma · conformity the javana that fits the mind to what follows, just before change-of-lineage, also called adaptation: insight in the last two or three sense-sphere javanas before change-of-lineage, still taking formations by one of the three marks (IX §34). The Visuddhimagga likens it to a king who agrees with his eight judges and with the ancient law: it agrees with the eight knowledges before it and with the thirty-seven aids to awakening to come (Vism XXI §804).

Emergence, and the path

When equanimity about formations and conformity are full grown they are called insight leading to emergence, the peak of insight (IX §34). The Visuddhimagga includes change-of-lineage in that peak as well (Vism XXI §782). The path is “emergence” because it rises out of formations to take Nibbāna as object, and out of the defilements (Guide to IX §34).

The rest is one process. Two or three moments of insight, called preparation, access and conformity, are followed by change-of-lineage, gotrabhūgotrabhū · change-of-lineage the javana that leaves the sense-sphere lineage for jhāna, or turns to Nibbāna, the first citta to take Nibbāna as object; then a single moment of path; then fruition and reviewing (IX §34). Change-of-lineage belongs to neither the sixth purification nor the seventh (Table 9.2; Vism XXII §806). The details are in The tree of purification, and what each of the four paths cuts is in Four noble persons.

Three doors

At its peak, insight settles on one of the three marks, and that mark is its door (IX §35). Contemplating non-self, which lets go of clinging to a self, is the door of the void. Contemplating impermanence, which lets go of the false sign of permanence, is the door of the signless. Contemplating suffering, which lets go of craving’s longing, is the door of the desireless. The commentaries add that faith leans toward impermanence, concentration toward suffering and wisdom toward non-self (Guide to IX §35).

The path is then named by its door: the void, the signless or the desireless emancipation, and the fruit takes the same name (IX §36). In later attainments of fruition, the name follows the insight used that time (IX §37). But by their object and their own quality, every path and fruit is all three at once, since Nibbāna is void, signless and desireless alike (Guide to IX §37).noteThe Visuddhimagga records that the Abhidhamma books themselves name only two kinds of path, the void and the desireless. By the strict method, it explains, insight still has formations as its sign and so is not strictly signless; the threefold naming, from the Paṭisambhidāmagga, holds in a looser sense (Vism XXI §770).

  • Path and fruition four pathsThe four moments of awakening that cut defilements for good, each followed by its fruition.
  • Nibbāna unconditionedThe one unconditioned reality, known in the moments of awakening called path and fruition.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The Visuddhimagga says the knowledge of fear does not itself fear: it judges that whatever dissolves is no refuge, as a man looking at pits of embers judges that whoever falls in will burn. What is the difference between seeing that something is dangerous and being afraid of it?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Fear is a reaction, and it is about oneself: it wants to get away. The judgement is about the thing: it sees what the thing is and what it cannot give. The man at the pits is not in them and is not trembling; he knows what they are, and so he will not walk into them. On the texts' account the knowledges of fear, danger and disenchantment are of this second kind. They change what the mind expects from formations, and on this reading it is that change, not a feeling, that turns it toward release.
