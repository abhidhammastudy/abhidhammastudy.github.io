Ten ways to act

Kamma is made through three doors, body, speech and mind, in ten typical ways for ill and ten for good. Which root drives each, when an intention becomes a full course of action, and the ten bases of merit.

About 8 minutes

In brief

  • The Abhidhamma sorts deeds (kamma) by three doors, body, speech and mind; the door names the kind of act, not the limb that carried it out.
  • There are ten unwholesome courses of action: killing, stealing and sexual misconduct; four kinds of wrong speech; and covetousness, ill will and wrong view.
  • An intention is already kamma when the effort starts; if it reaches its aim, it becomes a full course of action, strong enough to produce a rebirth.
  • The Manual gives each course a root: hatred for killing, harsh speech and ill will; greed for sexual misconduct, covetousness, wrong view; either for the rest.
  • The ten wholesome courses abstain from the seven wrongs of body and speech, and add non-covetousness, non-ill will and right view.
  • A second list, the ten bases of merit, is summed up in three: giving, virtue and meditation.

Kamma, death and rebirth sorts kamma by when and how it ripens. The Manual also sorts it by where it is done (V §§22–26). The answer is three doors: the body, speech and the mind. It is the Abhidhamma’s account of what counts as a deed.

Three doors

A door here is the medium a volition acts through. The door of the body is bodily intimation, the change in the body’s matter by which a mind expresses itself (VI §4); the door of speech is vocal intimation. The door of mind is the mind itself, acting without either.

The Manual says each kind of deed occurs “mostly” through its door, and the qualification matters. Killing done by giving an order uses speech, but it is still a bodily deed; a lie told in writing uses the hand, but it is still false speech (Guide to V §22). The door names the kind of act, not the muscles that carried it out.

Ten for ill

Three are bodily: killing, stealing and sexual misconduct. Four are verbal: false speech, slander, harsh speech and idle chatter. Three are purely mental: covetousness, ill will and wrong view. These are the ten unwholesome courses of action, kammapathakammapatha · course of action one of ten typical deeds of body, speech or mind, unwholesome or wholesome; complete, it can produce a rebirth (V §22).

Each of the first seven is a volition, and the volition is already kamma when the effort starts, whether or not it succeeds. If it reaches its aim, the victim dead, the goods taken, the lie believed, it becomes a full course of action: a kamma strong enough to produce a rebirth.noteThe commentaries list the conditions that complete each course; killing, for instance, needs a living being, the knowledge that it is one, the intention to kill, the effort, and the death that follows from it. The tips in the figure give them in short. The last three are not volitions but other mental factors grown strong: covetousness is greed that has become the wish to own what is another’s; ill will is hatred that has become the wish that someone be harmed; wrong view counts only when it denies that deeds have results at all.

Which root drives each

The Manual assigns each course a root (V §23). Killing, harsh speech and ill will come from hatred. Sexual misconduct, covetousness and wrong view come from greed. Stealing, lying, slander and idle chatter can come from either. And delusion underlies all ten.

The Guide adds a careful point. Someone may kill for gain, or misbehave sexually out of spite. Still, the Abhidhamma holds that the volition that actually cuts off a life is always aversion to that life continuing, and the one that drives sexual transgression is always desire. The motive may be elsewhere; the act itself has its fixed root.

Ten for good

The wholesome courses run through the same doors. The first seven are abstaining from the seven unwholesome deeds of body and speech: in the Abhidhamma’s terms, the abstinences (mental factors that hold back from a wrong act) right action and right speech, with the volition that goes with them. The last three are non-covetousness, non-ill will and right view, which are the three wholesome roots at work (Guide to V §24).

The Manual adds a second list, the ten bases of merit:

  • giving, virtue, meditation;
  • reverence, service;
  • sharing one’s merit, rejoicing in others’ merit;
  • hearing the Dhamma, teaching it, and straightening one’s own view.

The first three, giving, virtue and meditation, are the classical threefold summary.noteThe commentaries fold the other seven into the three: sharing and rejoicing into giving, reverence and service into virtue, hearing, teaching and straightening view into meditation, though straightening view is also said to belong to all. All twenty courses of action, ten for ill and ten for good, are made by the twelve unwholesome cittas and the eight great wholesome cittas: kamma of the sense sphere.

Beyond the three doors

The jhānas are kamma too, but only of the mind. Fine-material wholesome kamma is meditation that has reached absorption, fivefold by its jhāna factors; immaterial kamma is fourfold, by its object (V §§25–26). In every case the deed is made in the mind; the doors only say how it went out. What each kind of kamma gives as result is followed in From kamma to its results.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The Abhidhamma counts covetousness, ill will and wrong view as actions, though nothing is done or said. Is a thought you never act on really a deed?

One way to see it, after you have tried

In the Abhidhamma's sense, yes, when it is complete: when wanting becomes the wish to take what is another's, when anger becomes the wish that someone be harmed, or when a view denies that deeds have results. Each of these is a mental factor, greed, hatred or wrong view, grown into a full course and arising with volition, and each shapes the next citta and the next choice. That is also why the training does not wait for actions to appear: the mind door is where the other two doors are first opened.
