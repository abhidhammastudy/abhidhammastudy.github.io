An ensemble of fifty-two

Seven factors present in every moment of mind, two families that never meet, and why the Abhidhamma thinks of a mind as a chord.

About 8 minutesManual II §§1–9 (the 52 in four groups), with the guide's definitions

In brief

  • Each moment of consciousness (citta) comes with mental factors that arise and cease with it; if consciousness is a note, the factors make it a chord.
  • The Abhidhamma counts fifty-two factors, and seven of them, among them contact, feeling, perception and volition, are present in every moment of mind.
  • Six more come and go, and like the seven they take on the colour of the moment they join, so energy can serve greed as well as kindness.
  • The other thirty-nine form two families, fourteen unwholesome and twenty-five beautiful, which never occur in the same moment.
  • Good qualities arrive together: every beautiful moment has nineteen beautiful factors at once, and every unwholesome one carries delusion and a lapse of conscience.
  • Mixed motives are a rapid alternation of whole states, so a generous moment is not diluted by the selfish one that follows it.

A citta never arises alone. It comes with company: the mental factors, cetasikacetasika · mental factor a mental factor: arises and ceases with a citta, sharing its object and bases, which arise with it, cease with it, take the same object and depend on the same base. Citta is the knowing; the factors are what the knowing is like. If citta is a note, the factors make it a chord.

The Abhidhamma counts fifty-two, and their arrangement is one of the most useful things in the system.

Seven that are always there

Seven factors occur in every citta, from the dullest moment of hearing to the path that sees Nibbāna. They are the minimum equipment of any experience at all.

Phassaphassa · contact the meeting of object, base and consciousness; the first universal factor, contact, is the meeting of mind and object. Vedanāvedanā · feeling feeling: the pleasant, painful or neutral tone of a moment; not emotion, feeling, is the tone of that meeting: pleasant, painful or neither. Saññāsaññā · perception perception: marks an object's features so it can be recognised again, perception, marks the object so it can be recognised again. Cetanācetanā · volition the willing that organises the other factors; as kamma, it bears results, volition, organises the rest toward the object; in some cittas it is kamma. One-pointedness, ekaggatāekaggatā · one-pointedness unifies the mind on its object; when strong it is concentration (samādhi), fixes the mind on its single object. The life faculty sustains the mental factors for their moment. Manasikāramanasikāra · attention turns the citta and its factors toward the object; present in every citta, attention, turns the citta toward its object.The suttas already say that feeling, perception and consciousness are “conjoined, not disjoined”: you cannot separate them and point to the difference between them (MN 43). The Abhidhamma lengthens the list and keeps the principle.

It is a striking list. There is no moment of mind without feeling, without some recognising, without some willing. Even the most neutral glance has a tone and a direction.

Six that come and go

Six more are “occasional”: they occur in many cittas but not all. Initial application, vitakkavitakka · initial application lays the mind onto its object; a factor of the first jhāna only, lifts the mind onto the object; sustained application, vicāravicāra · sustained application keeps the mind moving over its object; a factor of the first two jhānas, keeps it there. Decision, energy, zest, and the desire to act complete the set. These are ethically variable, like the universals. They take on the colour of whatever citta they join, so energy can serve greed as well as kindness.

Two families

The remaining thirty-nine divide into two families that never occur together.

Fourteen are unwholesome. Four of them occur in every unwholesome citta: delusion, mohamoha · delusion mental blindness to an object's true nature; present in every unwholesome citta, which hides the nature of the object; shamelessness and fearlessness of wrongdoing, the absence of a conscience; and restlessness. The others come in groups: greed with wrong view or conceit; hatred with envy, avarice or worry; sloth and torpor; and doubt.

Twenty-five are beautiful. Nineteen of them occur in every beautiful citta, and the list reads like a portrait of a good moment. They are faith, mindfulness, shame and fear of wrongdoing, non-greed, non-hatred, neutrality of mind, and six pairs of qualities, tranquillity, lightness, malleability, wieldiness, proficiency and rectitude, each of mental body and of mind. Then three abstinences, from wrong speech, wrong action and wrong livelihood; two illimitables, the boundless attitudes toward beings: compassion and appreciative joy; and wisdom.Loving-kindness and equanimity, the other two illimitables, appear in the list, the commentaries explain, under other names: non-hatred and neutrality of mind. That is why only two are listed separately.

A good moment is complete

The arrangement makes a moral point without stating it. Every beautiful citta has nineteen beautiful factors at once. In any single beautiful moment you cannot have mindfulness without faith, or non-greed without non-hatred: in this analysis the good qualities arrive together. And every unwholesome citta has its four companions, so any greed is also, at that moment, delusion and a lapse of conscience.

It also means the two families never mix. In the moment of a generous gift there is no greed in the gift. If the giver then wants thanks, that is a later citta, in another process: the seven javanas of one process are all of one kind. What feels like mixed motives is a rapid alternation of whole states, each complete in its kind.

That is good news for practice. A beautiful moment is not diluted by the selfish one that follows it. It happened, whole, and it conditions what comes next.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Greed and generosity never occur in the same citta. Yet people often feel they give and want at once. How would the Abhidhamma describe that experience?

One way to see it, after you have tried

As alternation, very fast. One citta is generous, a following one wants something back, another is generous again. Because cittas succeed each other so quickly, the mixture feels simultaneous. Seeing it as alternation is useful: each generous moment is wholly generous, and the wanting does not cancel it.
