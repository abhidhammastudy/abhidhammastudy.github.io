The commentators' picture-book

A heap of coins, a struck bell, a bird gliding: how the old teachers made a list of dhammas memorable, and what each picture is pointing at.

About 7 minutesManual II guide (each factor's characteristic, function, manifestation, proximate cause)

In brief

  • The canonical Abhidhamma gives lists and definitions; the later commentators added similes to help students remember them, each chosen to show one property only.
  • The commentaries describe each element of experience by four questions: its mark, its function, how it shows itself, and what it most immediately depends on.
  • A child, a villager and a money-changer looking at coins show perception, consciousness and understanding as depths of knowing, all of which can be present in one moment.
  • A struck bell and its ringing, or a bird taking off and then gliding, show initial and sustained application as two phases of one act of attending.
  • Every simile also misleads, so the commentators used several for one factor; like the Buddha's raft, a picture is to be used and then put down.

The canonical Abhidhamma gives lists and definitions. It rarely gives pictures. The pictures came later, from the commentators, who had to teach the lists to people who had to remember them. Their similes are some of the best teaching tools in Buddhist literature, and they share a discipline worth learning: each is chosen to show one property, and only one.

Four questions for every dhamma

First, the method behind the pictures. The commentaries describe each dhamma by four questions. What is its characteristic, lakkhaṇalakkhaṇa · characteristic a dhamma's own nature: the first part of its identity card, the mark by which it is known? What is its function, rasarasa · function (of a dhamma) what a dhamma does: the second part of its identity card, what it does? What is its manifestation, paccupaṭṭhānapaccupaṭṭhāna · manifestation how a dhamma shows itself in experience: the third part of its card, how it shows up in experience? And what is its proximate cause, padaṭṭhānapadaṭṭhāna · proximate cause what a dhamma most directly depends on: the fourth part of its card, the condition it most immediately depends on?Applied to contact: its characteristic is touching, its function is the impact of mind and object, its manifestation is their coming together, and its proximate cause is the object that has come into range.

A simile usually illustrates one of the four. It makes a definition visible.

Three people and a heap of coins

A child, a villager and a money-changer look at the same heap of coins. The child sees shapes and colours, bright and round, but does not know they are money. The villager knows they are money and that they can be spent. The money-changer knows which coins are genuine and which are false, where each was struck and what it weighs.

The Visuddhimagga uses the picture to separate three things that are easily confused. Perception, saññāsaññā · perception perception: marks an object's features so it can be recognised again, is the child: it notes surface features so they can be recognised again. Consciousness, viññāṇaviññāṇa · consciousness (aggregate) consciousness, the fifth aggregate: all 89 cittas, the same reality as citta, is the villager: it knows the object as an object. Understanding, paññāpaññā · wisdom understanding things as they are; also called non-delusion and the faculty of wisdom, is the money-changer: it penetrates the object’s real nature.

It is a picture of depth, not of separate people. All three can be present in a single moment: in a citta that has understanding, perception and consciousness are there too.

A struck bell, a gliding bird

Two of the occasional factors, those found in some cittas but not all, are hard to tell apart: vitakkavitakka · initial application lays the mind onto its object; a factor of the first jhāna only, initial application, and vicāravicāra · sustained application keeps the mind moving over its object; a factor of the first two jhānas, sustained application. The commentaries offer several pictures. Initial application is like striking a bell; sustained application is the bell’s resonance. Initial application is like a bird spreading its wings to take off; sustained application is the bird gliding on outstretched wings. Initial application is like a bee diving toward a flower; sustained application is the bee buzzing above it.The images come from the Visuddhimagga IV and the Milindapañha. L. S. Cousins’s study of the two terms shows how much their meaning shifts between the suttas and the Abhidhamma.

Each picture adds a nuance. The bell shows that sustaining follows striking. The bird shows that the first is effortful and the second smooth. The bee shows that one goes toward the object and the other stays near it. Together they say what a definition struggles to: that these are two phases of one act of attending.

What a simile hides

Every picture can also mislead. The child, the villager and the money-changer are three people, and the factors they illustrate are not people. A bell rings on after it is struck, and a dhamma does not linger after its moment. The commentators knew this, which is why they used several similes for one factor and dropped each one quickly.

That is the reader’s discipline too. Ask what a picture is pointing at, take that, and leave the rest. The Buddha called his teaching a raft, for crossing over and not for carrying (MN 22). A simile is a small raft of the same kind: use it to reach a dhamma that lasts a single moment, then put it down.

The ideas in this reading

  • Cetasika mental factorsThe mental factors that colour each citta.
  • Citta consciousnessConsciousness: one moment of knowing an object. The Abhidhamma counts 89 kinds.
  • Two truths conventional, ultimateTwo ways of speaking: of persons and things, and of the ultimate dhammas they resolve into. The starting point of the Abhidhamma.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Choose a simile you rely on for your own mind, a river, a monkey, a mirror. Which single property is it pointing at, and which does it hide?

One way to see it, after you have tried

A river shows continuity without a fixed substance, but hides the discreteness of moments. A monkey shows restlessness, but suggests a single creature swinging about. A mirror shows bare reflecting, but implies a surface that stays the same. The commentators' discipline was to use each picture for one point only and to drop it before it says more.
