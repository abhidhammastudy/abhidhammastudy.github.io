The four noble truths

The first sermon's frame, read the Abhidhamma way: the realities sorted under the truths, with a remainder left outside, each truth with its own task, and all four seen in a single moment.

About 9 minutesManual VII §§38–40 (the four noble truths, and what falls outside them)

In brief

  • The four noble truths, first taught to five ascetics near Benares, are suffering, its origin, its cessation and the path that leads there.
  • Each truth has its own task: suffering is to be fully understood, craving abandoned, cessation realised and the path developed (SN 56.11).
  • On the Manual's count, suffering is everything in the three mundane planes except craving; the origin is craving; cessation is Nibbāna; the path is eight mental factors.
  • Strictly speaking, some states fall outside all four: the path cittas themselves with their other factors, and the fruitions with theirs.
  • At each of the four paths one knowledge sees all four truths in a single moment, and each path abandons only its own share of the defilements.
  • The common slip in practice is to fight suffering; the teaching asks for it to be understood, and for craving to be abandoned.

In the Deer Park at Isipatana the Buddha told the five ascetics who had once practised with him that one gone forth should take neither of two dead ends: a life given over to sense pleasure, and a life of self-torment. The middle way he had found between them, he said, is the noble eightfold path (SN 56.11, sn56.11:2.1–3.4). Then he set out four truths, the ariya-saccaariya-sacca · noble truth suffering, its origin, its cessation, the path: the four noble truths (sn56.11:4.1–4.10). There is suffering: birth, ageing, sickness and death, meeting what is unloved, parting from what is loved, not getting what one wishes; in short, the five aggregates of clinging. There is its origin: craving, which brings renewed existence, craving for sense pleasures, for existence and for non-existence. There is its cessation: the complete fading and ending of that same craving. And there is the way there: the eight factors of the path.

While this was being spoken, Koṇḍañña, one of the five, saw that whatever has the nature to arise has the nature to cease (sn56.11:11.1–11.2). That this “eye of Dhamma” was the first path, stream-entry, is the commentary’s reading (Sāratthappakāsinī on SN 56.11, CS §1081). The story is told in Koṇḍañña's eye of Dhamma.

Every Buddhist school teaches the four truths, though not every school makes them its frame or reads them alike. What this reading adds is the Theravāda Abhidhamma’s way of reading them: as a sorting of ultimate realities, each truth with its own task, and all four penetrated in one moment.

Four truths, three turnings, twelve aspects

The sermon does not stop at naming the truths. For each, the Buddha says, three knowings arose: this is the truth; it is to be fully understood, or abandoned, or realised, or developed; and it has been (sn56.11:5.1–8.3). Four truths, three turnings each: the sermon calls this knowledge tiparivaṭṭatiparivaṭṭa · three turnings each truth in the first sermon turned three ways, as this is it, it is to be done, it has been done: twelve aspects in all, three-turned and twelve-aspected. He did not claim awakening, he says, until all twelve were fully clear (9.1–10.3).

The middle column carries the point of the whole frame. Each truth has its own task (SN 56.29). Suffering is to be fully understood, pariññāpariññā · full understanding the task of the first truth, which is to be fully understood, not abandoned; its origin, craving, is to be abandoned; its cessation is to be realised; the path is to be developed. The tasks do not swap. Suffering is never the thing to be abandoned, and cessation is never the thing to be developed. The names for the three turnings, knowledge of the truth, of the task and of the task done (sacca-, kicca- and kata-ñāṇa), are the commentary’s (CS §1081).

What each truth is, in ultimate realities

The Manual closes its compendium of the whole with the four truths, the last of five ways it divides ultimate realities: aggregates, aggregates of clinging, bases, elements and truths (VII §§38–40; see Bases and elements). Here the sermon’s words become counts of paramatthaparamattha · ultimate what remains after analysis: citta, cetasika, matter and Nibbāna, ultimate realities. The Guide to VII §38 (p. 289) gives the reckoning.

The truth of suffering, dukkhadukkha · bodily pain painful bodily feeling: only in unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness, is everything of the three mundane planes except craving. Spelled out, which is the site’s counting and not the Guide’s: the 81 mundane cittas, every mental factor that arises in them except one, and all 28 kinds of matter. The origin, samudayasamudaya · origin the origin of suffering, the second noble truth: craving, for sense pleasures, for existence and for non-existence, is that one exception, craving, taṇhātaṇhā · craving greed as thirst: for sense objects, for existence or for non-existence; a root, which is the mental factor greed, in its three aspects. Cessation, nirodhanirodha · cessation (third truth) the cessation of suffering, the third noble truth, which is Nibbāna, to be realised; not the attainment of cessation, is nibbānanibbāna the unconditioned: the ending of craving; object of path and fruition, realised by the eradication of craving. It is not an event the path brings about: Nibbāna is unconditioned, reached by the path and not produced by it (Vism XVI §560, commentarial). It is called cessation because in reaching it craving ceases, and the Manual explains the word nibbāna itself as a departure from craving (VI §30 and Guide). The path is the noble eightfold path, and here the Guide is exact: in the teaching of the truths it is only the eight factors as they arise in the four supramundane path cittas. They are wisdom as right view, initial application as right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, energy as right effort, mindfulness and one-pointedness as right concentration. In the requisites of enlightenment the same factors may be mundane or supramundane; as the fourth truth they are only supramundane.

It follows, and it is surprising, that the path factors of ordinary good moments belong with suffering. In the teaching of the noble truths the path is only supramundane (Guide to VII §38), so a moment of mundane right view, or of real effort, or of mindfulness, is counted under the first truth: Buddhaghosa puts into it everything but craving and the taint-free states (Vism XVI §564). The objection is obvious: is a kind thought suffering? “Suffering” here does not mean what hurts. It names the aggregates of clinging, craving aside: what is conditioned and can be clung to, the stuff of the round. The supramundane states are conditioned too, and in that broad sense suffering, but they are not aggregates of clinging, and so not this truth (Vism XVI §566, commentarial). The suttas know three kinds: suffering as pain, suffering in change, and the suffering of formations (SN 38.14; SN 45.165), and the commentary places pleasant feeling under the second and neutral feeling, with all the other formations of the three planes, under the third (Vism XVI §539). So the first truth takes in pleasant and wholesome states too. A good mundane state is still to be developed as practice. As a reality, it belongs with what is to be fully understood, not with what is to be clung to.Sacca means both “true” and “real”, and the last of the Guide’s four reasons for calling the truths noble is that they are real and unalterable (Guide to VII §38). The sermon states the truths (“this is suffering”); the Abhidhamma counts what they are about, so that on its use a truth is also a set of dhammas, and “noble realities” would fit as well as “noble truths”.

What falls outside

The sorting leaves a remainder, and on the Manual’s count it is a large one. Its summary verse says that the states associated with the paths, and the fruits, are excluded from the four truths (VII §40). The path’s eight factors are the fourth truth, and nothing else in the path citta is: the citta itself and its other factors, and all four fruitions, fall outside the four truths (VII §40; the Guide to §40 says “strictly speaking”). Fruition holds the same eight factors again, as results, but on this count they are not the fourth truth (Guide to VII §40).Where teachers differed: Ledi Sayadaw counts what is left out: twenty-nine states in the path moment besides the eight, and thirty-seven in fruition (Paramatthadīpanī, CS §165). These match a path at the first or second jhāna level and a fruition at the first (II §19); that is our reading of his figures, not his statement. The Vibhāvinī (CS §53) allows a looser count: the fruit’s path factors under the path, since the Dhammasaṅgaṇī still calls them “included in the path”, and the other states of path and fruit under suffering, as conditioned, so that the truths can cover everything. And the Vibhaṅga itself, in one round of its Abhidhamma method, counts the whole path citta as the way to cessation (Vibh 4, vb4:97.1–105.3).

Why craving, and why this order

The tradition’s reasons are the commentaries’, and they are worth having. Why four truths? Because nothing can be added or taken away: the Buddha taught occurring with its cause, and stopping with its means, and four exhausts it (Vism XVI §535). Why this order? Suffering comes first because it is gross and shared by all; then its cause; then cessation, to show that when the cause ends the result ends; then the way to it (Vism XVI §536). Cessation is defined as the ending of craving, not of suffering directly, because the Tathāgatas, Buddhaghosa says, are like lions, who go for the cause, while other teachers are like dogs, who go for the result: they would end suffering by tormenting the body (Vism XVI §554).

Why craving, of all the causes of suffering? The Vibhāvinī answers that craving is the chief cause: it makes kamma varied and keeps kamma company (CS §52). Ledi Sayadaw takes up the harder objection, that ignorance is the root of the whole round. In dependent origination, he says, ignorance stands first in the chain of conditions; in the teaching of the truths craving is the owner and chief that produces the aggregates. A park is made by a gardener from ground and seed: asked whose park it is, we name the gardener (Paramatthadīpanī, CS §165). And ignorance, in the sutta explanation, is just not knowing these four truths (Guide to VIII §3, p. 295).

The two teachings fit together in the suttas themselves. AN 3.61 gives the origin of suffering as dependent origination in its forward order, and its cessation as the same chain ceasing, link by link (an3.61:13.6–17.4). The wheel across three lives draws that chain.Where teachers differed (two methods): The Vibhaṅga teaches the truths twice. Its sutta method gives the sutta definitions, with craving alone as the origin. Its Abhidhamma method drops the word “noble” and widens the origin step by step: craving; then all the defilements; then all unwholesome states; then the three wholesome roots that still carry taints; then all tainted wholesome states. Each time suffering is “the rest” of the mundane (Vibh 4, vb4:55.2–80.2). Dropping “noble”, the Sammohavinodanī explains, is what lets these other causes in, since they too produce suffering (CS §§206–214); at the widest step good mundane moments count under the origin, not under suffering. The Manual’s count matches the sutta method.

Similes

The commentaries set the four truths beside images with the same four parts, every one of them commentarial (Vism XVI §565). Suffering is a burden; its origin is taking the burden up; cessation is laying it down; the path is the means of laying it down. Or a disease, its cause, its cure and the medicine. Or a famine, a drought, plenty, and the rain that brings it. The same paragraph adds an enemy and the root of enmity, a poison tree and its root, fear and its cause, and a near shore, the flood, the far shore and the effort of crossing.Comparison: The four truths are often presented today as a physician’s diagnosis. In the commentaries the disease is one image among several (Vism XVI §565), beside the burden, the famine and the rest, not the frame of the teaching.

One knowledge, four tasks, one moment

Before the path, the truths are known one at a time, and not all of them directly. The Visuddhimagga calls this following knowledge, anubodha: mundane, and for cessation and the path only by learning and hearsay. Penetration, paṭivedha, is supramundane, and takes all four at once (Vism XVI §563).

At the path moment the twelve aspects collapse into one act. The path citta, taking Nibbāna as its object, does the four tasks together: it fully understands suffering, abandons craving, realises cessation and develops the path (IX §34 and the Guide, pp. 354–356). This happens four times, once at each path. Each path does all four tasks, but abandons only its share of the defilements: the first cuts wrong view and doubt, and craving is gone entirely only at the fourth (IX §§38–41 with the Guides). Seeing any one truth, the elder Gavampati reports, is seeing all four (SN 56.30).

The Visuddhimagga gives three images for this, quoting “the ancients” for the first (XXII §§839–841). A lamp, in one moment, burns the wick, dispels the darkness, makes light appear and uses up the oil. The rising sun lights up forms, dispels the darkness, shows light and allays the cold. A boat leaves the near shore, cuts across the stream, carries its cargo and reaches the far shore. Each act is one of the tasks, and none comes before another. In all three, the path is said to be developed by conascence: its factors arise together and support each other.Comparison: The Theravāda holds a single penetration of the four truths (SN 56.30; Kathāvatthu II.9, which argues that seeing them one by one would make a quarter-arahant). Its commentary names the Andhakas, Sabbatthikas, Sammitīyas and Bhadrayānikas as holding, in its description, a gradual realisation in sixteen parts, the four truths at each of the four paths (CS §339).

Practice

The frame gives a practice that is easy to get wrong. The task for suffering is not to get rid of it but to understand it: to see the conditioned as conditioned, the pleasant as well as the painful. Before the path this understanding is insight: seeing the formations of the three planes as impermanent, suffering and not self (IX §§32–33), in the stages the commentaries call full understanding as the known, as investigating and as abandoning (Vism XX §693, commentarial). The task for craving is to abandon it where it arises, and the suttas say where that is: wherever something is pleasing, in the six-door process itself, in the eye and the sight, the seeing, the contact, the feeling and the rest, sixty places in all (DN 22, dn22:19.4–19.67). The mango and the moment shows where in a process of seeing the wanting begins. The third task is not done by working on cessation itself: it is realised when the path that is developed reaches it. The fourth is the only one to be developed.

The fourth truth, the path, is the next reading but one, The noble eightfold path, after the meditation subjects that supply its concentration.

The ideas in this reading

  • The four noble truths ariya-saccaThe Buddha's four truths, which the Abhidhamma reads as a sorting of every mundane reality, with Nibbāna and the supramundane path set apart.
  • The noble eightfold path ariya aṭṭhaṅgika maggaEight factors, right view to right concentration. In the strict sense, eight mental factors arising together in the moment of a supramundane path.
  • Nibbāna unconditionedThe one unconditioned reality, known in the moments of awakening called path and fruition.
  • Dependent origination 12 links, 3 livesThe twelve links, read across three lives.
  • Two truths conventional, ultimateTwo ways of speaking: of persons and things, and of the ultimate dhammas they resolve into. The starting point of the Abhidhamma.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Each truth has its own task: suffering is to be fully understood, its origin abandoned, its cessation realised, the path developed. Which of the four tasks do you most often confuse with another?

One way to see it, after you have tried

A common confusion is trying to abandon suffering instead of understanding it. Resisting pain only adds a second dart, mental pain on top of the bodily, with aversion beneath it (SN 36.6). The teaching assigns abandoning to craving, not to suffering. Another is trying to "develop" cessation, which can only be realised. Knowing each truth's task is the second of the three turnings (SN 56.11). In the path moment the four tasks are no longer separate: one knowledge does all four (Guide to IX §34).
