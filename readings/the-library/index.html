The library

Seven books that take experience apart and put it back together, and the long shelf of commentary that grew around them.

About 7 minutesManual Introduction (the Abhidhamma Piṭaka, commentaries, the Saṅgaha and its commentaries)

In brief

  • The Abhidhamma is the third basket of the Pāli Canon: no stories, but lists and analyses, the same teaching as the discourses set out for no particular listener.
  • Its seven books run from listing what is present in every state of mind, in the first, to relating everything by twenty-four kinds of condition, in the last.
  • Tradition says the Buddha taught it to the gods in a heaven; modern scholars see it growing over centuries from the lists in the early discourses.
  • Commentaries grew around the books; Anuruddha's summary became the doorway to the whole system, and this site follows Bhikkhu Bodhi's edition, A Comprehensive Manual of Abhidhamma.
  • The library is best read as a map, asking of each part what it lets you see that you could not see before.

The Pāli Canon is a set of three baskets. The first holds the monastic discipline, the second the discourses, the suttas. The third is the Abhidhamma PiṭakaAbhidhamma Piṭaka the third basket of the Pāli Canon: seven books of analysis, and it reads like nothing else in the Canon. There are no stories in it, almost no people, hardly a setting. There are lists, and lists of lists, and questions asked of them with a patience that can feel inhuman.

Its name is usually rendered “higher teaching” or “special teaching”. The commentaries explain the prefix abhi as “excelling” and as “distinguishing”: the same Dhamma as the suttas, but taken apart further and set out without reference to any listener.noteThe suttas teach in conventional terms to a particular person in a particular situation. The Abhidhamma is said to teach “in ultimate terms”, to no one in particular. That is the difference the two truths name; see the next reading.

Seven books

There are seven books, and their order has a logic.

The first, the DhammasaṅgaṇīDhammasaṅgaṇī the first Abhidhamma book: lists every kind of citta and its factors, the “enumeration of dhammas”, takes every state of mind and lists what is present in it. It opens with a matrix, a mātikāmātikā · matrix the Dhammasaṅgaṇī's opening lists, 22 triads and 100 dyads, that sort every dhamma: a table of headings under which anything at all can be sorted. Is it wholesome, unwholesome or neither? Is it joined with pleasant feeling, painful feeling or neither? The matrix runs through more than a hundred such pairs and triplets. The rest of the book fills the table in.

The Vibhaṅga, the “analysis”, takes familiar sutta lists, the aggregates, the bases, dependent origination, and analyses most of them three ways: as the suttas teach it, as the Abhidhamma teaches it, and by question and answer. The Dhātukathā asks how every dhamma is included in or excluded from the aggregates, bases and elements. The Puggalapaññatti is the odd one out: a book of types of person, the one place where convention is catalogued in its own right.

The Kathāvatthu is a book of debates, two hundred and more points on which the Theravāda disagreed with other schools, each refuted in turn. Tradition ascribes it to Moggaliputta Tissa at the Third Council, more than two centuries after the Buddha. The Yamaka, the “pairs”, tests the edges of every term by asking each question both ways round.

The last is the PaṭṭhānaPaṭṭhāna the seventh Abhidhamma book: relates all dhammas by 24 conditions, the “conditional relations”, the largest book of the Abhidhamma Piṭaka. Where the first book took things apart, this one puts them back together: it relates every dhamma to every other by twenty-four kinds of condition. Analysis at one end, synthesis at the other. Everything in between is method.

Where it came from

The tradition tells a story. In the seventh year after his awakening, the Buddha spent a rainy season in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven teaching the Abhidhamma to the gods, among them his mother, who had been reborn as a god in the Tusita heaven and came to hear it. Each day he came down to collect alms and gave the day’s teaching to Sāriputta in outline. Sāriputta passed it on in fuller form to five hundred monks, and that, the Atthasālinī says, is the Abhidhamma we have.

Modern scholars tell a different story. The earliest suttas already contain lists, and the monks who memorised them built matrices to keep them in order and to settle what they meant. Over several centuries, in different schools, these matrices grew into the Abhidhamma books. Other schools had their own seven: the northern Sarvāstivāda’s survive mainly in Chinese translation.noteThe reading Other maps of the same ground, near the end of the site, sets the northern lists beside the Theravāda’s.

The two stories agree on one thing. The Abhidhamma is a reading of the Buddha’s teaching, carried out by people who took it very seriously, and it was not written to be read straight through.

The shelves below

Nearly everything since is commentary. In the fifth century Buddhaghosa wrote the Atthasālinī on the first book, and the Visuddhimagga, the “path of purification”, which sets Abhidhamma inside a manual of practice. Subcommentaries followed. Then, somewhere between the tenth and twelfth centuries, a monk called Anuruddha wrote the Abhidhammattha SaṅgahaAbhidhammattha Saṅgaha Anuruddha's manual of the whole Abhidhamma in nine chapters; Bodhi's CMA translates it: a summary of the whole system in nine short chapters, small enough to learn by heart. It is the book this site follows.

It became the doorway. In Burma and Sri Lanka students memorise the Saṅgaha before they open the canonical books, and commentaries on it multiplied. In the 1890s Ledi Sayadaw disputed one of them on hundreds of points and started a revival of lay Abhidhamma study that still continues.

In 1993 Bhikkhu Bodhi published a revision of Nārada Thera’s earlier English translation of the Saṅgaha with a full explanatory guide, as A Comprehensive Manual of Abhidhamma. This site follows it chapter by chapter.

How to read the shelf

A library like this rewards a particular attitude. It is not a book to agree or disagree with page by page. It is a map, drawn by people who spent their lives looking at the same ground, and the right question to put to any part of it is the one a traveller asks of a map: what does this let me see that I could not see before?

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The tradition says the Buddha taught the Abhidhamma; modern scholars say it grew over centuries from lists. Does it matter to how you read it which story is true?

One way to see it, after you have tried

It matters to what kind of authority you give it, less to whether it is useful. Read as the Buddha's own words, every count becomes binding. Read as a careful school's working out of the suttas, it becomes a map you can test against the suttas and against experience. Many teachers in the tradition hold both: the essentials are the Buddha's, the elaboration is the disciples'.
