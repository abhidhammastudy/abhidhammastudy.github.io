The mango and the moment

A man asleep under a mango tree, a fruit that falls, and the seventeen moments in which seeing happens and kamma is made.

About 9 minutesManual IV §§1–11 (six kinds of process, the five-door process and intensity of objects)

In brief

  • Buddhaghosa's Atthasālinī tells of a sleeping man woken by a falling mango to picture one act of seeing; later teachers fitted it to all seventeen moments.
  • The seeing itself is a result of past kamma: the man did not choose to see the mango, and the moment of seeing knows only colour.
  • The commentaries place the hinge at the moments that turn to the object and settle it: wise or unwise attention there decides what kind of mind follows.
  • Only in the seven moments of action (javana), here greed for the mango, is new kamma made; habits are long runs of such moments.
  • None of the seventeen moments thinks "mango" or "I": recognising and naming come in later processes, so "I see a mango" is a convention built afterwards.
  • Most of seeing is given; the small part that is not decides what continues, and that is where practice can work.

A man is asleep at the foot of a mango tree with his head covered. A breeze moves the branches and a ripe mango falls beside his head. He wakes at the sound, uncovers his face and sees the fruit. He reaches out and picks it up, squeezes it to test it, and smells it. Then he eats it.

That is the whole story. Buddhaghosa tells it in the Atthasālinī to explain something that takes far less time than the story does: one act of seeing.The simile is commentarial, not canonical. The suttas describe the same ground more loosely: eye and form give rise to eye-consciousness, the three together are contact, and contact conditions feeling (MN 18). In the Abhidhamma’s account a single sight is a process, a vīthivīthi · cognitive process a fixed series of cittas taking one object at one door of seventeen mind-moments, each a citta with its own job. The man and his mango are a way of keeping them in order.

Asleep

Before anything happens, the man is asleep, and the mind is not doing nothing. It runs as the bhavaṅgabhavaṅga · life-continuum the resultant citta, of the same kind as rebirth-linking, that runs between processes, the life-continuum: a stream of identical resultant cittas that keeps a life going whenever no process is active. It is the same kind of citta that linked this life to the last, and it holds the same object it held then. It knows nothing of the mango tree.

Three moments of this stream belong to our process. In the first, the visible form of the mango has already struck the eye, but the stream flows on unchanged. In the second it vibrates; in the third it is arrested, and the stream stops. A sense object is matter, and a unit of matter lasts exactly seventeen mind-moments, so the clock has started.Why seventeen: the commentaries hold that matter arises and ceases seventeen times more slowly than mind. A strong object can therefore carry one complete process before it is gone. Weaker objects strike later and leave fewer moments; some never get past vibrating.

Waking and looking

The fourth moment is the man waking and turning toward the sound. In the process it is pañcadvārāvajjanapañcadvārāvajjana · five-door adverting citta 28: turns toward an object arriving at one of the five sense doors, adverting at a sense door: the mind turns to whatever has arrived. It is a kiriyakiriya · functional a citta that neither makes kamma nor results from it, such as adverting citta, merely functional. It does its work and leaves no trace.

The fifth moment is seeing itself, eye-consciousness. It is worth staying with, because it upsets an assumption. Seeing is a vipākavipāka · resultant a citta that is the ripening of past kamma; it makes no new kamma, a result of past kamma. The man did not choose to see the mango, nor whether the sight would be pleasant. Whether one meets a desirable sight or a repulsive one is the ripening of what was done before. The moment of seeing is the most passive moment in the process. It knows only colour.

Handling the fruit

Three more moments follow, and the story gives each a gesture. Taking the mango in his hand is receiving (sampaṭicchanasampaṭicchana · receiving the citta that takes up the object just after sense consciousness), which takes up the object just seen. Squeezing it is investigating (santīraṇasantīraṇa · investigating the rootless resultant citta that examines the object just received), which examines it. Smelling it is determining (voṭṭhapanavoṭṭhapana · determining the moment that defines the object; done by mind-door adverting, citta 29), which settles what the object is for this process.

Receiving and investigating are results again. Determining is functional, like adverting. None of them makes kamma. Everything so far has happened of itself, the way water finds its level.

But determining, with adverting before it, is where the commentaries locate the hinge. How attention turns there, wisely or unwisely, yoniso manasikārayoniso manasikāra · wise attention attending in a way that leads to wholesome javanas; unwise attention leads to unwholesome or its opposite, decides what kind of citta follows.The Guide says only that wise or unwise attention decides whether the javanas are wholesome or unwholesome; it places that attention at no single moment (Guide to III §13). The Atthasālinī names both moments: when adverting and determining both turn wisely, unwholesome javanas cannot follow, and when both turn unwisely, wholesome ones cannot.

Eating

Now the man eats the mango. These are the seven javanajavana · impulsion the active phase of a process, usually seven alike cittas; here kamma is made moments, moments nine to fifteen, and in the whole process they are the only ones in which the mind acts. Here, and only here, kamma is made.

The javanas are seven cittas of one kind, each strengthening the next. In our story they are greed with joy: a sense-sphere citta rooted in lobhalobha · greed grasping at an object; roots eight cittas; as craving, a root of the wheel, wanting the mango, liking it. They could as easily have been wholesome: the same ripe fruit seen with a generous mind, meant for someone else, or seen with clarity as colour, smell and taste arising and passing. The object does not decide this. The javanas do.

Cetanācetanā · volition the willing that organises the other factors; as kamma, it bears results, volition, arises in every citta, but in the javana it does what the word kamma names: it wills, and the willing has consequences. A habit is a long run of javanas of one kind. A character is what those habits have become.In an arahant the javanas are merely functional: they act, perceive, even smile, but they leave no result. This is one of the Abhidhamma’s most exact ways of saying what liberation changes.

Swallowing, and sleep

Two moments of registration (tadārammaṇatadārammaṇa · registration resultant cittas that hold the javanas' object after a strong object) follow when the object is strong: resultant cittas that hold the object the javanas held, like the last taste of the fruit as it is swallowed. Then the process is over, exactly as the matter that started it falls away, and the mind sinks back into the life-continuum. The man lies down and sleeps.

The swallowing and the sleep are not in the Atthasālinī. Later teachers, the Vibhāvinī-ṭīkā among them, added them (Guide to IV §6) so that the simile would cover every moment. It is a reminder that this is a teaching device, extended over centuries to fit an analysis, not a report of a man’s afternoon.

What the story leaves out

The man thinks “a mango”, and later “I ate a mango”. None of the seventeen moments contains that thought. The five-door process knows a colour, and the javanas want or do not want it. Recognising mango, naming it, placing oneself as the one who sees: all of that is done by later processes at the mind door, which take this one as their object.The atlas’s Plate 7b sets out these mind-door processes and how a sight becomes a named thing.

So “I see a mango” is true as convention and is built afterwards. In the moment itself there were cittas and their factors, an eye, and a colour. There was no one behind them.

And it was fast. The commentaries say that many thousands of millions of cittas arise and pass in the time of a lightning flash. It is the commentaries’ figure of speech for more than can be counted, not a measurement. The seventeen moments are not a slow story. They describe a structure: what must happen, in what order, for seeing to become wanting. The story is slow so that the structure can be seen.

Read slowly, the simile leaves a practical question. Most of what happens in seeing is given. A small part is not, and that part decides what continues. The Buddha’s instruction to Bāhiya, “in the seen there will be only the seen” (Ud 1.10), can be read as practice at exactly this point: letting the process complete without javanas of wanting.

The ideas in this reading

  • Cognitive process 17 mind-momentsThe fixed sequence in which cittas take an object.
  • Javana impulsionThe active phase of a cognitive process, where volition becomes kamma (except in an arahant).
  • Life-continuum bhavaṅgaThe quiet stream of mind that runs whenever nothing is actively being known, as in dreamless sleep.
  • Kamma volitionVolition, and the way it ripens.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The seeing was a result of past kamma; the wanting was new kamma. Can you find the seam between them in your own experience, the moment when a sight becomes a want?

One way to see it, after you have tried

The commentaries put the seam at adverting and determining, the fourth and eighth moments: as they turn to the object wisely or unwisely, so the javanas follow. In practice you will not catch a single moment. What you can notice is the gap between "this is seen" and "I want it", and how often the second has already happened by the time the first is noticed. Sense restraint works there.
