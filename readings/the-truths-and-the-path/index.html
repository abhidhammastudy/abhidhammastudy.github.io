The noble eightfold path

Eight factors named as the mental factors they are: scattered over many moments in ordinary practice, and together, in one citta, at the moment of the path.

About 9 minutesManual VII §30 with the Guide (the eight path factors as mental factors)

In brief

  • The noble eightfold path runs from right view to right concentration, and the Abhidhamma names each factor as a mental factor, such as wisdom or energy.
  • The suttas group the eight under three trainings, virtue, concentration and wisdom; the list begins with right view, the training with virtue.
  • In ordinary good moments the factors arise a few at a time, at different moments, each with its own object.
  • At the moment of the path they arise together in one citta that takes Nibbāna as its object: eight, or seven above the first jhāna level.
  • Right speech, action and livelihood are abstinences: not the kind word itself, but the holding back of the wrong one.
  • Everyday practice is the path in a looser sense; in the teaching of the noble truths it still counts under suffering, yet it leads to the noble path.

In the first sermon the Buddha begins not with the truths but with a way between two dead ends, indulgence in sense pleasure and self-torment. That middle way, he says, is the noble eightfold path (SN 56.11, sn56.11:2.4–3.4). Elsewhere he defines each of its eight factors, and the first of them closes a circle: right view is knowledge of suffering, its origin, its cessation and the way to its cessation (SN 45.8, sn45.8:3.1–3.2). The path begins by knowing the truths that The four noble truths set out, and it is itself the last of them.

The eight factors, one page each ›

This reading asks what the eight factors are, in the Abhidhamma’s terms, and when they are there.

Eight path factors, eight mental factors

Each path factor, maggaṅgamaggaṅga · path factors twelve: eight right factors and four wrong ones; the eight right ones in path consciousness are the truth of the path, is one of the fifty-two mental factors (Guide to VII §30). Right view is wisdom, understanding the four truths. Right intention is initial application, vitakkavitakka · initial application lays the mind onto its object; a factor of the first jhāna only, the factor that directs the mind onto its object, here directed toward renunciation, goodwill and harmlessness. Right speech, right action and right livelihood are the three abstinences, virativirati · abstinences right speech, right action, right livelihood; singly in mundane cittas, together in a path: not good speech, good deeds and a good trade, but the deliberate holding back from false, divisive, harsh and idle speech, from killing, stealing and sexual misconduct, and from wrong ways of living (II §6, Guide). Kind and true words are still wholesome; what the path factor names is the refraining. Right effort is energy, working as the four supreme efforts. Right mindfulness is mindfulness, as the four foundations. Right concentration is one-pointedness, which the suttas define by the four jhānas (Guide to VII §30; SN 45.8).

Eight factors, eight distinct mental factors. The count matters most at livelihood. If right livelihood were only right speech and right action done for money, the path would have seven factors, not eight.noteWhere teachers differed. The Atthasālinī (CS §301) gives livelihood its own work: killing or lying done for a living, as by a hunter or a bribed witness, is wrong livelihood, and abstaining from it is right livelihood; the same deeds done for sport are wrong action or wrong speech. The elder Mahāsīva drew the line elsewhere: for him wrong livelihood proper is getting one’s requisites by trickery, and the deeds themselves, for gain or not, are wrong action and speech. noteComparison: is right speech mental or material? For the Theravāda the abstinences are mental factors (II §6), and the Kathāvatthu argues against the thesis that the path is matter (Kv X.2). The thesis was held, so the commentary says, by the Mahīsāsakas, Sammitīyas and Mahāsaṅghikas (CS §§573–575). The Manual’s insistence that right speech is an abstinence, a cetasika, takes a side in an old debate.

Four senses of “path”

The word path does four different jobs in the Abhidhamma, and the claims of this reading hold only for the sense they are made in.

  1. The path condition. Here a path is whatever leads somewhere, to the happy states, to the woeful states, or to Nibbāna. The Manual counts twelve path factors: the eight right ones and four wrong ones, wrong view, wrong intention, wrong effort and wrong concentration (VII §17). Wrong view is the factor wrong view; the other three are initial application, energy and one-pointedness in an unwholesome citta. All four arise together in a citta rooted in greed and accompanied by wrong view (Dhs §§365–398). The suttas do speak of wrong speech, action and livelihood, and even of wrong mindfulness (MN 117, mn117:35.7–35.11). The Abhidhamma counts none of them as a path factor of its own. The first three are unwholesome conduct, not separate mental factors. Mindfulness is only ever beautiful (Guide to VII §17). The commentaries take “wrong mindfulness” as a name for the unwholesome mental states together, not for one factor (Papañcasūdanī on MN 8, CS §83; Ledi Sayadaw, Paramatthadīpanī, CS §163; commentarial). This is the sense of the path condition, one of the twenty-four conditions of Chapter VIII (VIII §11).
  2. The requisites of enlightenment. The eight right factors are the last of the seven sets of thirty-seven requisites, and there they may be mundane or supramundane (VII §§30, 33).
  3. The fourth noble truth. In the teaching of the noble truths the path is only the eight factors as they arise in the four supramundane path cittas, the lokuttaralokuttara · supramundane beyond the world: the four paths, four fruitions and Nibbāna moments of awakening (Guide to VII §38). So on this count the path factors of an ordinary good moment, mundane right view and the rest, belong with the truth of suffering: conditioned, of the round, still to be fully understood.
  4. The path citta. The whole citta of that moment, maggamagga · path (citta) a supramundane citta that arises once, cutting defilements, with Nibbāna as object. On the Manual’s strict count only its eight factors are the fourth truth; the citta itself and its other factors fall outside all four truths, and so do the fruitions that follow (VII §40 and Guide).

The first sense is the widest and the fourth the narrowest. The surprise is in the third: in the teaching of the noble truths, kind and mindful moments are not yet the path of the four truths, though they are the path in the second sense.noteNot every text counts so strictly. Ledi Sayadaw calls the mundane factors “path” in a looser sense (Paramatthadīpanī, CS §165).

Right view first, virtue first

The list starts from view, and MN 117 says why: right view comes first (mn117:4.1). It knows wrong view as wrong and right view as right, and so for intention, speech, action and livelihood. Each of those five is twofold, says the sutta: one kind is tainted, meritorious and ripens in further existence; the other is noble, taint-free and supramundane, a factor of the path (mn117:6.3–6.4). And beside each of those five run three others: right view, right effort and right mindfulness (mn117:9.3, 15.3, 21.3, 27.3, 33.3). One sees the wrong, strives to drop it, and stays mindful while one does.

The suttas also gather the path into three trainings, sikkhāsikkhā · training one of the three trainings, virtue, concentration and wisdom, under which the eight path factors are grouped. Speech, action and livelihood fall under virtue; effort, mindfulness and concentration under concentration; view and intention under wisdom. The nun Dhammadinnā puts it with care: the path is included in the three trainings, not the trainings in the path (MN 44, mn44:11.2–11.5).

The trainings are developed in the opposite order to the list. The Manual’s seven purifications start from purification of virtue (IX §28) and of mind, which is access and absorption concentration (IX §29), and the last five belong to wisdom (IX §22, Guide; Table 9.2). The first six are mundane; the seventh is the supramundane paths. Neither order is wrong. The list follows what leads; the training follows what supports what.noteThe commentary on MN 117 counts five kinds of right view: insight, the view that beings own their kamma, and the right views of the path, of the fruit, and of reviewing (Papañcasūdanī, CS §142). This is commentarial.

Before the path: the factors one at a time

In ordinary life the factors turn up a few at a time. The abstinences are the clearest case. On the Manual’s account a mundane abstinence arises only in a sense-sphere wholesome citta, and only on an occasion of actually refraining, when a chance to do the wrong is there (II §6 and §15, with the Guides). It arises singly: if you hold back from a lie, right speech is present and the other two are not. And it reaches only the wrong in view: refraining from taking this life is abstinence from killing, not from stealing (II §15, Guide). Mundane abstinences never arise in the sublime cittas of jhāna, whose object is the counterpart sign and offers nothing to refrain from, nor in an arahant’s functional cittas, since an arahant has no tendency left to hold back (II §15, Guide).

The other factors come and go in the same way. Giving with understanding has five: view, intention, effort, mindfulness and concentration, with no abstinence, since nothing is being refrained from. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī itself counts a five-factored path in that first wholesome citta of the sense sphere (Dhs §§58, 89). Without understanding there are four, and right view is missing (Dhs §147). A mundane second jhāna has four again, now without initial application (Dhs §161). A rootless citta, such as eye-consciousness, has none (VII §22).

The Manual sums this up in a line: in the mundane, the factors occur according to circumstances in the course of the six purifications (VII §33). The commentaries call this the preliminary stage of the path, pubbabhāga-maggapubbabhāga-magga · preliminary path a commentarial name for the path factors as developed in ordinary moments, before the supramundane path: before the path the factors arise at different moments with different objects (Sammohavinodanī, CS §205); one abstains from lying with one citta and from slander with another (Atthasālinī, CS §299). On the strict count it is not yet the fourth truth. It is how the fourth truth is approached.

At the path: all together, in one moment

At the path moment the scattered factors arise together, in one citta, and all of them take one object, Nibbāna (IX §34; Guide to I §§30–31). The three abstinences are there at once, and each works in full: right speech now cuts the disposition to every kind of wrong speech, right action to every wrong deed, right livelihood to every wrong livelihood (II §15, Guide). This is abstinence by eradication, samucchedavirati, the only one of the three kinds of abstinence that is supramundane (II §6, Guide).

How many factors that is depends on the jhāna the path corresponds to. A path at the level of the first jhāna has all eight. From the second jhāna up initial application is absent, and with it right intention, so the path has seven (II §19; Guide to VII §§32–33). One who reaches the path by bare insight, without jhāna, has a path at the level of the first jhāna (Guide to I §§30–31), and the commentaries draw the conclusion: such a path has all eight factors (Vism XXI §798; Atthasālinī, CS §350). For such a meditator the Guide counts momentary concentration (khaṇika-samādhi), as strong as access, as purification of mind (Guide to IX §29).noteWhere teachers differed: what fixes the jhāna level. The Guide reports three views of the ancient teachers: the jhāna used as a basis, the jhāna examined by insight, or, for one who has mastered several, the meditator’s wish (Guide to I §§30–31). The commentaries add their own answer: whichever view one follows, it is the final insight before the path that settles the factors (Vism XXI §797; Atthasālinī, CS §350).

Two or three moments of fruition follow the path (IX §34). They have the same factors, now as results, the moral purity the path has won (II §15, Guide). They are not the fourth truth. On the Manual’s strict count the fruitions fall outside all four (Guide to VII §40).noteEight or five? The canon counts two ways. The Vibhaṅga runs the supramundane path once as eightfold and again as five-factored, leaving the three abstinences among the “associated” states (Vibh 11, §§490–503). The Kathāvatthu, though, rejects the thesis that the path is five-factored (Kv XX.5); its commentary says the Mahīsāsakas held it, taking right speech, action and livelihood to be matter. The Sammohavinodanī reconciles the two: there is no five-factored supramundane path; the five-count shows the five factors that do the work of abandoning when one wrong is given up. A disputant who will not accept this, it says, should be sent off to drink his gruel (CS §493).

The path in practice

MN 117’s three strands can be read as a practice. Know the wrong as wrong and the right as right; that is view. Make the effort to drop the one and take up the other; that is effort. Do it mindfully; that is mindfulness. Most of the time you will meet the virtue factors in the precepts: the commentators call keeping them abstinence by undertaking, still mundane, and it happens only at the moment you actually hold back (II §6, Guide).

The tree of purification is this same path unfolding in time, from the roots of virtue to the crown of knowledge. In each path moment its factors stand together, for that one moment; at the fourth its work is complete.

The ideas in this reading

  • The noble eightfold path ariya aṭṭhaṅgika maggaEight factors, right view to right concentration. In the strict sense, eight mental factors arising together in the moment of a supramundane path.
  • The four noble truths ariya-saccaThe Buddha's four truths, which the Abhidhamma reads as a sorting of every mundane reality, with Nibbāna and the supramundane path set apart.
  • Cetasika mental factorsThe mental factors that colour each citta.
  • Calm samathaTraining the mind to rest on one object, from access concentration to the five jhānas, the meditative absorptions.
  • Path and fruition four pathsThe four moments of awakening that cut defilements for good, each followed by its fruition.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

On the Abhidhamma's analysis, as already in the suttas' definition (SN 45.8), right speech is not the kind word but the holding back of a wrong one. When did you last notice that moment of refraining, and what else was present in it?

One way to see it, after you have tried

On this analysis, most likely initial application turned toward harmlessness, with energy, mindfulness and one-pointedness, and perhaps wisdom, if you saw clearly what you were doing; and one abstinence, right speech, reaching only the one wrong you held back from (Guide to II §15). That makes five or six path factors in the moment. At the path all three abstinences come together and each works in full, cutting the tendency itself.
