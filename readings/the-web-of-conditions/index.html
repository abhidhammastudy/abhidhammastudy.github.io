The web of twenty-four conditions

The Abhidhamma's last book relates every dhamma to every other. How one thing helps another to arise, and why the help is never a cause acting alone.

About 8 minutesManual VIII §§11–28 (the Paṭṭhāna method

In brief

  • The Paṭṭhāna, the last book of the Abhidhamma, asks how one thing helps another to arise, and answers with twenty-four kinds of condition.
  • Conditions help in different ways: by being the object known, by making way as the moment just before, by arising together, by long habit, or as past volition.
  • The Manual sorts conditions by direction, mind to mind, mind to matter, matter to mind and more, so the help runs both ways between mind and body.
  • All twenty-four fold into four: being known, leaning on strongly, being the volition that ripens, and being there at the same time.
  • The Paṭṭhāna never names a single cause: everything arises from many conditions, and nothing produces another by itself alone.
  • Like two sheaves of reeds leaning on each other, the web leaves no agent outside it; what we call a person acting is the web seen from inside.

The suttas teach that things arise dependent on conditions. The PaṭṭhānaPaṭṭhāna the seventh Abhidhamma book: relates all dhammas by 24 conditions, the last and longest book of the Abhidhamma, asks how. In what way does one dhamma help another to arise or to continue? It answers with twenty-four kinds of paccayapaccaya · condition a dhamma that helps another to arise or persist, in a specific way, condition, and it applies them to every dhamma in every combination. The printed text fills several volumes.

The Manual condenses all this into a few pages, and a few pictures are enough to see what kind of thing a condition is.

Some of the twenty-four

A root condition is like the root of a tree: greed, hatred and delusion, or their opposites, make the states that grow from them firm and stable. An object condition is anything that a citta takes as its object; it helps simply by being known. A proximity condition is the citta that has just ceased, which makes way for the next, as a runner hands on a baton.

A conascence condition, sahajātasahajāta · conascence support between things arising at the same moment, like a lamp and its light, helps by arising together: a citta and its factors help one another into existence at the same moment, like a lamp and its light. Mutuality is the same help in both directions, like the three legs of a tripod. Support is help by being a basis, as the earth supports a tree, or the eye supports seeing.

Decisive support, upanissayaupanissaya · decisive support a strong earlier cause, such as a habit, a teacher or a climate, is the strong, often distant help: a good friend, a suitable climate, a past habit, a resolution made years ago. Repetition is the way each javanajavana · impulsion the active phase of a process, usually seven alike cittas; here kamma is made strengthens the next, like a lesson learned by recitation. Kamma condition is the help volition gives: at once, to the citta and matter it arises with, and across any span of time, as past kamma, to its results. And absence is the help a ceased state gives simply by being gone.noteSome conditions restate others. Absence and disappearance are proximity seen as making way; presence and non-disappearance are help by being there at the same time. Association and dissociation, by contrast, are two different relations: between mental states, and between mind and matter.

Six directions

The Manual sorts the conditions by direction, and that sorting shows what they are for. There are six directions:

  • Mind to mind: the previous citta to the next.
  • Mind to mind and matter at once: volition to the mental factors and to the matter it produces.
  • Mind to matter: later cittas sustaining a body that already exists.
  • Matter to mind: the eye supporting seeing.
  • Concepts, mind and matter to mind: anything at all as an object or a decisive support. (Concepts, such as names and ideas, have no nature of their own, yet they can be objects.)
  • Mind and matter to mind and matter: arising together, supporting, feeding, governing, being present: nine ways, from the great elements holding up derived matter to food sustaining the body; and once in a life, at conception, the citta and the heart-base holding each other up (VIII §18; §21).

Matter, in other words, is not only helped by mind, and mind does not only ride on matter. The relation runs both ways, by different conditions.

Four that hold them all

At the end of its account the Manual says that all twenty-four conditions can be folded into four: object, decisive support, kamma, and presence. Everything that helps anything does so by being known, by leaning on it strongly, by being the volition that ripens in it, or by being there at the same time.

Never a cause alone

The most important thing about the Paṭṭhāna is what it never says. It never names a single cause of anything. The Manual’s Guide says the same of dependent arising: a collection of conditions always gives rise to a collection of effects, and the formula only singles out the chief of each (VIII §3). Every dhamma arises from many conditions, and every condition helps in a particular way. No dhamma is self-produced, and none produces another by itself alone.

The suttas give a picture of this: two sheaves of reeds that stand because they lean on each other; take one away and the other falls (SN 12.67). The Paṭṭhāna makes that picture exact. And in doing so it leaves no place for an agent standing outside the web and pulling its strings. What we call a person acting is the web itself, seen from inside.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Name something you did today and look for more than one condition that helped it arise. Which of them was "you"?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Any act will have an object that drew it, a previous moment it followed, a support in the body, habits that leaned it one way, and the factors that arose with it. The Paṭṭhāna lists these as different kinds of condition. What we call "I did it" is a summary of that web, and no single strand in it is the doer.
