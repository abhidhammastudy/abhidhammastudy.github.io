Thirty-one planes

The worlds of the Theravāda cosmology, from the hells to the formless realms, and how each one corresponds to a kind of mind.

About 7 minutesManual V §§2–8 (the four planes), §§9–17 (four kinds of rebirth-linking, lifespans)

In brief

  • The Theravāda cosmology counts thirty-one planes of existence; a being is reborn into one, lives out its span there, and dies into another.
  • They form four tiers: four woeful planes, then the human world and six heavens, then sixteen fine-material Brahmā worlds, then four immaterial planes of mind alone.
  • Lifespans grow almost beyond imagining, up to eighty-four thousand great aeons, yet every one of them comes to an end.
  • Each plane corresponds to a kind of mind: a meditative absorption (jhāna) kept until death, for example, leads to its matching Brahmā world.
  • The tradition holds the planes to be real worlds; some modern teachers also read them as states of mind. On either reading the way out is not up the ladder but out of it.

The Abhidhamma inherited a cosmology and gave it a structure. There are thirty-one planes of existence, bhūmibhūmi · plane one of 31 planes of existence, each matched to a kind of mind, in four tiers. A being is reborn into one of them, lives out its span there, and dies into another. None of them is permanent, the highest included.

No single sutta lists all thirty-one; the count is a later synthesis of material scattered through the Canon.The suttas speak of five destinations: hell, the animal realm, the ghost realm, humans and gods (MN 12). The thirty-one planes refine this into a ladder. But the structure has a logic the suttas already imply, and the Abhidhamma makes it explicit: each plane corresponds to a kind of mind.

Four tiers

At the bottom are four woeful planes, apāyaapāya · woeful planes hell, animals, ghosts and titans: the four unhappy destinations: hell, the animal realm, the realm of hungry ghosts, and the realm of the asuras. Rebirth here is the result of unwholesome kamma.

Above them are seven happy planes of the sense sphere: the human world and six heavens of the devadeva a being of the six sense-sphere heavens, above the human worlds, the gods. Together with the woeful planes these make eleven sense-sphere planes, the worlds of the five senses and the desires that go with them.

Above the sense sphere are sixteen fine-material planes, the worlds of the BrahmāBrahmā a being of the fine-material planes, reborn there through jhāna gods. Beings are reborn here by the power of the jhānas. Among them is a plane of beings without perception, and five Pure Abodes, where only non-returners (those who have reached the third of the four paths) are reborn and from which they attain Nibbāna.

At the top are four immaterial planes, named after the four formless attainments: boundless space, boundless consciousness, nothingness, and neither perception nor non-perception. Here there is no body at all, only mind.

Time

The spans grow almost beyond imagining. In the Heaven of the Thirty-three, one divine day is a hundred human years, and a god lives a thousand divine years. In the fine-material planes lifespans are counted in aeons; in the highest immaterial plane, in eighty-four thousand great aeons, mahākappamahākappa · great aeon one cycle of a world's arising and passing; four incalculable aeons.A great aeon is described by simile: longer than it would take to wear away a mountain of solid rock a league high by brushing it with a cloth once a century (SN 15.5).

The plate draws the planes on a logarithmic scale of time, and the effect is dizzying. Even so, every bar on it ends. The longest life in the cosmos is a conditioned thing, and it ends like any other, when its lifespan runs out or its kamma is spent (V §34).

A place, and a state of mind

The key to the cosmology is the match between planes and cittas. A sense-sphere citta that makes kamma can lead to rebirth in a sense-sphere plane. A first-jhāna citta, if the jhāna has not been lost by the time of death, leads to the corresponding Brahmā world. A citta of the base of boundless space leads to that plane.

So the planes are worlds, but they are also a map of kinds of mind. Who is reborn where works out which citta opens a life in each. A human being in the course of a day can pass through states that resemble many of them: moments of hungry-ghost craving, of hellish anger, of heavenly delight, even, in deep meditation, of Brahmā-like calm. Read this way, and many modern teachers do read it so, what is visited in a moment can, made habitual, become a place to live.

Why it matters

The tradition takes the planes as real worlds, each reached by the kind of mind that matches it: a jhāna kept until death, for example, ripens as rebirth in its own Brahmā world (V §31 and Guide). Some modern teachers also read the planes as states of mind met within this life, the hells in hatred and the heavens in calm; that reading is theirs: the commentaries take even the sutta’s “hell of the six contact-bases” (SN 35.135) as a real hell. On either reading the teaching points in the same direction. Every plane is a result, and every result ends. The way out is not upward through the tiers but out of the ladder altogether, to what the planes do not contain.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The highest formless plane lasts eighty-four thousand great aeons, and still ends. What does the impermanence of even the longest heavens say about where to look for peace?

One way to see it, after you have tried

It says that no state reached by refining conditions can be final, because what is conditioned ends when its conditions end. That is why the path does not aim at a better world but at the unconditioned. The cosmology's vast spans make the point by exaggeration: even this ends.
