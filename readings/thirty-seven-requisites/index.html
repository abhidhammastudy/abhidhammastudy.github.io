Thirty-seven requisites, fourteen states

The Buddha gathered his teaching on practice into seven sets: thirty-seven items in all. The Abhidhamma counts what they are made of and finds fourteen states, with energy and mindfulness doing most of the work.

About 9 minutesManual VII §§24–33 with the Guide and Table 7.3 (the requisites and their fourteen states)

In brief

  • The Buddha gathered his teaching on practice into seven sets, thirty-seven qualities in all, called requisites of enlightenment because they lead to the paths.
  • The sets are the foundations of mindfulness, the supreme efforts, the means to accomplishment, the faculties, the powers, the factors of enlightenment and the eightfold path.
  • The Abhidhamma finds that the thirty-seven are only fourteen states, thirteen mental factors and consciousness itself, named again in different uses.
  • Energy serves nine times and mindfulness eight, wisdom five, concentration four and faith twice; the other nine serve once each.
  • At the moment of a path nearly all are present together; before it, they appear in different combinations according to the work in hand.
  • So no one has to cultivate thirty-seven things: develop mindfulness and energy, balance faith with wisdom and energy with concentration, and the rest follows.

Near the end of his life, the Buddha reminded the monks of what he had taught them to practise: seven sets of qualities, thirty-seven items in all (DN 16). The suttas occasionally call such qualities bodhipakkhiyā dhammābodhipakkhiyā dhammā · 37 requisites the suttas' 37 aids to awakening, which reduce to 14 states, the states on the side of awakening, or the requisites of enlightenment; later tradition made it the name for exactly these thirty-seven. They are requisites because they lead to the knowledge of the four paths (VII §24).

The noble eightfold path took the last of the seven sets on its own. This reading takes all seven, and then does what the Abhidhamma always does with a list: asks what it is made of.

The seven sets

Four foundations of mindfulness, satipaṭṭhānasatipaṭṭhāna · foundation of mindfulness mindfulness set up on one of four fields: body, feelings, states of mind, mental objects; together, right mindfulness: mindfulness set up on the body, on feelings, on states of mind, and on mental objects, such as the hindrances, the aggregates, the sense bases, the factors of enlightenment and the four truths (§24). It is one practice applied to four fields, and the Guide equates it with right mindfulness.

Four supreme efforts, sammappadhānasammappadhāna · supreme effort energy in four tasks: discard arisen evil, prevent unarisen evil, develop unarisen good, increase arisen good; together, right effort: the effort to abandon evil states that have arisen, to keep out those that have not, to bring about good states not yet arisen, and to increase those that have (§25). One mental factor, energy, doing four jobs; together they are right effort.

Four means to accomplishment, iddhipādaiddhipāda · means to accomplishment desire, energy, consciousness or investigation, when it leads the mind toward the goal of the teaching: desire, energy, consciousness and investigation (§26). They are the four predominants, adhipatiadhipati · predominance the one factor leading a citta: desire, energy, citta or investigation; or, as object predominance, an object held in high regard, the factors that can lead a whole citta, but they count as means to accomplishment only when they lead it toward the goal of the teaching.

Five faculties and five powers, indriyaindriya · faculty a dhamma that governs its own domain; 22 faculties, and condition 16 and balabala · power a state unshaken by its opposite; nine, among them faith, energy and wisdom: faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration and wisdom, twice (§27–28). As faculties they govern their own fields: deciding, exerting, noticing, steadying, discerning. As powers they are the same five, now too strong to be shaken by their opposites. The Guide adds the teaching on balance: faith with wisdom, so that faith is not blind and wisdom not merely clever; energy with concentration, so that the mind is neither restless nor sluggish; and mindfulness always, since it is never in excess.

Seven factors of enlightenment, bojjhaṅgabojjhaṅga · enlightenment factor seven: mindfulness, investigation of states, energy, zest, tranquillity, concentration, equanimity: mindfulness, investigation of states, energy, zest, tranquillity, concentration and equanimity (§29). Investigation, energy and zest lift a sluggish mind; tranquillity, concentration and equanimity calm an excited one; mindfulness watches both. Equanimity here is the mental factor of neutrality, not a neutral feeling.

Eight path factors, maggaṅgamaggaṅga · path factors twelve: eight right factors and four wrong ones; the eight right ones in path consciousness are the truth of the path: right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration (§30).

Fourteen states

Now the Abhidhamma’s move. Each of the thirty-seven is a mental state the earlier chapters have already counted, under a different name. Right view is wisdom. Right intention is initial application turned toward renunciation, goodwill and harmlessness. Right speech, action and livelihood are the three abstinences. The four foundations of mindfulness are one factor, mindfulness; the four supreme efforts are one factor, energy (§31). Collected, the thirty-seven are fourteen states: thirteen mental factors and consciousness itself (§32).

The counts are the point (§33). Energy serves nine times: four efforts, a means to accomplishment, a faculty, a power, a factor of enlightenment and right effort. Mindfulness serves eight times, wisdom five, concentration four and faith twice. The other nine serve once each: desire, consciousness, initial application, zest, tranquillity, equanimity and the three abstinences. The path, seen this way, is not thirty-seven separate virtues. It is a handful of qualities, named again at each stage and in each use.

Where they are found

At the moment of a path, all of them are present together, the Manual says, with two exceptions. Right intention is initial application, which the second and higher jhānas have left behind; zest drops away at the fourth jhāna. So a path citta of a higher jhāna lacks one or both (§33), while every bare-insight path, reached without jhāna, is at the first-jhāna level (Guide to I §§30–31), and so, by II §19, eightfold. Before that, in ordinary practice, the requisites appear in different combinations, according to the work in hand, through the six stages of purification that lead up to the path (see The tree of purification).

That is the practical lesson of the grid. No one has to cultivate thirty-seven things. Develop mindfulness and energy, balance faith with wisdom and energy with concentration, and the rest of the list fills itself in. The paths they lead to, and what each one cuts away, are followed in Four noble persons.

The ideas in this reading

  • Path and fruition four pathsThe four moments of awakening that cut defilements for good, each followed by its fruition.
  • Calm samathaTraining the mind to rest on one object, from access concentration to the five jhānas, the meditative absorptions.
  • Insight vipassanāThe knowledges that see mind and matter rise and fall.
  • Cetasika mental factorsThe mental factors that colour each citta.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Energy turns up nine times among the thirty-seven and faith only twice. In your own practice, which do you find you have to supply most often, and which comes on its own?

One way to see it, after you have tried

For most people it is energy that has to be renewed: the effort to drop what has arisen, to keep out what has not, to start and to continue. That is why it appears as four efforts, a means to accomplishment, a faculty, a power, a factor of enlightenment and a path factor. Faith appears less because once it is steady it does its work quietly, as a faculty and as a power. Mindfulness, the next most frequent, is the one the Guide says is never in excess: it keeps the others in balance.
