The twelve unwholesome cittas

Eight rooted in greed, two in hatred, two in delusion alone: what each label means, the scenes the old commentaries chose to show each one, and where the teachers disagreed about prompting.

About 11 minutesManual I §§4–7 with the Guides and Table 1.2 (the greed-, hate- and delusion-rooted cittas

In brief

  • Every unwholesome citta has delusion as a root; greed and hatred add themselves to it but never come together, which gives eight greed-rooted, two hate-rooted and two delusion-rooted cittas.
  • The greed-rooted eight differ by three switches: joy or neutral feeling, with or without wrong view, unprompted or prompted. The hate-rooted two differ only by prompting, and always come with displeasure.
  • The commentaries illustrate each citta with everyday scenes: the Visuddhimagga with a short standard set, the Atthasālinī with livelier ones, such as a crowd peering at dancers. The Guide's boy stealing an apple is a modern example, not the commentaries'.
  • 'Prompted' is not a moral grade. All the commentators agree that the push may come from oneself or from another; they differ on what the word names, and on whether the two delusion-rooted cittas are 'neither' or 'always unprompted'.
  • The restlessness citta is the only one of the twelve that cannot produce rebirth-linking (V §27); the Guide calls it the weakest, but the Atthasālinī counts it stronger than the doubt citta and gives another reason.

A map of the eighty-nine placed twelve cittas at the head of the sense sphere and called them unwholesome. This reading takes them one at a time. The Manual gives them in three short paragraphs and a verse (I §§4–7), Bodhi sets them out in Table 1.2, and the Dhammasaṅgaṇī, where they first appear, describes each at length (Dhs §§365–430). The labels are terse: “accompanied by joy, associated with wrong view, unprompted”. Each word in them does work, and it is worth knowing what before meeting the twelve.

What the labels mean

Rooted. A root, hetuhetu · root one of six factors that steady a citta: greed, hatred, delusion, or their opposites, is a mental factor that gives a citta stability; cittas without roots are the weaker for it (Guide to I §8). There are three unwholesome roots: greed, hatred and delusion. Delusion is in all twelve. Greed and hatred cannot stand in the same citta. So a citta is called after its most prominent root: eight are rooted in greed, two in hatred, and in two delusion stands alone (Guide to I §4). Greed here is any wanting, gross or faint; hatred, which the Manual names by its synonym paṭighapaṭigha · aversion “striking against”: the Manual's name for hatred in the two hate-rooted cittas, from rage to slight irritation; it always comes with displeasure, aversion, is any pushing-away, fierce or mild (Guides to I §§4–5).

Joy or neutral feeling. Every citta has a feeling. Joy, somanassasomanassa · joy pleasant mental feeling; it accompanies 62 of the 121 cittas, is pleasant feeling of the mind. The other option is upekkhāupekkhā · equanimity (feeling) neutral feeling, neither pleasant nor painful; in 55 of the 121 cittas, which the Manual’s translation calls equanimity; but here, the Guide notes, it names only neutral feeling, neither glad nor sad, and not the meditator’s even-mindedness (Guide to I §4). Greed can be cheerful or flat; hatred always comes with displeasure, domanassadomanassa · displeasure painful mental feeling; only in the two hate-rooted cittas; delusion alone is always neutral.

Accompanied by wrong view. Wrong view, diṭṭhi, can come with greed in two ways. It may give the wanting a reason, or be itself the thing held dear (Guide to I §4). The cittas “dissociated from view” are greed with no reason given.

Prompted. The last switch is prompting, sasaṅkhārikasasaṅkhārika · prompted arising after urging or persuasion, by oneself or by another. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī itself marks only the second, fourth, sixth and eighth citta of a set “with prompting”; it has no word for unprompted, and the Atthasālinī says the first is to be understood by contrast with the second (on Dhs §1). The Atthasālinī defines prompting as effort and means added to the same conditions that would otherwise produce the citta (on Dhs §146). The Visuddhimagga puts it as a prior effort, by oneself or by others, and gives the difference in two words: the unprompted citta is naturally keen and unurged, the prompted one sluggish and urged (Vism XIV §§452–453). The Guide adds that the push may work through the body, through words, or in the mind alone, as when one talks oneself into acting against reluctance (Guide to I §4).

Two consequences follow. Prompting is not a moral grade: nothing in these sources makes a prompted deed better or worse than an unprompted one. And the sluggishness is real: sloth and torpor can arise only in the five prompted unwholesome cittas, never in the unprompted, which are naturally keen (II §13 with the Guide).

Two sets of examples

The commentaries illustrate the cittas with scenes, and there are two sets. The Visuddhimagga gives a short standard one (Vism XIV §453), repeated almost word for word by Buddhadatta’s Abhidhammāvatāra and by the Vibhāvinī. The Atthasālinī, the commentary on the Dhammasaṅgaṇī, has a different and livelier set (on Dhs §§365–429). The Guide’s examples, a boy stealing an apple and a murder, are modern; they are not the commentaries’ (Guide to I §7). All are only illustrations. The citta is the state of mind, and the same state can show in many deeds.

The eight rooted in greed

1. Joy, with view, unprompted. The Visuddhimagga’s case: someone happily enjoys sense pleasures, keen of himself, holding that there is no harm in them, or trusts lucky sights and other auspicious signs as things of real worth (Vism XIV §453). The first is a view the suttas already know: MN 45 describes ascetics who say there is no harm in sensual pleasures. The Atthasālinī gives no scene here, but lists how such a view comes about: hearing false teaching, bad friends, not wishing to see the noble ones, unwise attention, a taste for omens and festivals (on Dhs §365).

2. Joy, with view, prompted. The Atthasālinī tells a story (on Dhs §399). A young man wants to marry a girl from a family of another faith. Her people refuse, until his relatives promise that he will do as they do. He goes with them to their teachers, doubtful at first, and in time comes to like their ways and takes up their view. The view arrived with help. The Visuddhimagga simply repeats case 1 with a sluggish mind that has to be urged.

3. Joy, without view, unprompted. The Visuddhimagga gives plain appetite: sexual indulgence, coveting another’s wealth, stealing another’s goods, with joy and keen of itself (Vism XIV §453). The Atthasālinī puts us in a crowd: watching shows, wrestling and dance festivals, or absorbed in pleasant sounds, without any view at all (on Dhs §400). Here, it adds, conceit may come in. The Manual agrees: conceit can arise only in the four greed-rooted cittas without view, though they need not have it (II §13 with the Guide).

4. Joy, without view, prompted. The same crowd, but now people are spitting and kicking dust about, and you have to work to keep watching, peeping between their heads; or the royal dancers come out, the crowd is pushed back, and you peer through the gaps (on Dhs §402). The pleasure is still there, but it takes effort.

5–8. The same four with neutral feeling. The Visuddhimagga says when they arise: when the sense objects are nothing special, or some other cause for joy is lacking (Vism XIV §453). Greed does not need delight. Much of it is flat: the habitual reach, the idle wanting. The Atthasālinī treats these four as the first four without joy and without zest (on Dhs §§403, 409–412).

One small difference between the sets: the Guide uses stealing for all eight greed-rooted cittas; the Visuddhimagga names stealing only among the cittas without view, though its sub-commentary also counts views that excuse some theft under the first (on Vism XIV §453).

The two rooted in hatred

9. Displeasure, aversion, unprompted. The Visuddhimagga says it arises in killing and the like when the mind is keen (Vism XIV §453); the Atthasālinī, that it arises in one who is displeased, as aversion to any of the six objects (on Dhs §413). The Guide’s example is a murder in a sudden fit of rage (Guide to I §7).

10. Displeasure, aversion, prompted. The Atthasālinī’s example is more telling (on Dhs §421). Someone is stirred up by others, or reminded of another’s offence; or, recalling it himself, grows angry. The last needs no one else. The Guide’s example for this citta is a murder after premeditation (Guide to I §7), which can suggest that “prompted” means “worse”; the commentary’s examples do not.

Displeasure and aversion always go together, but they are two things: displeasure is the feeling, aversion the attitude, and they fall in different aggregates (Guide to I §5). These two cittas never carry wrong view. A view may lead to hatred, but in an earlier citta (Guide to I §5). And they never take an object as predominant: an angry man, the Atthasālinī says, holds nothing in esteem (on Dhs §421). Greed can prize its object; hatred cannot. Envy, avarice and worry, when they arise, arise only here, one at a time (II §§13, 26).

The two rooted in delusion

In the last two cittas there is no greed and no hatred, only delusion, and the Manual calls these two momūhamomūha · sheer delusion delusion with neither greed nor hatred: the two delusion-rooted cittas, one with doubt and one with restlessness, “sheer delusion” (I §6 with the Guide). Their feeling is always neutral. The Guide’s reason is that a mind caught in doubt or agitation cannot form a clear liking or disliking of its object, so neither joy nor displeasure can arise (Guide to I §6).

11. With doubt. The Visuddhimagga says only: at the time of indecision (Vism XIV §453). The canon lists eight things one doubts: the Teacher, the Dhamma, the Saṅgha, the training, the past, the future, both, and dependent origination (Dhs §1008). The Guide’s example is doubting the Buddha’s awakening or the Dhamma as a way to freedom (Guide to I §7).

12. With restlessness. At the time of distraction, the Visuddhimagga says (Vism XIV §453): the mind cannot settle on anything (Guide to I §7). Restlessness is in all twelve unwholesome cittas, but only here is it the chief factor (Guide to I §6).

The Atthasālinī compares the two (on Dhs §§427–429). Restlessness wavers on one object, doubt between several. Both roll on their object, but the doubting citta rolls freely, while the restless one has a foothold, since it has adhimokkhaadhimokkha · decision resolves on the object; absent from doubt and from sense consciousness, decision, and doubt does not: like a round gem and a square gem let go on a slope. The restlessness citta is also the only one of the twelve that cannot produce rebirth-linking; the Guide explains this by its weakness. Any of the other eleven, as kamma, can bring rebirth in a woeful plane; the twelfth gives results only in the course of a life (V §27 with the Guide). The Atthasālinī gives its own reason: it is not among the states abandoned by the first path (after Dhs §429). It does not call the citta weak: with its foothold of decision it is the stronger of the two, and the sub-commentary to the Visuddhimagga names the doubt citta the weakest of all (on Vism XIV §453).Where teachers differ. On one point the commentators all agree: the push behind a prompted citta may come from oneself or from another (Atthasālinī on Dhs §146; Vism XIV §452; Vibhāvinī on I §4; Ledi, Paramatthadīpanī §22). They differ on what the word names. For the Atthasālinī it is the effort or means added to the usual conditions; for the Visuddhimagga, an earlier effort. The Vibhāvinī takes it as a keenness that the earlier effort gives the citta. Ledi Sayadaw objects that on that reading the prompted citta would be the keen one, the reverse of the teaching; for him the word qualifies the set of conditions the citta comes from. They also differ on the two delusion-rooted cittas, which carry no label (I §6). The Vibhāvinī and the sub-commentary on the Visuddhimagga say neither word fits: these cittas have no natural keenness, and no one sets out to arouse them. Ledi holds they are always unprompted, since they arise in beings of themselves, without effort (Guide to I §6; Vibhāvinī on I §6; sub-commentary on Vism XIV §453; Paramatthadīpanī §28). The Guide’s count for the sense sphere, 17 unprompted, 17 prompted and 20 neither (Guide to I §17), follows the Vibhāvinī; if the two delusion-rooted cittas are counted as unprompted, as Ledi holds, the figures become 19, 17 and 18 (our arithmetic).

Which path removes which

The twelve do not fall all at once. Stream-entry removes five for good: the four greed-rooted cittas with view, and the citta of doubt. Once-returning removes none, but weakens sensual desire and ill will. Non-returning removes the two hate-rooted cittas. Arahantship removes the last five: the four greed-rooted cittas without view, and restlessness (Guide to I §§26–28). The Atthasālinī pictures this as a king at war with twelve bandit chiefs, taking them in turn (after Dhs §362, closing its commentary on the wholesome).

So a stream-enterer can still want, cheerfully or flatly, with or without a push; what is gone is wanting backed by a view, and doubt. What an arahant lacks is not only greed with joy but the quiet, unjustified liking, and the faint restlessness that came with every unwholesome state. How the paths do this is told in Four noble persons.

None of this is a catalogue to memorise first. Each label names something that can be noticed: a want with a reason attached, a want without one; a pleasure that comes of itself, and one that has to be worked up; an irritation kept alive by going over it. The cittas that have no roots at all, and the beautiful ones, are the subject of Rootless and beautiful cittas.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

The Atthasālinī's example of the prompted hate-rooted citta includes someone who, with no one urging him, recalls an old offence and grows angry all over again. Why would the commentary count that as "prompted", when nobody else was involved?

One way to see it, after you have tried

Because prompting is about how the citta arises, not about who is present. The unprompted citta comes of itself, keen, from the conditions as they stand; the prompted one needs something added, an effort or a means, and that can be one's own. Going back over an injury is exactly such a means: the anger was not there of itself, so the mind works it up. All the commentators agree that the push can come from oneself or from another. It is worth noticing, because it means a resentment we keep alive by rehearsing it is, in this analysis, a prompted state, and the rehearsing is the prompt.
