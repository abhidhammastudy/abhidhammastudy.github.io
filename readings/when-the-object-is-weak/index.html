When the object is weak

Not every sight or sound gets the full seventeen moments. How late an object comes into range decides where the process stops: after registration, after javana, at determining, or before it has begun.

About 6 minutes

In brief

  • Not every sight or sound gets a full mental process: how late an object comes into range decides where the process stops.
  • The Theravāda tradition gives a unit of matter a life of seventeen mind-moments, and that clock runs whether the mind notices the object or not.
  • At the five sense doors the Manual names four grades of object, whose processes end after registration, after the javanas, at determining, or before they begin.
  • At the mind door the Manual asks only whether the object is clear or obscure; Ledi Sayadaw, much later, gave four grades there.
  • This account belongs to the later manuals and commentaries; the suttas and the canonical Abhidhamma books do not speak of seventeen moments or graded objects.
  • Kamma is made only in the javanas, where the mind acts; a course cut short before them makes none, and has no room for restraint.

You are reading. Somewhere in the street a door closes. A moment later you could not say whether you heard it or not. Or something moves at the edge of sight, and by the time you look there is nothing to look at.

A caution first. Any sound you could half notice was not one process but, on this account, a great many, at the ear door and the mind door by turns, many of them cut short (Guide to IV §12). What follows concerns single processes, far below anything you could notice. A half-heard sound is a run of courses in which few went the whole way.

The mango of the last reading fell beside the sleeper’s head, and nobody could have missed it. Most of what reaches the senses is not like that. The later Theravāda Abhidhamma tradition, its commentaries and manuals, has an account of the half-heard and the barely seen (whose words these are, a later section asks). It is the same account as before, cut short.

A clock that is already running

What is seen or heard (the visible form, the sound itself, not the experience of it) is classed as matter, rūparūpa · matter matter: the third reality, 28 kinds; as an aggregate it is called form, and the Theravāda tradition gives a unit of matter a fixed life: as long as seventeen mind-moments (IV §6). Other Abhidharma schools held matter to be as momentary as mind; the long life of matter is a Theravāda teaching. A mind-moment, cittakkhaṇacittakkhaṇa · mind-moment the life of one citta, in three sub-moments: arising, presence, dissolution, is the life of one citta, and the Manual divides it into three: arising, presence and dissolution (IV §6). The tradition gives no clock time for it, only the ratio, and the commentators say that billions of mind-moments pass in a flash of lightning (Guide to IV §6). Many modern readers take the numbers as a scheme for ordering rather than a measurement.noteThe sub-commentaries record dissent on both counts. Some held that matter lasts sixteen moments, not seventeen, and some, Ācariya Ānanda among them, denied that a citta has a moment of presence, since the Yamaka names only arising and dissolution. The Vibhāvinī rejects both views, and the Manual follows the seventeen and the three (Guide to IV §6).

The object does not wait for the mind. It arises, and its seventeen moments begin to run whether anything notices or not. Until it comes into range, the mind goes on as the bhavaṅgabhavaṅga · life-continuum the resultant citta, of the same kind as rebirth-linking, that runs between processes, the life-continuum: a stream of resultant cittas, one at a time, each holding the object taken at rebirth, that fills every gap between processes. It runs between processes, never beneath them, and stores nothing. Those moments are spent.

An object comes into range when its impact on the sense door is enough to disturb that stream. The tradition ties weakness and lateness together: a feeble impact comes into range late, and an object that arrives late has little life left and is too feeble to carry the process further (Guide to IV §5; Vibhāvinī on IV §8). So the grades rank the presentation of the object, not the thing presented. A loud sound can make a slight presentation if the ear is weak or the sound nearly over, and a sight can be slight in dim light; a keen organ and good light raise the grade. The grade measures the force of the impact, and it shows in how many cittas of the process arise (Guide to IV §5).

On the commentary’s reckoning the question then becomes one of arithmetic. How many moments have already gone when the object comes into range? Whatever is left is all the process has to work with, and two rules decide what fits: in the ordinary course javana runs seven times or not at all (IV §§6, 21), and registration twice or not at all.noteThe rule for registration was not uncontested: an older view allowed a single moment of it. The Vibhāvinī, following the Sammohavinodanī, rejects that view. The Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha (the Manual itself) calls the result the presentation of objects, visayappavattivisayappavatti · presentation of objects very great, great, slight or very slight: decides how far a process runs, and at the five sense doors it names four grades (IV §5). Bhikkhu Bodhi’s explanatory guide in the same book, the Guide, does the counting.

Four grades, four endings

Very great. One moment passes before the object is in range (moment 1), for matter can strike a sense door only once it has reached its stage of presence (IV §6). The stream vibrates (2) and is arrested (3). Then the full course runs: adverting, sense consciousness, receiving, investigating and determining (4–8), seven moments of javanajavana · impulsion the active phase of a process, usually seven alike cittas; here kamma is made (9–15), and two of registration, tadārammaṇatadārammaṇa · registration resultant cittas that hold the javanas' object after a strong object (16–17), where registration can occur at all (IV §§17–20). The object ends with the process (IV §6; Table 4.1). This is the mango.

Great. Two or three moments pass first. Everything runs as before up to the last javana, and then one moment is left, or none. Registration always takes two moments. With only one left, or none, it cannot happen, so it is skipped, and the mind sinks back into the life-continuum. The object and the sense door perish together (IV §7 and its Guide).

Slight. Four to nine moments pass first. Now the seven javanas do not fit, so, on the commentary’s reckoning, none arise. The process reaches determining, voṭṭhapanavoṭṭhapana · determining the moment that defines the object; done by mind-door adverting, citta 29; determining occurs two or three times in all, and the mind sinks back (IV §8 and its Guide). There has been a moment of ear-consciousness, in the technical sense, and no javana: nothing in the course likes, dislikes or understands.

Very slight. Ten to fifteen moments pass first. The object is close to its end when it arrives. The life-continuum vibrates twice and flows on. No citta of a process arises: no adverting, no ear-consciousness (IV §9 and its Guide; Table 4.2). The Manual calls this the futile course, moghavāra (IV §10).

Each possible number of moments gone is one case, so counted out there are fifteen: one very great, two great, six slight and six very slight (Guide to IV §10; Table 4.2). The strips show the first of each.noteThe Manual says only that one moment or several have passed (IV §6). The reasons (registration needs room for two moments, javana for seven) are the Vibhāvinī’s, and so are the counts of moments gone; Bhikkhu Bodhi’s Guide sets them out as fifteen cases (Guide to IV §§7–10; Table 4.2). The four endings are the four courses at the five doors: the course ending with registration, with javana, with determining, and the futile one (IV §10).

At the mind door

A thought or a memory strikes no sense organ, and its object need not be present at all: it may be past or future, or a concept, which stands outside time (III §17). The Manual does not count moments at the mind door. It asks only whether the object is clearvibhūta · avibhūta · clear and obscure an object's strength at the mind door; only a clear one gets registration or obscure. When it is clear, the stream vibrates and is arrested, mind-door adverting (manodvārāvajjanamanodvārāvajjana · mind-door adverting citta 29: opens mind-door processes, and does determining in five-door ones) turns to the object, seven javanas run, and two moments of registration follow (in the sense sphere). When it is obscure, the javanas run and registration does not (IV §12 and its Guide; Table 4.3).

That gives the mind door two grades where the sense doors have four. Ledi Sayadaw, the Burmese teacher who wrote some eight centuries after the Manual, held that it gives only the fullest case of an obscure object, and the Guide reports his fourfold version (Guide to IV §12). The Manual’s clear he calls very clear; the Manual’s obscure he calls clear. Below these he adds two: obscure, where mind-door adverting occurs two or three times and no javana follows, and very obscure, where the life-continuum only vibrates. Clarity, he adds, can come from either side: a prominent object can appear clearly to a weak mind, and a strong mind can clearly know a subtle object (Guide to IV §12). Everyday examples, ours and not his: a vivid memory is a clear object; a name on the tip of the tongue, turned to again and again and never taken up, an obscure one.

Whose words these are

None of this is in the suttas or in the canonical Abhidhamma books. They do not speak of seventeen moments or of graded objects.

The fourfold ladder (very great, great, slight, very slight) belongs to the later manuals, the Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha above all. The Visuddhimagga (XIV) already speaks of a great and a very great object, and of a clear one at the mind door; slight and very slight are not yet there. Buddhaghosa’s Atthasālinī speaks more loosely of a strong or a weak object, and of three futile rounds that a weak object produces: one in which the life-continuum is never turned from its course (our very slight), one that gets as far as determining and falls back (our slight), and one in which javana runs but no registration follows (our great). For the second it gives the report anyone will recognise: it is as if seen by me, as if heard by me.noteThe Vibhāvinī notices the difference in wording: the older commentary calls all three shortened courses futile, because they lack registration or javana; the Manual keeps the word for the last, where no process arises at all.

For the third, where javana runs but registration does not, it has a simile. A river is dammed and its water turned into a channel. The water fills the fields on both banks, and what is left over drains back to the river through the crab-holes. The river is the life-continuum, the dam is adverting, the channel is the process, the filling of the fields is javana. The draining back is the return to the life-continuum, with no registration in between.

Why it matters

The four endings are not equally important. What registration adds is slight: two resultant moments more. For kamma, the line that matters is the one between the great object and the slight one, because that is where javana drops out.

Kammakamma wholesome or unwholesome volition, which later ripens as results is made only in the javanas. Everything before them is a result or functional (Guide to III §8). So in the slight and very slight courses, and in Ledi Sayadaw’s two obscure ones, no kamma is made. No citta in them wanted the object or pushed it away; there was no room for one to, and no merit in it either.

The Visuddhimagga (I) puts restraint of the senses and its lack at the javana stage, though they are named after the door: guard the city gate and the whole city is guarded. So a slight course is not a restrained one; there was no room in it for restraint, or for its lack. Restraint belongs to the courses that run in full. And since, on Ledi Sayadaw’s account, a strong mind can know clearly what a weak one barely meets, the mind’s side of the grade is the side that practice can work on.

The ideas in this reading

  • Cognitive process 17 mind-momentsThe fixed sequence in which cittas take an object.
  • Javana impulsionThe active phase of a cognitive process, where volition becomes kamma (except in an arahant).
  • Life-continuum bhavaṅgaThe quiet stream of mind that runs whenever nothing is actively being known, as in dreamless sleep.
  • Kamma volitionVolition, and the way it ripens.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Think of something today that you only half heard or half saw. What was actually missing from it: the sound itself, or everything the mind usually does with a sound?

One way to see it, after you have tried

On this account the sound was there, and for much of the time the hearing was too. What fell away was the later part of the course: the javanas, in which the mind likes, dislikes or understands. On Ledi Sayadaw's account, as the Guide reports it, a sound is named only in the mind-door processes that follow the hearing (Guide to IV §12); with a half-heard sound, we may suppose, those fell away too. A half-heard thing is one the mind met and hardly acted on, and little kamma was made about it.
