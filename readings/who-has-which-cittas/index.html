Who has which cittas

The object decides what is seen; the person decides what is done with it. Which of the eighty-nine cittas can arise depends on how a being was reborn, what they have attained, and the world they live in.

About 9 minutes

In brief

  • In a process, the cittas that simply receive an object follow the object and are set by past kamma; the javanas, where the mind acts, follow the person.
  • The Manual grades sense objects as undesirable, moderately desirable or extremely desirable, judged by an average person and not by anyone's taste.
  • The citta a being is reborn with sets a ceiling: without wisdom among its roots, no meditative absorption (jhāna) and no path can arise in that life.
  • Attainment removes cittas: stream-enterers lose those with wrong view and doubt, non-returners those with aversion, and arahants every wholesome and unwholesome javana.
  • The plane removes more: fine-material beings have no aversion and no smelling, tasting or touching; the immaterial worlds have no matter and no sense-door process.
  • The point to carry away is the first: what comes with the object is given, and the javanas are where a person can work.

The mango and the moment follows one process of seeing. Chapter IV of the Manual ends with two questions. In any process, what depends on the object and what on the person? And which cittas can arise at all, for which beings, in which worlds?

Three grades of object

The Manual sorts sense objects into three grades (IV §17): the undesirable, aniṭṭhaaniṭṭha · undesirable of an object, undesirable; met through unwholesome kamma; the moderately desirable, iṭṭhaiṭṭha · desirable of an object, desirable (moderately, or extremely, ati-iṭṭha); met through wholesome kamma (also called iṭṭha-majjhatta); and the extremely desirable, ati-iṭṭha. The Guide insists this is a quality of the object, judged by an average person, and not of anyone’s taste: someone who finds a desirable object repugnant is misperceiving it.

Which grade one meets is the work of past kamma. An undesirable object comes through unwholesome kamma, so the cittas that simply receive it, the sense consciousness, receiving, investigating and registration, are all unwholesome results. A desirable object comes through wholesome kamma, and the same positions are filled by wholesome results; an extremely desirable one even brings investigation and registration with joy. These cittas match the object without deliberation, the Guide says, as a reflection in a mirror matches a face.

The javanas do not. They follow the person. Faced with something extremely desirable, one person’s javanas run with greed and another’s with calm equanimity; faced with a corpse, a meditating monk’s run with knowledge and joy. The object sets the results; the response is made in the javanas.

When registration happens

Registration, the two moments after the javanas that hold their object a little longer, does not always come. It happens only after sense-sphere javanas, only to beings of the sense sphere, only when the object is of the sense sphere (IV §19), and only when the object is very great at a sense door or clear at the mind door (§20).

There is a further rule about feeling. A citta with joy cannot follow one with displeasure directly, nor the reverse. Suppose javanas of displeasure end in someone whose life-continuum is joyful, and no registration comes to bridge them. Then, the teachers say, a single investigating citta with equanimity arises on some familiar object, only to pave the way back. They call it the adventitious life-continuumāgantuka-bhavaṅga · adventitious life-continuum an equanimous investigating citta that bridges displeasure in the javanas and a joyful life-continuum (§18).

Who you are reborn as

The first fact about a person, for the Abhidhamma, is the citta they were reborn with, since it runs all life long as the life-continuum (IV §§24–26). There are three kinds of rebirth:

  • Rootless: by an investigating citta with equanimity. In the woeful planes every rebirth is of this kind; in the happy ones it means a weakened birth.
  • Two-rooted (duhetukaduhetuka · two-rooted a being reborn by a great resultant citta without knowledge; jhāna and the path are closed to them in that life): by a great resultant without knowledge.
  • Three-rooted (tihetukatihetuka · three-rooted a being reborn by a great resultant citta with knowledge; every attainment is open to them): by a great resultant with knowledge.

The roots of rebirth set a ceiling. For the rootless and two-rooted, no jhāna and no path can arise in that life, however they practise: absorption needs a three-rooted base (§24). The three-rooted may become anything. Then attainment changes the set of cittas a person can have. Stream-enterers lose the four cittas with wrong view and the one with doubt; non-returners lose the two with aversion; arahants lose every wholesome and unwholesome javana, and act only through functional cittas (§25).

The Manual’s own summary is simpler: those beyond training, the arahants, experience forty-four kinds of citta; trainees, the other noble persons, fifty-six (counting the three lower fruitions together and adding the four paths), and everyone else fifty-four (§26).The Guide fills in the rest: worldlings in the woeful planes, thirty-seven; the rootless and two-rooted in happy planes, forty-one; stream-enterers and once-returners, fifty; non-returners, forty-eight. Each figure is a maximum; someone who lacks a jhāna lacks its citta too.

Where you are

The plane removes more (IV §§27–29). In the fine-material worlds there are no cittas of aversion, suppressed in the training that led there, and no registration; and since those beings have no working nose, tongue or body, the consciousnesses of smell, taste and touch never arise. In the immaterial worlds there is no matter at all: no sense-door process, no smile, no fine-material jhāna, no immaterial jhāna lower than the one that brought the being there, and no first path.

So, of the eighty-nine, eighty can arise in a process in the sense sphere, all but the nine sublime resultants, which only ever serve as rebirth and life-continuum. Sixty-four can arise in the fine-material worlds, and forty-two in the immaterial (§29). Two special cases complete the picture (§28): a being without an eye has no eye-door process at all, and the non-percipient beings have no process of any kind.

The point to carry away is the first one: results come with the object, and the javanas are where a person can work. The highest attainments, and what each path removes, are followed in Four noble persons; how kamma decides the rebirth that starts all this, in From kamma to its results.

Reflect and discuss

Opening question

Two people see the same beautiful thing. The Abhidhamma says their seeing is the same kind of citta, but their javanas may be utterly different. What, in your own experience, belongs to the seeing, and what to you?

One way to see it, after you have tried

The seeing, and how pleasant or unpleasant the object is, come with the object: they are results, set by past kamma, and they do not ask your opinion. What comes after is yours: whether you want it, resent it, compare it, or simply let it be known. Two people can stand before the same sunset with the same resultant cittas and part with opposite javanas. That is why the tradition puts the training in the javanas, and why it says the object does not make anyone greedy.
