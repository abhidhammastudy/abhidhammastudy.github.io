Pictures for hard ideas

The Buddha taught mostly in pictures: a fire that lives on its fuel, a handful of leaves, a lute with no sound inside it. The commentators added many more. Here are the ones that make the hardest ideas of the Abhidhamma easy to see, each retold in plain words from its source, with what it shows and where it stops working.

Two truths

The king who wanted the sound of the lute

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · SN 35.246

What looks like one thing is many parts working together. Searched down to the ash, the lute gives up no sound to be found anywhere in it; the monk who searches his aggregates the same way is left with no "I", "mine" or "I am".

A king who had never heard a lute heard one played and was enchanted. "What is that sound?" he asked. "That is a lute, Your Majesty." "Bring it to me." They brought him the lute.

"I have no use for the lute," said the king. "Bring me that sound." They told him a lute is made of many parts: a body, a skin, a neck, the bindings, the strings, a plectrum, and the right effort of the player. Only with all of these together does it give its sound.

The king split the lute into ten pieces, then a hundred, then splinters, burned them, and let the wind or a fast river carry the ashes away. "A poor thing, it seems, this lute," he said, "and so is anything called a lute. Yet people are so careless about it, and so taken in."

In the same way, the Buddha said, a monk searches into form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness, as far as each of them reaches. When he searches like that, whatever he had of "I", "mine" or "I am" is no longer there for him.

Where the picture stops working: The player can look like the one who makes the music happen. He is one condition among the others. And the smashing is not the point: in the commentary the king breaks the lute to find the sound, while the monk only examines. The Visuddhimagga takes the lute further, for impermanence: the sound was not stored anywhere before it sounded and goes nowhere afterwards; it comes to be from the lute, its fittings and the player's effort (Vism XX, CS §723).

Told in: SN 35.246, the simile of the lute; Sāratthappakāsinī (commentary to the Saṃyutta) on SN 35.246, CS §246: the key; Visuddhimagga XX, CS §723: the lute's sound, from nowhere and to nowhere

The teacher who speaks every dialect

From the commentaries · Commentary to the Aṅguttara Nikāya (Manorathapūraṇī), on AN 1.170

The two truths are two ways of speaking, both used by the Buddha, chosen to suit the listener. Neither is a lie.

There was once a teacher who explained the meaning of the three Vedas and was skilled in the languages of many regions. To pupils who spoke Tamil he explained them in Tamil; to those who spoke the Andhra tongue, in theirs. Each pupil heard the teaching in words he already knew, and learned it quickly.

The commentary says the Buddha is like that teacher. Some listeners can understand and make progress when he speaks of persons and beings, women and men, gods and Māra; for them he uses the ordinary words of the world. Others can do so when he speaks of impermanence, suffering and not-self, of the aggregates, the elements and the bases; for them he speaks in ultimate terms.

The commentary adds three verses: the best of speakers taught two truths, the conventional and the ultimate, and there is no third; conventional speech is true by the world's agreement, ultimate speech by the real nature of things; so the Teacher tells no lie when he speaks the world's way. It then gives eight reasons why he speaks of persons at all.

Where the picture stops working: It can make the two ways sound like two languages for one and the same content, each as good as the other. In the Abhidhamma the ultimate way of speaking has its own content, the dhammas themselves, which ordinary speech does not name (CMA I §2 and Guide). Ordinary speech is not false, but it is not the same teaching in other words.

Told in: AN 1.170, the one person born for the good of the world; Manorathapūraṇī on AN 1.170, CS §170; the same simile in Papañcasūdanī on MN 5, CS §57

Citta

A wheel touches the ground at one point

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · Visuddhimagga VIII, CS §176, quoting verses from the Mahāniddesa

In the ultimate sense a life lasts one mind-moment at a time. The past moment is gone, the next has not come, and only the present moment is lived.

Watch a chariot wheel. Whether it is rolling or standing still, it touches the ground at only one point of its rim. In the same way, the Visuddhimagga says, a being's life, in the ultimate sense, lasts only as long as a single moment of consciousness; when that moment ceases, the being is said to have ceased.

The wheel is the Visuddhimagga's own picture. It goes on to quote a passage and verses from the Mahāniddesa, in the canon. In a past moment of mind one lived, but does not live now and will not live. In a future moment one will live, but does not live now. In the present moment one lives, and did not live before and will not live after. Life, selfhood, pleasure and pain are all joined to that one moment of mind, and it passes swiftly. The aggregates that have ceased, in one who dies or in one who lives on, are all alike: gone, with no relinking. With the breaking up of the mind the world is dead; that is how it is spoken of in the ultimate sense.

The Visuddhimagga gives this as one of the ways of recollecting death: the shortness of the moment.

Where the picture stops working: The wheel itself lasts while it rolls from point to point. The Visuddhimagga's point goes further than its picture: there is no lasting wheel underneath the moments, only one moment after another. And it measures life by the mind: in the Abhidhamma a material phenomenon lasts seventeen mind-moments (CMA IV §6 and Guide).

Told in: Visuddhimagga VIII (recollection of death, the shortness of the moment), CS §176; Mahāniddesa, on Sn 775: the passage and verses on the moment (Pāli)

Cognitive process

The deaf doorkeeper and the king's gift

From the commentaries · Atthasālinī, the commentary on the Dhammasaṅgaṇī, CS §498

In a cognitive process each moment does one small job and passes the object on. Only the javana moments experience its taste.

A king lies asleep in his chamber. A servant sits rubbing his feet. At the door stands a doorkeeper who is deaf, and three attendants stand in a row.

A man from the borderland comes with a gift and knocks. The deaf doorkeeper hears nothing. The servant rubbing the king's feet gives him a sign, and the doorkeeper opens the door and looks. The gift is taken by the first attendant, who passes it to the second, who passes it to the third, who hands it to the king. The king enjoys it.

The Atthasālinī, the commentary on the first book of the Abhidhamma, gives the key. The knock is the object striking the eye's sensitive matter. The servant's sign is the adverting moment, which turns the life-continuum, the mind's resting state, towards the object. The deaf doorkeeper opening and looking is eye-consciousness: it sees, and does nothing else. The three attendants are receiving, investigating and determining. The king enjoying the gift is javana: only javana experiences the taste of the object.

Where the picture stops working: A king at the end of a line of servants suggests someone in charge, a self who finally gets the gift. There is no one: each moment arises from its conditions, among them the moment before, does its work and passes away (CMA IV §6 and Guide). The picture also stops at the king; the two registration moments that can follow javana are not in it. Nor does it speak of kamma. That javana is where wholesome and unwholesome kamma is made comes from elsewhere in the Manual (Guide to CMA III §8); in an arahant the javanas are only functional and make none (CMA III §13 and Guide).

Told in: Atthasālinī, on the great resultant cittas (Vipākuddhārakathā), CS §498; CMA IV §6 and Guide: the eye-door process

Dependent origination

A fire that lives on its fuel

From the canon · SN 12.52, 12.53 and 12.55

The chain of dependent origination goes on only as long as it is fed, and it ends when the feeding stops.

Imagine a great bonfire, as big as ten, twenty, thirty or forty cartloads of wood. Someone keeps throwing on dry grass, dry cowdung and dry wood. Fed like that, the fire burns for a very long time.

The Buddha says it is the same with a person who keeps looking for satisfaction in the things that can be clung to. In that person craving grows. Craving leads to clinging, clinging to becoming, becoming to birth, and birth to ageing, death, sorrow and pain. The whole mass of suffering burns on. (In these discourses the chain is told from craving onwards, not through all twelve links.)

Now imagine that no one adds any more fuel. When what it had is used up, the fire goes out. In the same way, when a person keeps looking at the danger in what can be clung to, craving ceases, and with it clinging, becoming, birth, and the whole mass of suffering.

In the next discourses the Buddha tells the same story with an oil lamp that burns as long as oil and wick are added (there he speaks of the things that fetter, rather than those that can be clung to), and with a great tree that lives as long as its roots draw up sap, and ends when it is cut down, uprooted, burnt and its ashes scattered.

Where the picture stops working: One fire and one fuel can make it look as if the round had a single cause. In the Abhidhamma no effect has a single cause: the formula names only the chief condition among many (Guide to CMA VIII §3). The commentary also reads the picture its own way: the fire is the round of the three planes, the fuel is kamma done through the six doors by way of craving, and the fire going out is an arahant's final passing away (SN commentary, CS §52).

Told in: SN 12.52, the great fire; SN 12.53, the oil lamp; SN 12.55, the great tree; Sāratthappakāsinī (commentary to the Saṃyutta) on SN 12.52, CS §52

The old road to the lost city

From the canon · SN 12.65

The eightfold path is found, not invented. Buddhas before walked it, and it had only to be found again.

A man wandering through a forest comes on an old road, the kind people used long ago. He follows it, and it leads him to an ancient city where people once lived, with parks, groves, ponds and walls, still lovely, though no one lived there any more.

He goes to the king and tells him what he has found. The king has the city rebuilt. In time it is full of people again, prosperous and busy.

The Buddha said his own discovery was like that. He had found the old road that the buddhas of the past had walked: the noble eightfold path, from right view to right concentration. Following it, he came to know ageing and death, where they come from, their ending, and the way to their ending; and then each link of the chain the same way, back to the formations. He made it known, and the holy life spread among many people.

Where the picture stops working: Reading the city as Nibbāna, as the commentary does, can make Nibbāna sound like a place one walks into. It is not a place. What the picture shows is that the way to it was walked before and can be walked again.

Told in: SN 12.65, the city; Sāratthappakāsinī (commentary to the Saṃyutta) on SN 12.65, CS §65

Whose child is this?

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XIX, CS §§678–679

Having seen mind and matter, one asks where they come from, as naturally as one asks whose a baby found lying in the street is. The answer is a family of conditions, neither a creator nor chance.

The Visuddhimagga says the meditator who wants to overcome doubt sets about it like a good physician who sees a disease and looks for where it came from; or like a kind man who sees a tiny baby lying on its back in the street and wonders whose child it can be, and thinks about its mother and father. So he looks for the causes and conditions of this mind and matter.

First he reasons that it is not without a cause, for then it would be the same everywhere, always, for everyone. Nor is it made by a Lord or anything of the kind, for there is nothing of that kind over and above mind and matter. So it must have causes and conditions. What are they?

He begins with the body, which, the sub-commentary says, is the coarser and plainer of the two. This body is not born inside lotuses or water lilies, or among gems and strings of pearls, but in a cramped and foul place between the stomach and the bowel, with the wall of the belly behind it and the backbone in front of it, like a worm in rotten fish. Ignorance, craving, clinging and kamma are its causes, because they produce it, and nutriment is its condition, because it supports it. Of these, ignorance, craving and clinging stand to the body as a mother to her child, as its decisive support; kamma as a father, who begets it; and nutriment as a nurse, who keeps it going.

For mind he uses another method, the sutta's: dependent on the eye and forms, eye-consciousness arises, and so on for each door.

Where the picture stops working: Parents are persons who outlive the birth; conditions are dhammas, and the kamma that begot this body ceased long ago. The mother, father and nurse are given for the body. Ignorance and the rest condition mind too, the sub-commentary notes, but the Visuddhimagga traces mind's conditions by another method. And a child is someone, while here there is no one besides the mind and matter whose conditions are sought (CMA IX §30 and Guide). The unclean birthplace is not decoration: the sub-commentary says it is there to turn the mind from delight in the body. The Visuddhimagga goes on to call one who has this knowledge a "lesser stream-enterer", who has found comfort and a footing in the Dispensation and is sure of his destination (XIX §691); the sub-commentary explains that destination as a good rebirth, so long as this knowledge is not lost. That is the commentary's honour for a worldling, not the path.

Told in: Visuddhimagga XIX (purification by overcoming doubt), CS §§678–679; the lesser stream-enterer, §691; SN 12.43, dependent on eye and forms (the method for mind); Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XIX §§679, 691; CMA IX §31 and Guide: purification by overcoming doubt

Kamma

A lump of salt in a cup, and in the Ganges

From the canon · AN 3.100

Kamma is not a fixed price. The same deed ripens differently depending on the person who did it and what else they have become.

Suppose someone said that however a person acts, he feels the result in just that measure. If that were so, the Buddha said, there would be no point in living the holy life, and no chance of ever ending suffering. But if a deed is felt in the way it is fit to be felt, then the holy life has a purpose, and suffering can be ended.

Take two people who do the same small bad deed. The first has not developed body, virtue, mind or wisdom; he is a small person, with little to him, and lives in misery. For him that small deed leads to hell. The second has developed all of these; he is not small but great, and lives without measure. For him the same deed is felt here, in this life, and not the least trace of it shows, let alone more.

It is like a lump of salt. Drop it into a small cup of water and the water can no longer be drunk. Drop the same lump into the river Ganges and the river is as drinkable as before.

In the same discourse the Buddha gives two more pictures. A poor man may be put in chains over half a coin, a coin or a hundred, where a rich man would not be. And a sheep-owner or butcher may beat, bind or fine a poor man who steals a sheep from him, but not a rich man, a king or a king's minister: him he can only beg, with joined hands, to give the sheep back or pay for it.

Where the picture stops working: It can sound as if a good life washes bad deeds away. It does not: in the sutta the small deed is still felt. What changes is how far it reaches, because results depend on many supporting and obstructing conditions (CMA V §§18–20 with the Guide). The commentary takes the developed person to be an arahant, who has no later life in which an old deed could ripen, but who still meets the results of past deeds in this one (AN commentary, CS §101).

Told in: AN 3.100 (AN 3.99 in the PTS numbering), the lump of salt; Manorathapūraṇī (commentary to the Aṅguttara) on this sutta, CS §101

31 planes

Where the road leads

From the canon · MN 12

A plane of rebirth is not a punishment or a prize handed out later. It is where the road a person is already on leads.

The Buddha told Sāriputta that he could look at a person's mind and see where their way of living would take them, as clearly as a man with good eyes can see where a traveller, worn out by the heat and thirsty, will arrive by the road he is on.

One road leads to a pit of glowing coals, without flame or smoke: so the Buddha sees a person heading for hell, with nothing but pain. Another leads to a pit full of filth: rebirth as an animal. Another leads to a tree on rough ground, giving thin and patchy shade: the realm of ghosts, with much pain. Another to a tree on level ground, in thick shade: the human world, with much pleasure. Another to a fine house with a shuttered room and a well-made couch: the heavenly worlds, with nothing but pleasure.

The last road leads to a lotus pond, clear and cool, with a pleasant grove beside it, where the traveller bathes and drinks and sits at ease. That one, the Buddha said, is freedom: the person who, here and now, has destroyed the taints.

Where the picture stops working: The sutta's five destinations are broader than the Manual's thirty-one planes: its "gods" are not divided into sense-sphere heavens, Brahmā worlds and formless worlds, and there are no titans (asuras), whom the Manual counts among the woeful planes (CMA V §4). And the sixth picture is not a place to be reborn in; it is the ending of rebirth.

Told in: MN 12, the greater discourse on the lion's roar

The four noble truths

A handful of leaves

From the canon · SN 56.31

Why the four noble truths sit at the centre: out of everything the Buddha knew, he taught what leads to freedom.

The Buddha was staying in a grove of rosewood trees near Kosambī. He picked up a few leaves and asked the monks: which are more, the leaves in my hand, or the leaves on the trees of this grove? The monks said the leaves in the grove were far more.

In the same way, he said, the things he had come to know but had not taught were far more than the things he taught. He left the rest untaught because it did not help: it did not lead to turning away, to peace, to awakening.

And what had he taught? This is suffering; this is its origin; this is its ending; this is the way to its ending. Those few he taught, because they help: they lead to turning away, to peace, to awakening and to Nibbāna. That, he told the monks, is what to work at.

Where the picture stops working: Read carelessly, it seems to put all detailed analysis, the Abhidhamma included, among the leaves of the grove. This site reads the Abhidhamma the other way, as a closer look at these same four truths, which the Manual itself sets out among its categories (CMA VII §§38–40).

Told in: SN 56.31, the rosewood leaves

Calm

The toddler and the strong man

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga IV, CS §58

The difference between access concentration and full absorption (jhāna).

Lift a small child and stand it on its feet, and it falls down again and again. A strong man can get up from his seat and stand all day long.

The Visuddhimagga says concentration grows like that. In the stage called access, the factors of jhāna have arisen but are not yet strong. The mind takes the meditation sign as its object for a while, then drops back into the life-continuum, the mind's resting state, then takes up the sign again: the child standing and falling.

In absorption the factors are strong. Once the mind has cut through the life-continuum, it can go on in one unbroken run of wholesome moments for a whole night or a whole day: the strong man standing.

Where the picture stops working: The strong man fits absorption once it is mastered: in a beginner's first absorption the jhāna javana runs only once before the mind falls back into the life-continuum, and only with practice does the run grow longer (CMA IV §22 and Guide). And the man stands by his own strength, while the run of javanas is held up by no one: each javana is a condition for the next by repetition (CMA VIII §13).

Told in: Visuddhimagga IV (the earth kasiṇa), CS §58; CMA IV §14 and Guide: the absorption process; IV §22 and Guide: javana in attainments; IX §§17–18: access and absorption

The muddy lake and the clear lake

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · AN 1.45–46

Only a mind that is not clouded can see. Purification of mind is for the sake of seeing.

Suppose a pool of water is cloudy, stirred up and muddy. A man with good eyes stands on the bank, but he cannot see the shells, the gravel and pebbles, or the shoals of fish, moving or at rest, because the water is clouded. In the same way, the Buddha said, a monk whose mind is clouded cannot know his own good, or the good of others, or of both, or reach any knowledge and vision beyond the ordinary that is fit for the noble ones.

Suppose instead the pool is clear, settled and unclouded. The same man on the bank sees the shells, the gravel and pebbles, and the fish, moving or at rest. So a monk whose mind is unclouded can know his own good and others', and reach that knowledge and vision.

The commentary on the Aṅguttara says what clouds the mind: it is wrapped in the five hindrances; the clear mind is one freed from them. The knowledge and vision fit for the noble ones it takes to be the divine eye, insight knowledge, the path and fruition, and reviewing. The Vibhāvinī, the commentary on the Manual, defines purification of mind in the same terms: it is so called because it cleans the mind by freeing it of the hindrances.

Where the picture stops working: Water clears if it is left alone; a mind is cleared by effort, and by the right kind of attention. Nor does the picture say how clear is clear enough. The Manual makes purification of mind access or absorption concentration (CMA IX §29); the Guide adds that for one who takes the vehicle of pure insight it is momentary concentration, steady enough to count as access. Tying the two lakes to this purification is the site's reading, through the Vibhāvinī's definition; neither the sutta nor its commentary speaks of the purifications. And a clear lake shows what is there only to someone who looks: the seeing is the work of the purifications that follow (CMA IX §§30–34).

Told in: AN 1.45 and 1.46, the clouded and the clear pool (an1.45:1.1–1.6, an1.46:1.1–1.6); Manorathapūraṇī (commentary to the Aṅguttara) on AN 1.45–46, CS §§45–46; Vibhāvinī on CMA IX §29 (CS §50): purification of mind as freeing the mind of the hindrances; CMA IX §29 and Guide: purification of mind

Insight

The tree without its branches and leaves

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · AN 5.24

Each step of the path is the supporting condition of the next, beginning from virtue. Damage the branches and leaves, and nothing inside the tree grows to fullness.

In one who lacks virtue, the Buddha said, right concentration has lost its support. Without right concentration, the knowledge and vision of things as they really are has lost its support; without that, disenchantment and fading away; and without those, the knowledge and vision of release.

It is like a tree whose branches and leaves are damaged: its outer bark does not grow to fullness, nor its inner bark, nor its sapwood, nor its heartwood.

In one who has virtue it is the other way round. Each step has its support, as in a tree whose branches and leaves are whole the outer bark, the inner bark, the sapwood and the heartwood all grow to fullness. The same tree comes back in other suttas of the Aṅguttara, with longer chains of steps.

The commentary on the Aṅguttara reads the chain as the path of insight. The knowledge and vision of things as they really are is tender insight, beginning from the knowledge that defines mind and matter. Disenchantment is strong insight. Fading away is the path. The knowledge and vision of release is fruition, together with the reviewing knowledge.

Where the picture stops working: A tree grows by itself, slowly and without a break. Insight passes through distinct knowledges, and the path is a single moment (Guide to CMA I §§26–28; CMA IX §34). The sutta names virtue and right concentration but none of the purifications; setting them beside the purification of virtue and of mind (CMA IX §§28–29) is this site's reading. And "disenchantment" here, in the commentary's sense of strong insight, is wider than the single knowledge of disenchantment in the Manual's list (Guide to CMA IX §33); the commentary does not say where strong insight begins. So too, "tender insight" here is the commentary's name for all insight from defining mind and matter onward. That is wider than the Visuddhimagga's tender insight, which is only the first phase of the knowledge of rise and fall (Vism XX §731; Guide to CMA IX §32).

Told in: AN 5.24, the tree (an5.24:1.1–2.9); The same tree with longer chains: AN 6.50, AN 7.65, AN 8.81, AN 10.3; Manorathapūraṇī (commentary to the Aṅguttara) on AN 5.24, CS §24; CMA IX §§28–34: the seven purifications

The blind man and the cripple

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XVIII, CS §677; Atthasālinī, CS §498

Mind and matter each lack strength of their own and work only by leaning on each other. There is no third thing that walks.

A man blind from birth and a cripple who could only shuffle along on a low seat both wanted to go elsewhere. The blind man said: I can do what feet do, but I have no eyes to see where the ground is smooth or rough. The cripple said: I can do what eyes do, but I have no feet to go forward or back. The blind man, delighted, lifted the cripple onto his shoulders, and the cripple, sitting there, called out: leave the left, take the right; leave the right, take the left.

The Visuddhimagga brings it in as a simile that others cite (the commentary teachers, says its sub-commentary), and draws the point. Neither man goes by his own power or his own strength, and yet, leaning on each other, they do go. So mind has no power of its own to arise or to act, and matter has none either. Mind does not eat, drink, speak or take up a posture by itself, and matter has no wish to; but when there is mind's wish to eat or drink, to speak or move, matter eats, drinks, speaks and moves. It adds verses: as people cross the ocean depending on a ship, and the ship crosses depending on the people, so mind and matter each depend on the other.

The Atthasālinī, the commentary on the first book of the Abhidhamma, tells the story more fully. The two sat in a hall at the city gate. The cripple told the blind man of a district where food was plentiful and asked why he did not go and live there in comfort. Why not you? said the blind man. I have no feet, said the cripple; I have no eyes, said the blind man. Then let your feet be mine and my eyes be yours. With the cripple on his shoulders, holding his head with the left hand and pointing out the way with the right (a root here, a stone there), they reached the place together and lived there at ease.

Where the picture stops working: Two persons, one of them giving directions, make mind sound like a pilot: a self riding the body. Both texts rule this out by saying that neither has strength of its own, and the "mind" here is itself many dhammas, the four mental aggregates, arising and passing. The Visuddhimagga's sub-commentary raises the obvious objection, that a hundred blind men together see no better than one, and answers with the simile itself, recalling Sāriputta's words, before his two sheaves of reeds, that some wise people understand a meaning through a simile (SN 12.67, sn12.67:7.3). And the Atthasālinī says the picture is told for existence with all five aggregates. Elsewhere it would not hold: in the immaterial planes mind occurs without matter, and among the non-percipient beings matter occurs without mind (CMA VI §§28–29).

Told in: Visuddhimagga XVIII (purification of view), CS §677; Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XVIII §677; SN 12.67, the two sheaves of reeds (sn12.67:7.3); Atthasālinī, on the great resultant cittas, the miscellaneous similes (Vipākuddhārakathā), CS §498; CMA IX §30 and Guide: purification of view

The lamp and the three men

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XX, CS §711

How insight looks at matter in ever shorter spans of time, from a whole lifetime down to a single phase of a step, and finds that nothing passes over from one span to the next.

A man from the borderland, who knew torches of wood and grass but had never seen a lamp, came to a city and saw an oil lamp burning in the market street. He thought it wonderful. A first man told him there was nothing to wonder at: when the oil and the wick are used up, the flame goes out and leaves no trace.

A second man said that was a coarse way to see it. As the wick burns down, the flame in each third of it goes out without passing over into the next third. A third man said that too was coarse. At each finger's breadth, each half finger's breadth, each thread and each fibre of the wick, the flame goes out without passing to the next, and apart from the fibre that is burning no flame can be pointed to at all.

The Visuddhimagga uses the lamp for the meditator who examines matter by its lifespan. Seen coarsely, matter ends at the end of a hundred-year life. Seen more finely, it ends with each stage of life: youth, middle age, old age. Then with each span of ten, five, four, three, two and one years; each season, of four months and of two; each fortnight, each day and night, and each of the six parts of a day and night; and at last with each movement, each phase of a single step. Nothing passes over from one span to the next.

Where the picture stops working: The picture stops at a phase of a step. This is comprehension "by way of duration": seeing the three characteristics in ever shorter spans of time (CMA IX §32 and Guide). The Manual adds that comprehension also works by continuity and by moment, and that the knowledge of rise and fall then watches momentary things arise and pass. The lamp does not reach that far.

Told in: Visuddhimagga XX (the seven ways of comprehending matter), CS §§706–716; the lamp at §711; CMA IX §32 and Guide: knowledge of comprehension

The dewdrop at sunrise

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XX, CS §731

How formations look once the knowledge of rise and fall has begun: always new, gone almost as soon as they come, and without a core.

When the truths, dependent origination, its methods and the characteristics have become plain to the meditator, the Visuddhimagga says, formations appear to him as always new. So these things arise, he sees, that never arose before, and what has arisen ceases.

And not only new: they appear to last only a little while, like a drop of dew when the sun comes up, a bubble on water, a line drawn on water with a stick, a mustard seed balanced on the point of an awl, or a flash of lightning.

And they appear to have no core, like a conjuring trick, a mirage, a dream, a firebrand whirled in a circle, a city of spirits seen in the sky, foam, or the trunk of a plantain tree.

With this the meditator has reached what the Visuddhimagga calls tender insight, the first stage of the knowledge of rise and fall, and from now on he counts as one who has begun insight.

Where the picture stops working: Each of these things lasts a while; formations do not. The sub-commentary warns that although the five images are given as ever briefer, all of them cease more slowly than formations do. The first three images are also in AN 7.74, where the Buddha retells the teaching of an ancient teacher, Araka, on how short a human life is: that is the sutta sense, a lifetime soon over. The Visuddhimagga uses such images in the Abhidhamma sense, of states that last a moment, and it does not cite AN 7.74; the parallel is ours. The whirled firebrand stands here for having no core; it is not the Visuddhimagga's picture of continuity. And this is tender insight, still exposed: it is just here that the ten imperfections arise (Vism XX §732; Guide to CMA IX §32).

Told in: Visuddhimagga XX (knowledge of rise and fall), CS §731 (both paragraphs); Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XX §731; AN 7.74, Araka (an7.74:2.1–4.3), a parallel the Visuddhimagga does not cite; CMA IX §32 and Guide: knowledge of rise and fall, tender and mature

The elder and the conjured elephant

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XX, CS §733

Bright, calm and powerful states can persuade a practised meditator that he has arrived. A sudden test of what he reacts to shows what is still there.

The ten imperfections of insight, the Visuddhimagga says, arise in one who practises rightly and has begun insight; not in a noble disciple who has already penetrated, nor in one who practises wrongly, a lazy one who has dropped his meditation subject. The first is the light. When it appears, the meditator thinks no such light has ever come to him before, and that he must have reached the path and its fruit. He takes what is not the path for the path, leaves his meditation subject, and sits enjoying the light. This error comes mostly to one who has both calm and insight: because defilements held down by his attainments do not stir, he believes he is an arahant.

The elder Dhammadinna of Talaṅgara, an arahant and a teacher of many monks, sat one day in his day-quarters and wondered whether his own teacher, the elder Mahānāga of Uccavālika, had finished the work of a monk. He saw that Mahānāga was still a worldling, and that unless he went to him he would die one. He went through the air by his psychic power, paid his respects, and said he had come with a question. He asked a thousand, and the old elder answered every one without hesitating. Your knowledge is very sharp, venerable sir, said Dhammadinna; when did you reach this? Sixty years ago, said Mahānāga. Can you use concentration too? That is not hard. Then make an elephant.

Mahānāga made an elephant, all white. Now make it charge at you, said Dhammadinna, ears spread, tail out, trunk in its mouth, trumpeting. He did, and when he saw the terrible thing bearing down on him he jumped up to run. Dhammadinna reached out, caught the edge of his robe, and asked: venerable sir, is there fear in one whose taints are gone? Then Mahānāga knew he was still a worldling. He asked Dhammadinna to be his support and sat down at his feet. I came for that, said Dhammadinna; do not worry. He gave him a meditation subject, and Mahānāga went up onto his walking path and at the third step reached arahantship.

The Visuddhimagga adds that the elder was of hating temperament, and that monks like this waver over the light. Its sub-commentary explains: in one of that temperament fear comes suddenly, even if he is brave; and such a temperament is quick to let go, which is why he broke through so fast.

Where the picture stops working: No light appears in the episode itself; the Visuddhimagga tells it under the light and closes by saying that monks like this waver over the light. The test is a psychic feat no reader can copy, and fear is not the test the CMA gives. Bhikkhu Bodhi's Guide gives the way through: recognise these states as by-products of maturing insight, see them as impermanent, suffering and not-self, and go on (Guide to CMA IX §32). Nor are the ten unwholesome in themselves: the Visuddhimagga says the light and the rest are called imperfections because they are a basis for imperfection; only the attachment is both an imperfection and its basis. Taken as mine, as wonderful, or relished, each becomes a grasping of view, conceit or craving (Vism XX §735). Telling what is the path from what is not is the purification named after it (CMA IX §32). The "sixty years" are the time since his supposed attainment, not his years as a monk.

Told in: Visuddhimagga XX (the ten imperfections of insight), CS §§732–736; Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XX §733; Paṭisambhidāmagga, the mind seized by agitation about the Dhamma (ps2.1), quoted in Vism XX §732; CMA IX §32 and Guide: the imperfections, and purification by knowledge and vision of what is and is not the path

The iron rods and the lump of foam

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §739

What hides the three characteristics, and what it looks like when the cover is broken: things come apart like separate rods, and the solid mass melts like foam.

Why do the three characteristics not show? The Visuddhimagga answers: impermanence does not show because rise and fall are not attended to, and continuity hides it. Suffering does not show because the constant oppression is not attended to, and the postures hide it. Not-self does not show because things are not resolved into their elements, and compactness hides it. When rise and fall are grasped and continuity is broken, impermanence shows as it really is; when the oppression is attended to and the postures are seen through, suffering shows; when the elements are taken apart and the compactness is broken up, not-self shows.

This comes after the imperfections, when the knowledge of rise and fall, freed of them, is taken up again for just this purpose: to mark the characteristics.

The sub-commentary gives two pictures. Continuity: to one who rightly watches rise and fall, the arising phase of a state never reaches its falling phase, nor the falling its arising, and states no longer show up as joined in a series. They show up unconnected, like separate iron rods, and impermanence becomes plainer still.

The postures it explains without a picture: a change of posture that relieves the pain born in another works as its cover, and so with each of them. Once this is seen for what it is, the ceaseless oppression of formations shows.

Compactness, it says, is of three kinds: of mass, taking states that hold each other up as one thing; of function, taking their different functions as one; and of object, taking their different ways of taking an object as one. When knowledge separates the elements (earth is one thing, water another; contact one thing, feeling another), these compactnesses dissolve like a lump of foam rubbed in the hand, and not-self shows: these are mere states, empty, occurring as their conditions allow.

Where the picture stops working: These are two pictures and one explanation, all from the sub-commentary, not the Visuddhimagga, which gives none. Rods lying apart are lasting things side by side; states are not, for each rises and falls in a moment. The foam recalls the lump of foam in SN 22.95, there a picture of form, but the sub-commentary does not cite it; the link is ours. Breaking the compact destroys nothing: the single mass was never there. The Manual does not name the three concealments; it uses continuity only as a way of comprehending (CMA IX §32 and Guide), and the three are the Visuddhimagga's (XXI §739).

Told in: Visuddhimagga XXI (knowledge of rise and fall freed of the imperfections), CS §§737–739; Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XXI §739; SN 22.95, the lump of foam (sn22.95:2.1), a parallel the sub-commentary does not cite; CMA IX §23 and Guide: the three characteristics; IX §32 and Guide: comprehension by duration, continuity and moment

Bubbles in heavy rain

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §748; Dhp 170

At the knowledge of dissolution attention no longer reaches the arising of things; it rests on their breaking up, everywhere and all the time.

As the meditator goes on weighing mind and matter as impermanent, suffering and not-self, his knowledge grows sharp and formations come into view quickly. Then his mindfulness no longer reaches their arising, their standing or their going on; it settles on their ending, their breaking up. He sees formations breaking like a fragile pot, scattering like fine dust, crackling like sesame seeds roasting in a pan.

It is as if a man with good eyes stood on the bank of a pond or a river while heavy rain was falling, and watched big bubbles rise on the surface one after another and quickly burst. In the same way he sees all formations breaking, breaking.

It was of such a meditator, the Visuddhimagga says, that the Buddha spoke the verse: the king of death does not see one who looks on the world as one would look at a bubble, or at a mirage (Dhp 170).

The commentary on the Dhammapada gives the verse a story. Five hundred monks, making little progress in the forest, were coming back to the Buddha for a better meditation subject, contemplating a mirage on the way. As they reached the monastery it began to rain, and they watched the bubbles rise and burst under the downpour and took them as their object: this body too arises and breaks like a bubble. The Buddha spoke the verse to them, and when it ended they reached arahantship where they stood.

Where the picture stops working: The man on the bank watches bubbles burst without bursting himself. At this knowledge there is no safe onlooker: the Visuddhimagga says the meditator sees the object break up, and then, with a later mind, the breaking up of the mind that saw it, so that both the known and the knowing are seen to dissolve (Vism XXI §§741–742). Seeing only breaking does not mean that nothing arises; it is where attention rests: arising is simply not taken up (the sub-commentary on §748; Guide to CMA IX §33). And the verse's promise, that the king of death does not see, is fulfilled only with arahantship, after which there is no further becoming (so the sub-commentary); the knowledge of dissolution is a stage on the way, and leads on to the knowledge of fear.

Told in: Visuddhimagga XXI (knowledge of dissolution), CS §§741–748; Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XXI §748; Dhammapada 170 (dhp170:1–4); Dhammapada commentary on verse 170 (Lokavagga, story 3), the five hundred monks practising insight; CMA IX §33 and Guide: knowledge of dissolution

The mother at the execution ground, and the pits of embers

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §§750–751

Seeing past formations gone and present ones going, the meditator knows that those still to come will go the same way, and lets go of attachment to them. The knowledge that sees this is a judgement, not a fright.

As the meditator keeps watching dissolution, the Visuddhimagga says, formations in every kind of existence come to appear as a great terror, as lions, tigers, leopards, bears and hyenas, spirits and ogres, savage bulls and dogs, elephants in rut, deadly snakes, thunderbolts, charnel grounds, battlefields and blazing pits of embers appear to a timid man who wants to live in comfort. He sees: past formations have ceased, present ones are ceasing, and those still to come will cease in the same way.

A woman's three sons had offended the king, who ordered them beheaded. She went with them to the place of execution. When she saw the eldest's head cut off, and the middle son's being cut, she let go of her attachment to the youngest: he too will go the way of the others.

Another woman, whose children never survived, had borne ten. Nine were dead, the tenth was dying in her arms, and she was carrying another in her womb. Seeing the nine dead and the tenth dying, she let go of her attachment to the child she was carrying: it too will be like them.

Then the Visuddhimagga asks whether the knowledge of fear is itself afraid, and answers no: it only judges. Someone with good sight looking at three pits of glowing embers by the city gate feels no fear for himself; he simply judges that whoever falls in will be badly hurt. So too a man looking at three stakes set in a row, of acacia wood, of iron and of gold. In the same way the knowledge of fear only judges, of the three kinds of becoming, which are like those pits and stakes, that past formations have ceased, present ones are ceasing, and future ones will cease.

Where the picture stops working: Both mothers are in anguish, as the story implies, and the timid man is frightened; the knowledge is neither. The Visuddhimagga says plainly that the knowledge of fear does not fear, and its sub-commentary explains why: fearing belongs to a mind with aversion, while knowledge simply knows what is to be feared as fearful. What the Visuddhimagga draws from the mothers is the letting go of attachment; the onlooker at the pits is the picture of the knowledge itself. "Fearful" here is the sense in which the Manual calls formations suffering ("in the sense of fearfulness", CMA IX §32); the Guide explains that what is impermanent offers no security, and so is to be feared. And fear is not a separate experience from danger and disenchantment: the Ancients call them one knowledge with three names, and the Paṭisambhidāmagga, quoted there, says they are one in meaning and differ only in the letter (Vism XXI §755).

Told in: Visuddhimagga XXI (knowledge of appearance as fearful), CS §§750–751; one knowledge with three names, §755; Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XXI §§750–751; CMA IX §33 and Guide: knowledge of the fearful

The vipers, the killers and the empty village

From the commentaries, quoting the canon · SN 35.238

There is no shelter anywhere in the elements, the aggregates or the senses; safety is only on the far shore. The Visuddhimagga takes up these images for the knowledge of danger.

A man who wants to live and not to die, who wants happiness and shrinks from pain, is handed four vipers of fierce heat and deadly venom. He is told that he must rouse them, bathe them, feed them and put them to rest, each at its proper time, and that if any one of them is angered he will die, or suffer close to death. Afraid of the vipers, he runs off this way or that.

He is told that five murderous enemies are on his heels, to kill him wherever they find him. He runs. He is told that a sixth killer, a hidden one who moves in close to him, is following with a raised sword to cut off his head. He runs, and comes to an empty village: every house he enters is void, every pot he touches is hollow. He is told that village-raiding bandits are coming into it now. He runs again.

He comes to a great stretch of water. The near shore is dangerous and frightening, the far shore safe, and there is no ferry and no bridge. He gathers grass, sticks, branches and leaves, binds them into a raft and, relying on it and working with hands and feet, crosses safely. Crossed over, gone beyond, the brahmin stands on dry ground.

The Visuddhimagga, describing the knowledge of danger, uses the same images without naming the sutta. To one with this knowledge no shelter or refuge appears anywhere in any kind of existence. The three kinds of becoming appear like pits full of glowing embers, the four great elements like deadly vipers, the five aggregates like murderers with raised swords, the six inner sense bases like an empty village, the six outer ones like village-raiding bandits, the stations of consciousness and the abodes of beings as burning with eleven fires; and all formations as a boil, a disease, a dart, a calamity, an affliction: without any satisfaction, a great heap of danger.

Where the picture stops working: A chase story is full of panic and flight. The knowledge of danger sees; it does not run. The Visuddhimagga's own picture for it is a timid man who, in his fright, sees danger on every side (XXI §752), but the point is what he sees, not his fright: of the knowledge of fear it says plainly that it does not fear (§751). In the sutta the picture teaches the elements, aggregates and sense bases, and is not tied to any stage of insight. The Visuddhimagga also moves one image: it gives the raised swords to the five aggregates, where in the sutta the sword belongs to the sixth killer, relishing and lust. The raft and the far shore reach beyond this knowledge to the path and to arahantship: the knowledge of danger only sees that nothing on the near shore is safe, and that there is security only in the unconditioned (Guide to CMA IX §33). The Visuddhimagga counts it one knowledge with fear and disenchantment, under three names (Vism XXI §755). The eleven fires it names match those of SN 35.28; that link is ours, for the Visuddhimagga cites nothing here.

Told in: SN 35.238, the simile of the vipers (sn35.238:1.1–17.1; the key from sn35.238:7.1); Visuddhimagga XXI (knowledge of contemplation of danger), CS §752; SN 35.28, the eleven fires (sn35.28:1.7), for comparison; CMA IX §33 and Guide: knowledge of danger

The snake taken for a fish

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §758 (with §791)

Why the meditator who wants to be free turns back and looks at formations again: laying the three characteristics on them is the way to let them go safely, as swinging the snake is the way to be rid of it.

A man who wanted to catch fish set a fish-trap in the water. He put his hand in through the mouth of the trap and, under the water, took hold of a snake by the neck. He was pleased: he had caught a fish, and a big one. He lifted it out to look, and when he saw three svastika marks on it he knew it for a snake, and was afraid. He saw the danger, he was weary of holding it, and he wanted to let it go.

He went about it carefully. He freed his hand from the coils, beginning at the tail, swung the snake two or three times round above his head to tire it out, and threw it away. Then he scrambled up the bank of the pond and stood there looking back the way he had come: he had escaped from the jaws of a great snake.

The Visuddhimagga tells the story for the knowledge of reflection and keys it step by step. Swinging the snake until it is too weak to turn and bite is the meditator laying the three characteristics on formations until they are too weak to present themselves again as permanent, pleasant, beautiful or self; then he can let them go, and let them go well.

Later, among its twelve similes of the insight that leads to emergence, the Visuddhimagga takes up the same story, which it calls the simile of the black snake, and carries it on to the end: flinging the snake away is change-of-lineage, standing looking back along the way he came is the path, and going on to stand in a place where there is nothing to fear is fruition.

Where the picture stops working: The man is in danger from the snake and gets free by force. Insight weakens formations only by seeing them as they are, and the one who holds and lets go is a way of speaking: what holds is clinging, what lets go is a run of moments of knowledge. The man is afraid; the knowledge of fear is not (Vism XXI §751). The story's steps are not wholly separate experiences: the Ancients call fear, danger and disenchantment one knowledge with three names, and the Paṭisambhidāmagga, quoted there, says they are one in meaning and differ only in the letter (Vism XXI §755); the same is said of desire for deliverance, reflection and equanimity (Vism XXI §§778–781). The story has no step for equanimity or conformity: it goes straight from the swinging to the throw. And standing looking back along the way he came looks like reviewing, but the Visuddhimagga keys it to the path; reviewing comes only after fruition (CMA IX §34).

Told in: Visuddhimagga XXI (knowledge of reflection), CS §758; Visuddhimagga XXI (the twelve similes of insight leading to emergence), CS §§789, 791; Visuddhimagga mahāṭīkā on XXI §758 (the trap, the black snake, the unwinding); CMA IX §33 and Guide: the nine insight knowledges; IX §34: change-of-lineage, path and fruition

The man who let his wife go, and the direction crow

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXI, CS §§766–767

Equanimity about formations is the end of "mine", not coldness: with nothing taken as I or mine, fear and delight both drop away. And while it does not yet see Nibbāna as peace, it keeps coming back to formations.

A man had a wife he loved so dearly that he could not bear to be without her for a moment. He held her as his own above everything, and if he saw her standing or sitting, talking or laughing with another man, he was angry and deeply unhappy. Later he saw her faults, wanted to be free, and let her go: he no longer took her as his. From then on, whatever she did and with whomever, he was neither angry nor unhappy, only uninvolved and even.

So it is with the meditator, the Visuddhimagga says. Wanting to be free of all formations, he examines them by the contemplation of reflection, finds nothing in them to take as I or mine, gives up both fear and delight, and becomes uninvolved and even towards all formations. His mind then draws back from every kind of existence and does not reach out to it, as drops of water on a lotus leaf that slopes a little gather up and run off instead of spreading, or as a feather or a strip of sinew shrivels in the fire (it quotes AN 7.49 for the feather and sinew).

Sea traders take aboard a bird they call the direction crow. When a storm drives the ship far out and no land can be seen, they let it go. It rises from the masthead and flies to every quarter. If it sees the shore, it flies straight there; if not, it comes back to the mast again and again. In the same way, if this knowledge sees Nibbāna, the state of peace, as peace, it lets go of all formations and launches into Nibbāna; if not, it goes on again and again with formations as its object.

Meanwhile it keeps working over formations in many ways, like flour turned on a winnowing tray, or cotton with its seeds taken out being carded with a bow. The sub-commentary explains both pictures: the coarse is separated from the fine, as insight grows finer with each new round of comprehension.

Where the picture stops working: Read carelessly, the wife's story praises indifference to people. It is about formations, and about the end of taking them as mine; what is dropped is fear at their failing and delight in their success (so the sub-commentary). The calm is earned: the Visuddhimagga says the man lets go because he wants to be free, and the sub-commentary counts all insight from desire for deliverance until equanimity as reflection. The Ancients call the three one knowledge with three names, and the Paṭisambhidāmagga says they are one in meaning (Vism XXI §§778–781). The crow is restless, the knowledge even; and its coming back is not failure, only a knowledge not yet ripe. When the crow reaches the shore it sounds as if equanimity itself takes Nibbāna as its object. The sub-commentary says that here the knowledge is spoken of as one with conformity and change-of-lineage; the first moment that takes Nibbāna as object is change-of-lineage (CMA IX §34 and Guide). The bird recalls the land-spotting bird of the suttas: in DN 11 it stands for a monk who searched for an answer as far as the Brahmā world and came back to the Buddha, and in AN 6.54 for a monk turned out of his monasteries who came back to him. The Visuddhimagga cites neither; the parallel is ours.

Path and fruition

The leap, the moon and the archer

From the commentaries · Visuddhimagga XXII, CS §§807–809

What each moment at the threshold of the path does: conformity clears the way, change-of-lineage sees Nibbāna for the first time, and the path breaks through greed, hatred and delusion never broken before.

The leap. A man wants to land on the far side of a wide ditch. He runs, grabs a rope or pole hanging from a tree on this side, springs up, swings across leaning towards the far bank, lets go when he is above it, and lands there, a little unsteadily, until he steadies himself. The Visuddhimagga says the run is the knowledge of rise and fall and the insights after it; the rope or pole is one of the aggregates, seen as impermanent, suffering or not-self. The spring, the lean and arriving above the far bank are the three conformity moments. Letting go and landing is change-of-lineage, the first moment that has Nibbāna as its object. It is unsteady because no moment before it has taken that object, so it has nothing to repeat; what makes him stand firm is the path.

The moon. A man goes out at night to see the moon, but clouds hide it. A first wind blows away the thick clouds, a second the middling ones, a third the thin ones, and then he sees the moon. The winds can clear clouds but cannot see; the man can see but cannot clear clouds. So the conformity moments dispel the darkness that hides the truths but cannot see Nibbāna, and change-of-lineage sees Nibbāna but cannot dispel that darkness.

The archer. An archer stands, his face wrapped in cloth, on a turning wheel, with a hundred planks set up some way off. A helper spins the wheel and, when the archer faces the planks, gives him a sign with a tap of a stick. Not letting go of that signal, the archer shoots, and the arrow goes through all hundred planks. The signal is change-of-lineage; the archer is the path knowledge, which splits masses of greed, hatred and delusion never split before.

Where the picture stops working: The leaper and the archer are each one person all through. At the threshold there is no one: each stage is a different moment of mind, each conditioning the next. The Visuddhimagga speaks of three conformity moments. The Manual names the moments before change-of-lineage preparation, access and conformity, and allows two or three (CMA IX §34); the Guide explains that one of unusually keen faculties skips preparation and has two. And the passage is told of the first path, stream-entry, which does not yet end all greed, hatred and delusion.

Told in: Visuddhimagga XXII (purification by knowledge and vision), CS §§807–809; CMA IX §34 and Guide: the path process

Water poured where the fire was

From the commentaries · Vibhāvinī on CMA IX §34, CS §59

Fruition cuts nothing new. Right after the path it calms the heat that is left in a mind whose defilements the path has already cut.

The Manual says that after the path two or three moments of fruition consciousness arise and cease, and then the mind sinks back into the life-continuum.

The Vibhāvinī, the commentary on the Manual, pictures those moments. A fire has been carried away, but the ground where it burned is still hot, and water is poured on it from pots to cool it. So the two or three fruition cittas, two or three according to how the path arose, occur in a continuum whose defilements have already been cut off, and calm the distress that remains there.

Where the picture stops working: Someone pours the water, from outside and after a pause. Fruition is the path's own result, arising at once, with no gap and no one doing it (Vism XXII §811; CMA IX §34). Whether there are two moments or three depends on the path process: three for one of keen faculties who skips the moment of preparation, two for others (Guide to CMA IX §34); the Visuddhimagga rejects the view that there can be one or four. The picture covers only these first moments. Fruition can later be entered again and, with practice, held for many moments in the attainment of fruition, which the picture does not show (Guide to CMA I §§26–28; CMA IX §42 and Guide). And "distress" is the Vibhāvinī's word; it does not mean that defilements cut by this path remain.
