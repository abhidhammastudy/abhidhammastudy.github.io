Aṅgulimāla and the stones

The bandit Aṅgulimāla had killed many. Meeting the Buddha, he went forth and became an arahant. On alms round people threw stones and sticks at him. The Buddha said: “Bear it, brahmin. You are experiencing here and now the result of deeds for which you might have suffered in hell for many years.”

Where
The kingdom of Kosala, the road to Aṅgulimāla's forest, and Sāvatthī
Who
  • Aṅgulimāla, “garland of fingers”, a bandit; born Ahiṃsaka, “harmless”
  • King Pasenadi, who rode out to hunt him
  • The Buddha, who walked into the forest alone
Source
MN 86

The story

The commentary tells how a gifted student called Ahiṃsaka, “harmless”, was slandered by jealous fellow students, and his teacher, to be rid of him, demanded a fee of a thousand human fingers. In the sutta he is already a bandit in the realm of King Pasenadi, wearing a garland of fingers, who has laid waste villages and towns.

One morning the Buddha took the road toward his forest. Cowherds and farmers warned him that men in groups of ten, twenty, thirty, even forty had gone that way and fallen to Aṅgulimāla. He walked on in silence. Aṅgulimāla saw him and ran after him with sword and shield, but, running his hardest, could not catch the Buddha walking at his ordinary pace. “Stop, recluse!” he shouted. “I have stopped, Aṅgulimāla,” said the Buddha. “You stop too.” I have stopped, he explained, because I have laid down the rod toward all beings; you have not. Aṅgulimāla threw his weapons into a chasm, bowed, and asked to go forth.

King Pasenadi rode out with five hundred horsemen to take him, and found him sitting at Jetavana with shaven head. The king, amazed, offered him robes and requisites, which he declined, having all a forest monk needs: “what we could not tame with force and weapons, the Blessed One has tamed without them.” On alms round Aṅgulimāla saw a woman in a difficult childbirth, and the Buddha gave him words to say to her: since I was born with the noble birth, I do not recall ever taking a life; by this truth may you and your child be well. She was safe. The verse is still chanted for women in labour.

He became an arahant. One day he came back from alms round with a broken head, his bowl smashed and his robe torn, from a stone, a stick and a potsherd thrown at him. The Buddha said: “Bear it, brahmin. You are experiencing here and now the result of deeds for which you might have been tormented in hell for many years.” His verses close the sutta: one who was heedless and later is heedful lights up the world like the moon freed from a cloud.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Kamma by time (“Kamma, death and rebirth”): what could ripen in this life did. With arahantship there is no next life, so kamma waiting for later lives could never ripen.

Read with: Kamma, death and rebirth

The dhammas it shows

  • hatred dosa: a bandit who laid waste villages and towns
  • non-hatred adosa: I have laid down the rod toward all beings
  • kamma kamma: old deeds ripening here and now as stones

Where it is told

  • MN 86 Aṅgulimāla Sutta
  • Theragāthā 16.8, his verses
  • Majjhima commentary on MN 86 (Ahiṃsaka, the teacher's fee, his mother)

The Aṅgulimāla paritta, the words he said to the woman in labour, is one of the protective chants of the Theravāda.
