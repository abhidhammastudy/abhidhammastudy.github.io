Ariṭṭha and the ten pictures

Ariṭṭha held that the things the Buddha called obstructions do not obstruct one who indulges in them. The monks reminded him of the ten pictures the Buddha had given for sensual pleasures, a bare skeleton, a scrap of meat, a grass torch and the rest. He would not let go, and the Buddha called him to account.

Where
Jetavana, Anāthapiṇḍika's park at Sāvatthī
Who
  • Ariṭṭha, a monk known as “formerly of the vulture-trainers”
  • The monks, who tried to talk him out of his view
  • The Buddha, who called him to account
Source
MN 22

The story

Ariṭṭha announced his view: as he understood the Dhamma, the things the Blessed One called obstructions could not obstruct anyone who engaged in them. Monks went to him and pressed him. Do not misrepresent the Blessed One, they said. He has called sensual pleasures a skeleton, a piece of meat, a grass torch, a pit of coals, a dream, borrowed goods, fruit on a tree, a butcher's knife and block, a stake of swords, a snake's head: much suffering, much despair, and the danger in them greater. Ariṭṭha held to his view all the same. (The commentary says he meant sexual relations, for a monk.)

Brought before the Buddha, he was asked from whom he had heard the Dhamma taught that way. He sat silent, shoulders drooping, head down, with nothing to say. The Buddha went through the ten pictures again himself. Elsewhere, in the Potaliya Sutta, he draws them out one by one: the hungry dog gnawing a bone with no meat on it, the bird with a scrap of meat chased by the others, the man carrying a burning torch against the wind, the dream that is gone on waking, the borrowed goods their owner takes back, the man up a fruit tree while another fells it.

Then he spoke of how the teaching itself can be grasped. Some learn it for the sake of finding fault and winning arguments; they hold it like a man who seizes a snake by the tail, and it turns and bites him. Others grasp it well, like a man who pins the snake down with a forked stick and takes it behind the head. And the teaching, he said, is like a raft: for crossing over, not for carrying on one's head once across.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Ten pictures for one thing, sensual pleasures: the objects that greed fastens on, each picture showing a different danger. As “The lists reduced to their factors” shows for the taints, floods, bonds and fetters, many names can point to one factor, greed, each name catching it in a different act.

Read with: The lists reduced to their factors

The dhammas it shows

  • greed lobha: the greed that the ten pictures of sensual pleasure warn against
  • wrong view diṭṭhi: Ariṭṭha's view that obstructions do not obstruct
  • wisdom paññindriya: grasping the teaching rightly, like a snake behind the head

Where it is told

  • MN 22 Alagaddūpama Sutta
  • MN 54 Potaliya Sutta, the pictures drawn out

The ten pictures are one thing under ten names, each catching a different side of it: no nourishment, rivalry, burning, brevity, loss. The raft at the end of the same sutta closes “The commentators' picture-book”.
