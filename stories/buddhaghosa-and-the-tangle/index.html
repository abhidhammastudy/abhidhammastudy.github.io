Buddhaghosa and the tangle

The chronicle tells that a learned brahmin, won over by the elder Revata, became a monk and went to Sri Lanka to put the old Sinhala commentaries into Pali. To test him, the elders gave him two verses on a tangle. His answer was the Visuddhimagga, the book where many of the commentators' pictures are found.

Where
Near the tree of awakening in India, then the Mahāvihāra at Anurādhapura, in the reign of King Mahānāma
Who
  • Buddhaghosa, a learned brahmin who became the greatest of the Pali commentators
  • Revata, the elder who, in the chronicle, brought him into the Saṅgha
  • The elders of the Mahāvihāra, keepers of the old Sinhala commentaries
Source
Cūḷavaṃsa 37

The story

Near the tree of awakening, the chronicle says, there lived a young brahmin who knew the Vedas and every art, and went about India looking for debates. One night in a monastery he recited his own doctrine, and the elder Revata, hearing him, answered it, then recited a passage of the Abhidhamma that the brahmin could not follow. Whose teaching is that? The Buddha's. He asked to learn it, was told he must first go forth, and did. Because his voice was deep like the Buddha's, he was called Buddhaghosa, voice of the Buddha.

Revata told him that only the texts had been kept in India; the commentaries survived in Sinhala, in Sri Lanka. So Buddhaghosa crossed over to the Mahāvihāra at Anurādhapura, heard the Sinhala commentaries, and asked to translate them. The elders gave him a test: two verses from the Saṃyutta. A deva asks the Buddha about a tangle within and a tangle without, and who can untangle it. The Buddha answers that one who stands firm in virtue, wise, developing mind and understanding, can untangle it. Show your skill on these, they said.

He wrote the Visuddhimagga, the Path of Purification, laying out the whole teaching under virtue, concentration and understanding. The devas, the chronicle says, hid the book to show his skill, and he wrote it a second time, and they hid that too, and he wrote it a third time. When the first two were brought back and the three were read side by side, they agreed in every word. Then the elders gave him the books, and he put the commentaries into Pali.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

The heap of coins, the struck bell and the gliding bird of “The commentators' picture-book” are read in the Visuddhimagga; this is the tradition's account of how that book came to be written.

Read with: The commentators' picture-book

The dhammas it shows

  • wisdom paññindriya: the wise one who untangles the tangle, in the test verse
  • one-pointedness ekaggatā: “developing mind”: concentration, the second of the book's three parts

Where it is told

  • Cūḷavaṃsa 37 (the continuation of the Mahāvaṃsa), translated by Wilhelm Geiger
  • SN 1.23 Jaṭā Sutta, the verses on the tangle (also SN 7.6)
  • Visuddhimagga I.1; translated by Bhikkhu Ñāṇamoli as The Path of Purification

This is a legend: the chronicle that tells it was written some eight centuries after Buddhaghosa. The Visuddhimagga itself says only that it was made at the request of an elder named Saṅghapāla. Many of its pictures were not new: he drew on the Sinhala commentaries and the Milindapañha, and often credits the ancients.
