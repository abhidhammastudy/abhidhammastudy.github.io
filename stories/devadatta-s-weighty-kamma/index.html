Devadatta's weighty kamma

The Buddha's cousin Devadatta drew the Buddha's blood with a rock hurled from Vulture Peak, and split the Saṅgha. The commentary says that as he came at last to ask forgiveness, the earth swallowed him and he was reborn in the Avīci hell.

Where
Rājagaha, Vulture Peak, Gayāsīsa, and the gate of Jetavana
Who
  • Devadatta, the Buddha's cousin and brother-in-law, a monk with psychic powers
  • Prince Ajātasattu, of Magadha, his patron
  • Sāriputta and Moggallāna, who won back the monks he led away
Source
Vinaya, Cūḷavagga VII; Dhammapada commentary, v. 17

The story

Devadatta went forth with the Sakyan princes Bhaddiya, Anuruddha, Ānanda, Bhagu and Kimbila, and the barber Upāli. He gained psychic powers, and with them won the favour of Prince Ajātasattu, appearing in his lap as a boy wearing a girdle of snakes. With gain and honour, the Vinaya says, came the wish to lead the Saṅgha.

He asked the Buddha, now old, to hand the Saṅgha over to him, and was refused. He urged Ajātasattu to kill his father and take the throne, and he himself would kill the Buddha. He sent archers, who were converted. He hurled a rock from Vulture Peak; it broke, and a splinter cut the Buddha's foot and drew blood. He had the fierce elephant Nāḷāgiri loosed in the Buddha's path; the elephant was calmed by the Buddha's loving-kindness and knelt.

Then he proposed five strict rules: monks should live only in the forest, eat only alms food, wear only rag robes, live at the foot of trees, and never eat fish or meat. The Buddha allowed each as a choice but would not make them binding. Devadatta took this as his cause, and led five hundred newly ordained monks of Vesālī away to Gayāsīsa: a schism. Sāriputta and Moggallāna went there and brought them back, and the Vinaya says hot blood gushed from Devadatta's mouth.

The Dhammapada commentary tells his end. After nine months of illness he wished to see the Buddha and was carried towards Jetavana on a litter. When it was set down by the pond at the gate, he stood, and the earth opened and drew him down. Going down, he spoke a verse taking refuge in the Buddha with his bones and his last breath. He was reborn in Avīci; and the commentary adds that after a hundred thousand aeons he will become a paccekabuddha.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Wounding a Buddha and splitting the Saṅgha are two of the five heinous deeds: weighty kamma, which ripens before any other kind (“Kamma, death and rebirth”). His last act was to take refuge, a wholesome thought at death, and still the weighty kamma came first. The commentary still holds out hope: the Buddha ordained him, it says, knowing that as a monk he could lay a condition for his eventual release, and he will be a paccekabuddha a hundred thousand aeons from now.

Read with: Kamma, death and rebirth · Thirty-one planes

The dhammas it shows

  • greed lobha: gain and honour breed the wish to lead
  • envy issā: envy of the Buddha's standing
  • hatred dosa: the rock, the archers and the elephant
  • kamma kamma: weighty kamma that ripens before any other

Where it is told

  • Vinaya, Cūḷavagga VII (Saṅghabhedakkhandhaka)
  • Dhammapada v. 17, with its commentary (story I.12)
  • AN 8.7 on Devadatta
  • Ud 5.8 on Devadatta

Scholars have long noted that Devadatta's five rules were a call for stricter practice, and that later Chinese pilgrims met monks in India who followed Devadatta's rules. The Pāli tradition remembers him only as the Buddha's enemy.
