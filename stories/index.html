Stories

The Abhidhamma is abstract; its tradition is not. These 61 stories come from the suttas, the Jātakas and the commentaries. Each has its own page with the full story, who is in it, where it is told, and a reading, marked as a study aid, that links it to an idea.

Foundations

What is the Abhidhamma, and what does it take apart?

Where the Abhidhamma was first taught

The Buddha's life · Atthasālinī, introduction

The commentary tells that the Buddha spent a rainy season in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven, teaching the Abhidhamma to the devas, among them his mother, reborn as a deva. Each day he came down to Lake Anotatta, where he gave Sāriputta the outline, and Sāriputta taught it to five hundred monks. At the end of the rains he descended at Saṅkassa. The Tipiṭaka itself does not tell this story.

This is the tradition's account of where the seven books described in “The library” came from, and why Sāriputta is honoured as the one who first passed it on.

The last line of every Jātaka

Jātaka · Jātaka, closing identification

Each Jātaka ends by naming who was who: “At that time, so-and-so was the king, and I myself was the ascetic.” One continuity is followed across many lives as if it were one person.

Conventional truth at work (“A chariot of parts”). Ultimately there is only a stream of cittas and matter, each moment conditioning the next. “The Bodhisatta” names the stream: in Nāgasena's words to King Milinda, neither the same person, nor a different one.

Vakkali and the body

Disciples · SN 22.87

The monk Vakkali longed to keep looking at the Buddha. The Buddha said: “Enough, Vakkali. What is there to see in this foul body? Whoever sees the Dhamma sees me.”

The commentary takes this Dhamma as the ninefold supramundane Dhamma; the sutta goes straight on to teach him the five aggregates, impermanent and not self. Seen that way, even “the Buddha” is a convention for aggregates, the shift “A chariot of parts” asks of you.

Nun Vajirā's chariot

Disciples · SN 5.10

Māra asked the nun Vajirā who had created “this being”. She answered: “Why do you assume a being? This is a heap of formations. As the word ‘chariot’ is used when the parts are assembled, so ‘a being’ is the convention when the aggregates are present.”

Her reply is the hinge of “A chariot of parts”: the five aggregates underneath, the convention “a being” on top of them.

Rohitassa and the end of the world

Disciples · AN 4.45; SN 2.26

A deva asked the Buddha whether one could reach the end of the world by travelling. In a former life, he said, he had walked for a hundred years at the speed of an arrow and died without finding it. The Buddha answered that the world, its arising and its ending are found in this fathom-long body, with its perception and mind.

The world that cannot be reached by walking is the world of “The All, counted twice”: six doors, what comes through them, and the knowing of it, inside a fathom-long body.

The honey-ball

Disciples · MN 18

Asked what he taught, the Buddha gave a riddle-like answer about proliferating perceptions. The monks took it to Mahākaccāna, who unfolded it: from eye, form and eye-consciousness comes contact, then feeling, perception, thinking, and at last a swarm of notions that besets the one who thought them.

Mahākaccāna's chain runs from realities to concepts: contact, feeling and perception are dhammas, and what they swell into are the names and notions of “Names and the named”. The commentary names what drives the swelling: craving, conceit and views.

Elements

What is experience made of?

Kisāgotamī and the mustard seed

Disciples · Dhammapada commentary, v. 114

Mad with grief, Kisāgotamī carried her dead child from house to house asking for medicine. The Buddha asked her for a mustard seed from a house where no one had died. She found none, laid the child down, and understood that death comes to all.

Her grief is displeasure (domanassadomanassa · displeasure painful mental feeling; only in the two hate-rooted cittas), which in “A map of the eighty-nine” occurs only in the two hate-rooted cittas: grief is aversion toward loss. Its soil is attachment (“from the dear comes sorrow”, Dhp 212), and the commentary says her heart, soft with love for her son, had to grow firm before she could let him go. The search turned a private wound into a universal fact, and the verse on death turned that fact into the path.

Why Moggallāna smiled

Disciples · SN 19.1

Coming down Vulture Peak, Mahā Moggallāna smiled. Asked why, he said he had seen a skeleton moving through the air, pursued by crows and vultures. The Buddha confirmed that he too had seen such beings, and named the deeds that led there.

An arahant smiles with one of five cittas, none of which makes kamma: the smile-producing consciousness found only in arahants (in “A map of the eighty-nine”), or one of the four joyful great functional cittas. What he saw belongs to the woeful planes of “Thirty-one planes”.

Khantivādi, the teacher of patience

Jātaka · Ja 313

A drunken king, Kalābu, asked an ascetic what he taught. “Patience,” he said. To test him, the king had his hands, feet, nose and ears cut off. The ascetic felt no anger and wished the king long life. Leaving, the king was swallowed by the earth.

“An ensemble of fifty-two” at its sharpest: non-hatred (adosaadosa · non-hatred Non-hatred is the mind's not opposing its object: friendliness and patience, not only the absence of ill will.) and hatred (dosadosa · hatred aversion, from irritation to rage; root of two cittas, always with displeasure) never share a citta. The king's fate returns in “Kamma, death and rebirth” as kamma that ripens in this very life.

The monkey king's bridge

Jātaka · Ja 407

To save his troop from archers, a monkey king stretched himself across a river as a living bridge. The troop crossed over his back, but a jealous monkey leapt down on him, and his heart burst. The king of Benares, moved, tended him as he died.

Compassion (karuṇākaruṇā · compassion Compassion is the wish that suffering be removed from others.) is one of the variable beautiful factors in “An ensemble of fifty-two”. It joins a wholesome citta only now and then, when the suffering of others is the object. The Jātaka's own lesson is for rulers: one who leads should seek the good of all who depend on him.

Dhammadinnā on feeling

Disciples · MN 44

Questioned by the layman Visākha, the nun Dhammadinnā explained that lust lies latent in pleasant feeling, aversion in painful feeling and ignorance in neutral feeling. The Buddha said he would have answered in the same way.

This joins the colours of feeling in “A map of the eighty-nine” to the wheel in “The wheel across three lives”: feeling (link 7) is where craving (link 8) takes hold.

Pukkusāti and the elements

Disciples · MN 140

One night in a potter's shed, the Buddha taught a stranger, Pukkusāti, to see his body as earth, water, fire, air, space and consciousness, none of it “mine”. Pukkusāti recognised his teacher, but while seeking robes for ordination he was killed by a cow. The Buddha declared him a non-returner.

The four great elements are the core of the 28 kinds of matter in “How matter is made”. Analysing the body into elements is also one of the subjects in “Forty meditation subjects”.

Nakulapitā, sick in body, not in mind

Disciples · SN 22.1

An old, ailing householder asked the Buddha for a teaching. Train yourself, said the Buddha: though my body is afflicted, my mind will not be afflicted. Sāriputta explained how: the body's pain need not become the mind's grief, if one does not take the body, or feeling or anything else, as self.

In “Feeling, roots and objects” bodily feeling alone is painful or pleasant, and mental pain comes only with the two hate-rooted cittas. Nakulapitā's training is the gap between the two.

Two sheaves of reeds

Disciples · SN 12.67

Mahākoṭṭhita asked Sāriputta whether name-and-form and consciousness were made by oneself, by another, by both, or by neither. By none of these, said Sāriputta: each arises with the other as its condition, like two sheaves of reeds standing propped against each other. Pull one away and the other falls.

The two sheaves are consciousness on one side and name-and-form on the other (the mental factors and matter); “Mind and matter together” models the same mutual support: two currents that hold each other up, and that arise together at conception.

Buddhaghosa and the tangle

Disciples · Cūḷavaṃsa 37

The chronicle tells that a learned brahmin, won over by the elder Revata, became a monk and went to Sri Lanka to put the old Sinhala commentaries into Pali. To test him, the elders gave him two verses on a tangle. His answer was the Visuddhimagga, the book where many of the commentators' pictures are found.

The heap of coins, the struck bell and the gliding bird of “The commentators' picture-book” are read in the Visuddhimagga; this is the tradition's account of how that book came to be written.

Mālukyaputta's teaching in brief

Disciples · SN 35.95

In old age Mālukyaputta asked for a teaching in brief, to take into solitude. The Buddha asked: could desire arise for sights you have never seen and never will? No. Then let the seen be only the seen. Mālukyaputta answered in verses: lust grows not from seeing a form, but from attending to its pleasing sign with mindfulness lost.

Seeing is one of the fourteen jobs of “What each citta does”, done by a resultant citta that cannot be otherwise. The harm in Mālukyaputta's verses begins after it, in attending to the pleasing sign, at the one station where the javanas run and kamma is made.

The royal cook's sauce

Disciples · Milindapañha, the questions on distinguishing marks

King Milinda asked whether the factors that arise together in one mind could be pulled apart and shown: this is contact, this is feeling, this is perception. No, said Nāgasena. They are like the flavours in a sauce made by a royal cook: no one can lift out the sourness or the salt, yet each is there by its own mark.

The cook's sauce is a picture of one column of “Which factors meet which cittas”: a single citta assembled from a fixed menu of factors, each present by its own mark and none separable from the moment.

Process

How does experience unfold, moment by moment?

Bāhiya: in the seen, only the seen

Disciples · Ud 1.10

Bāhiya stopped the Buddha on his alms round, begging to be taught. The Buddha said: “In the seen there will be only the seen, in the heard only the heard, in the sensed only the sensed, in the cognized only the cognized.” Bāhiya became an arahant there and then, and was killed by a cow soon after.

“The mango and the moment” shows where the instruction bites, and the Udāna commentary reads it the same way: let the javanas stay at the measure of eye-consciousness, which neither lusts, hates nor is deluded. Seeing itself, at moment 5, is a result and makes no kamma. Liking, disliking and “I” enter only in the javanas.

Nanda, guardian of the doors

Disciples · Ud 3.2; AN 8.9; AN 1.230

The Buddha's half-brother Nanda was ordained on his wedding day and pined for his bride. In time he became an arahant, and the Buddha named him foremost among those who guard the sense doors: whichever way Nanda looked, he looked with full awareness, so that longing and grief could not flow in.

The Buddha met Nanda's desire where it was and led it past itself: once free, Nanda wanted nothing of the promise. The doors he then learned to guard are the doors of “The mango and the moment”. Guarding them means the seeing is not followed by unwholesome javanas.

Makhādeva's first grey hair

Jātaka · Ja 9

King Makhādeva told his barber to report the first grey hair. When it came, the king held it in his hand, saw death approaching, handed the kingdom to his son and went forth.

A small object can carry a large process: the same grey hair can stir vanity or urgency (saṃvega), depending on how it is attended to (“The mango and the moment”). MN 83 adds the Buddha's own point: Makhādeva's good custom led to the Brahmā world; the custom he himself founded, the noble eightfold path, leads to Nibbāna.

Rāhula and the mirror

The Buddha's life · MN 61

The Buddha asked his young son Rāhula what a mirror is for. “For reflection, sir.” “In the same way,” said the Buddha, “reflect before, during and after every action of body, speech and mind: does it lead to harm for myself, for others, or both?”

Reflection is a discipline for the javanas, where volition becomes kamma (“The mango and the moment” and “Kamma, death and rebirth”).

Ānanda lies down

Disciples · Vinaya, Cūḷavagga XI

The First Council was to begin the next day, and Ānanda, who would recite the suttas, was not yet an arahant. He spent the night in mindfulness of the body. Near dawn he decided to lie down, and as he leaned back, before his head reached the pillow and after his feet had left the ground, his mind was freed.

The path process in “Other processes” runs at the mind door and needs no posture at all: preparation, access, conformity, cleansing (the name change-of-lineage takes for the higher paths), then path and fruition, in the space between standing and lying down.

Pilindavaccha's old word

Disciples · Ud 3.6; Dhammapada commentary, v. 408

Pilindavaccha, an arahant, called his fellow monks vasala, outcast, a brahmin's insult. They complained. The Buddha looked into his past and told them: Vaccha does not speak so from hatred. For five hundred lives he was born a brahmin, and the word is old in his mouth.

An arahant has lost every wholesome and unwholesome javana, as “Who has which cittas” explains. Pilindavaccha's rough word is moved by a functional citta: there is no hatred in it and no kamma is made, though the habit of the word remains.

Mahātissa and the laughing woman

Disciples · Visuddhimagga I §15

On the road to Anurādhapura for alms, the elder Mahātissa met a young wife going home after a quarrel, who laughed out loud on seeing him. He looked up and saw only the bones of her teeth; the perception of foulness he had practised came back to him, and he became an arahant where he stood. Asked by her husband whether a woman had passed, he said he had seen only a heap of bones going along the high road.

Straight after the story the Visuddhimagga explains restraint by the cognitive process of “The mango and the moment”: when a visible form strikes the eye there arise adverting, eye-consciousness, receiving, investigating and determining, then the javanas. At none of the moments before javana is there restraint or its lack; they arise only at javana. Guarding the doors means guarding the javanas: the Visuddhimagga's city with its gates shut keeps its goods safe even if the inner doors stand open. The process itself is the Manual's eye-door process (CMA IV §6). Restraint of the senses is the second of the four kinds of purified virtue (CMA IX §28), and the Visuddhimagga's placement of the story there is its own; calling it the ground of insight is the site's framing.

Relations

How does everything hold together?

The night of awakening

The Buddha's life · MN 36; Ud 1.1–1.3

Under the Bodhi tree, in the first watch of the night he recalled his past lives; in the second he saw beings pass away and reappear according to their kamma; in the third he realised the destruction of the taints. In the days that followed he reviewed dependent origination forward and in reverse.

The three watches map onto the readings: past lives across the planes (“Thirty-one planes”), beings reborn by kamma and its fruit (“Kamma, death and rebirth”), and the wheel with its ending (“The wheel across three lives” and “The tree of purification”).

Assaji's four lines

Disciples · Vinaya, Mahāvagga I.23

The wanderer Upatissa saw the monk Assaji on alms round and asked for his teacher's doctrine. Assaji gave it in one verse: “Of things that arise from a cause, the Tathāgata has told the cause, and also their cessation.” Upatissa, later Sāriputta, gained the eye of Dhamma on hearing it.

The shortest summary of “The web of twenty-four conditions” and “The wheel across three lives”: whatever is conditioned has conditions, and they can cease. Sāriputta went on, the commentary says, to pass the Abhidhamma on to its first human disciples (“The library”).

“Do not say so, Ānanda”

The Buddha's life · DN 15

Ānanda remarked that dependent origination, though called deep, seemed clear to him. The Buddha replied: “Do not say so, Ānanda. This dependent origination is deep and appears deep. Through not understanding it, this generation has become like a tangled skein.”

“The web of twenty-four conditions” and “The wheel across three lives” make the structure visible, but reading a diagram is learning (suta). Seeing a link happen is development (bhāvanā).

Rays from the Jewel House

The Buddha's life · Atthasālinī, introduction

In the fourth week after the awakening, the commentary says, the Buddha sat at the “jewel house” and contemplated the Abhidhamma. Through the first six books no rays appeared; when he reached the Paṭṭhāna, rays of six colours streamed from his body.

The tradition marks the Paṭṭhāna, the book behind “The web of twenty-four conditions”, as the vastest of all. Its colours inspired the modern Buddhist flag.

Ariṭṭha and the ten pictures

Disciples · MN 22

Ariṭṭha held that the things the Buddha called obstructions do not obstruct one who indulges in them. The monks reminded him of the ten pictures the Buddha had given for sensual pleasures, a bare skeleton, a scrap of meat, a grass torch and the rest. He would not let go, and the Buddha called him to account.

Ten pictures for one thing, sensual pleasures: the objects that greed fastens on, each picture showing a different danger. As “The lists reduced to their factors” shows for the taints, floods, bonds and fetters, many names can point to one factor, greed, each name catching it in a different act.

Selā and the seed

Disciples · SN 5.9

Māra tried to frighten the nun Selā out of her meditation: who made this puppet, where is its maker, where does it come from and where does it end? She answered that it was made neither by itself nor by another. It comes to be because of a cause and ceases when the cause breaks up, as a seed grows in a field from the soil and its moisture.

The purification by overcoming doubt (CMA IX §31) in two verses: mind and matter seen to have conditions, so that doubt about a maker falls away. In the Guide to §31 the meditator turns dependent arising on his own mind and matter: past ignorance, craving, clinging and kamma, not chance or a maker, account for them: “The wheel across three lives” turned on oneself. Matching her verse to this stage is the site's reading; the sutta names no stage. It pairs with Vajirā's chariot, the stage before it, discerning mind and matter with no being behind it.

Lives and worlds

What carries on from one life to the next, and where?

Maṭṭhakuṇḍalī's last thought

Disciples · Dhammapada commentary, v. 2

A miserly brahmin's son lay dying, left on the veranda so visitors would not see inside the house. The Buddha passed and let himself be seen. The boy's mind filled with faith, he died with that thought, and he was reborn in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven.

Death-proximate kamma in “Kamma, death and rebirth”: the last javanas were full of faith, and the rebirth-linking that followed took up their object.

Queen Mallikā's seven days

Disciples · Dhammapada commentary, v. 151

Queen Mallikā was generous and devout, but as she died her mind went to one wrong deed she had hidden from the king. She was reborn in a woeful state for seven days, and then in the Tusita heaven.

The other face of death-proximate kamma: one unwholesome last thought outweighed a good life, but only for a while. Her stored good kamma then took its turn (“Kamma, death and rebirth”).

Aṅgulimāla and the stones

Disciples · MN 86

The bandit Aṅgulimāla had killed many. Meeting the Buddha, he went forth and became an arahant. On alms round people threw stones and sticks at him. The Buddha said: “Bear it, brahmin. You are experiencing here and now the result of deeds for which you might have suffered in hell for many years.”

Kamma by time (“Kamma, death and rebirth”): what could ripen in this life did. With arahantship there is no next life, so kamma waiting for later lives could never ripen.

Devadatta's weighty kamma

Disciples · Vinaya, Cūḷavagga VII; Dhammapada commentary, v. 17

The Buddha's cousin Devadatta drew the Buddha's blood with a rock hurled from Vulture Peak, and split the Saṅgha. The commentary says that as he came at last to ask forgiveness, the earth swallowed him and he was reborn in the Avīci hell.

Wounding a Buddha and splitting the Saṅgha are two of the five heinous deeds: weighty kamma, which ripens before any other kind (“Kamma, death and rebirth”). His last act was to take refuge, a wholesome thought at death, and still the weighty kamma came first. The commentary still holds out hope: the Buddha ordained him, it says, knowing that as a monk he could lay a condition for his eventual release, and he will be a paccekabuddha a hundred thousand aeons from now.

Why Moggallāna was beaten

Disciples · Dhammapada commentary, vv. 137–140

Late in life the great disciple Mahā Moggallāna was beaten to death by hired bandits. The commentary traces this to a deed many lives before, when he turned on his own blind parents; the Dhammapada commentary says he killed them, the Jātaka commentary that he stopped.

Kamma that can ripen “in any later life” (“Kamma, death and rebirth”) waits for its chance, even for an arahant; so the Jātaka telling says. In the Dhammapada telling the deed was the killing of his parents, a heinous deed that ripened first in hell; what met him at the end was its remainder. Either way, what arahantship ends is new kamma and any further rebirth.

King Nimi's chariot

Jātaka · Ja 541

Sakka sent his charioteer Mātali to bring the righteous King Nimi to the Tāvatiṃsa heaven. On the way Mātali showed him the hells, naming the deeds that led there, and then the heavenly mansions, naming the deeds that earned them.

A guided tour of the hells and sense-sphere heavens of “Thirty-one planes”. Each world is paired with a kind of deed, the same pairing as kamma “by place” in “Kamma, death and rebirth”.

The brahmins of Sālā

Disciples · MN 41

The brahmin householders of Sālā asked why some beings go to bad destinations after death and some to good ones. The Buddha named ten ways to act wrongly, three by body, four by speech and three by mind, and the ten ways to act rightly that are their opposite.

The ten courses of “Ten ways to act”, as the Buddha first taught them: the three doors, and a deed matched to every world of “Thirty-one planes”.

Subha and the dog

Disciples · MN 135, with the Majjhima commentary

The commentary tells that the miser Todeyya was reborn as a dog in his own house, and showed his son where he had buried his treasure. Subha then came to the Buddha with a question: why are some people long-lived and others short-lived, some rich and others poor? Beings, the Buddha said, are the owners and heirs of their deeds.

“From kamma to its results” in seven pairs: one kind of deed gives the rebirth, and the same deed, ripening within a later human life, shapes its length, health, looks and wealth.

The frog who heard the Buddha's voice

Disciples · Vimānavatthu 5.1, with its commentary

A frog at the edge of a lake took pleasure in the sound of the Buddha's voice. A cowherd leaning on his staff crushed it without knowing. It died with that pleasure in mind, and was reborn as a deva in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven.

“Who is reborn where” says a being's next life depends on what it dies with. The frog lived in a woeful plane, but died with a wholesome sense-sphere citta, and its rebirth-linking was among the devas.

Practice

What do I do with all this?

The rose-apple tree

The Buddha's life · MN 36

As a boy, the Bodhisatta sat in the shade of a rose-apple tree while his father was at work (at the ploughing festival, the commentaries say), and entered the first jhāna. Years later, worn out by austerities, he remembered it and asked: “Could that be the path to awakening?”

On the tree in “The tree of purification”, concentration is a root. The commentaries say his subject was the breath, one of the eleven in “Forty meditation subjects” that can reach all five jhānas.

Cūḷapanthaka's white cloth

Disciples · Dhammapada commentary, v. 25

Cūḷapanthaka could not learn a single verse in four months, and his brother told him to leave. The Buddha gave him a clean white cloth to rub while repeating “removing dirt”. As the cloth grew soiled he saw impermanence; then the Buddha told him that the real dust is lust, hate and delusion, and he became an arahant.

A subject fitted to the person (“Forty meditation subjects”): a simple object you can see and touch, which ripened into insight (“The tree of purification”). The Buddha's verses then turned the outer dirt into the three unwholesome roots of “A map of the eighty-nine”: what is to be removed is lust, hate and delusion.

The goldsmith and the lotus

Disciples · Dhammapada commentary, v. 285; Ja 25

A young monk, a goldsmith for five hundred lives, made no progress with meditation on the foulness of the body. The Buddha gave him a lotus instead. Its beauty steadied his mind; then the flower withered, and he saw impermanence.

“Forty meditation subjects” in action, with a twist: by the rule, a young man prone to lust is given foulness, but five hundred lives of handling red gold had shaped this mind, and only a beautiful object suited it.

Āḷāra and Uddaka

The Buddha's life · MN 26

Before his awakening, the Bodhisatta reached the base of nothingness under Āḷāra Kālāma, and neither perception nor non-perception under Uddaka Rāmaputta. He left both, seeing they did not lead to Nibbāna. After awakening he thought first of teaching them, but both had died.

The highest calm leads to the highest immaterial planes of “Thirty-one planes”, with lifespans of 60,000 and 84,000 aeons, but not out of the round. That is why the tree in “The tree of purification” needs a trunk of insight.

Paṭācārā and the water

Disciples · Therīgāthā 112–116; Dhammapada commentary, v. 113

Having lost her whole family, Paṭācārā became a nun. Washing her feet, she watched the water trickle down, some a little way, some further, as beings die young, in middle age, or old. That night, as she drew down the wick of her lamp, her mind was freed.

The knowledge of rise and fall in “The tree of purification”, seen first in water and then in her own mind. In her verse the lamp going out is the image of the mind's release, Nibbāna, not of death. The water showed her death at the three stages of a life, young, middle-aged and old (Dhammapada commentary VIII.12): the rise and fall of a whole life. The Abhidhamma's rise and fall is of momentary formations, arising and ceasing by condition and by moment; seeing formations across a lifetime and its three stages is where the Manual's comprehension begins, “by way of duration” (CMA, Guide to IX §32).

Koṇḍañña's eye of Dhamma

The Buddha's life · SN 56.11

At the first sermon in the Deer Park, while the Buddha was speaking, Koṇḍañña understood: “Whatever is subject to arising is all subject to cessation.” The Buddha exclaimed, “Koṇḍañña has understood!”

“The four noble truths” reads this sermon the Abhidhamma way: each truth a set of realities with its own task, and all four seen in a single moment. The commentaries take Koṇḍañña's insight as stream-entry: path knowledge took Nibbāna as its object and the first three fetters fell away. The sutta says only that the eye of Dhamma arose. The Saṃyutta commentary takes it here as the first path alone, and has him established in the fruit of stream-entry (SN-a on SN 56.11, CS §1081); that fruition follows the path in the same process is the Manual's account (CMA IX §34).

Kāla, the paid listener

Anāthapiṇḍika paid his son Kāla to go and hear the Buddha. The Buddha arranged that he could not hold the words in mind, so he had to keep listening, and he entered the stream. Offered his fee the next day, Kāla was ashamed to take it.

Stream-entry in “Four noble persons”: the first path cuts identity view, doubt and clinging to rites for good, and a stream-enterer will never again be reborn in a woeful plane.

Soṇa's lute

Disciples · AN 6.55; Vinaya, Mahāvagga V

The monk Soṇa walked up and down in meditation until his feet bled, and still his mind was not freed, so he thought of going home. The Buddha asked him about his lute: did it play when the strings were too tight, or too slack? Only when evenly tuned. So too with energy, he said.

The five faculties of “Thirty-seven requisites, fourteen states” tuned like strings: no one of them may run ahead, and energy has to be kept in balance with concentration, with mindfulness watching both.

Nāgasamāla and the dancer

Disciples · Theragāthā 4.1 (vv. 267–270); Theragāthā commentary

On his alms round the monk Nāgasamāla saw a dancer in the main street, dressed and garlanded, dancing to music, and she looked to him like a snare that Death had set out. Wise attention arose in him, the danger became plain, disenchantment stood firm, and his mind was freed. The commentary reads his four verses as the insight knowledges, one after another.

“Ten knowledges, one by one” in four verses. The verse's own words are wise attention, danger and disenchantment; the commentary unfolds them as rise and fall, breaking-up, appearance as fearful, danger, disenchantment and the wish to be free, ending in indifference, then path and fruit (CMA IX §§25, 33–34). Marking mature rise and fall, dissolution and equanimity is the site's reading of the commentary's words; it does not say which phase of rise and fall it means. The fear is the fearfulness of formations, seen by knowledge, not his own fright: the knowledge of fear does not itself fear (Vism XXI §751). Wise attention is where it turned (CMA, Guide to III §13, after Ledi Sayadaw): the same sight that feeds greed-rooted javanas in one onlooker gave rise to wholesome ones in him. The swinging body moved by mind-born air links to “How matter is made” (CMA VI §§4, 11); that link is the site's.

Yasa wakes in the night

Disciples · Vinaya, Mahāvagga I.7–8

Yasa woke in the night in his pleasure palace and saw his musicians asleep around him, sprawled with their instruments, drooling and muttering, like a charnel ground. The danger struck him, his mind settled in disenchantment, and he walked out of the city in the dark crying that it was oppressive. At the Deer Park the Buddha told him that here nothing was oppressive, and taught him; he entered the stream, and soon after was freed.

Danger and disenchantment here are plain words, not yet the knowledges of “Ten knowledges, one by one”: Yasa was weary of a house of pleasure, not yet of all formations. The site links the story to danger, disenchantment and the wish to be free as an image of what those knowledges feel like, not as the knowledges themselves. The canonical sequence after it fits the Manual's: the Dhamma-eye at the four truths, which the commentaries take as the path of stream-entry (CMA IX §34), and release while “reviewing the ground he had seen and known”. The Visuddhimagga says a stream-enterer who has reviewed goes on to the next path “on that same seat or later” by turning insight on formations again (Vism XXII §813). A Vinaya sub-commentary takes Yasa's “reviewing” as seeing the same truths again with the three higher paths (Vimativinodanī on Mv I.7, CS §28), so the site does not mark it as reviewing knowledge.

Mahānāga and the conjured elephant

Disciples · Visuddhimagga XX §733; Sammohavinodanī §882; Majjhima and Aṅguttara commentaries

The arahant Dhammadinna saw that his old teacher Mahānāga, who thought himself an arahant, was still a worldling, and went to him. The old man answered a thousand questions without a pause. Then Dhammadinna asked him to conjure an elephant and make it charge; Mahānāga jumped up to run, and his pupil caught his robe: does an arahant feel fear?

The imperfections of “Ten knowledges, one by one” as the Visuddhimagga illustrates them (CMA IX §32). Its placement is the Visuddhimagga's own; the Sammohavinodanī and the Majjhima commentary use the story for overestimation, which the Vibhaṅga defines as a kind of conceit, and the Aṅguttara commentary for defilements held down by attainment. The lesson is suppression against cutting off: jhāna holds defilements down and leaves their seeds, the path uproots them (Guide to CMA I §§30–31). Note what the test showed. His running was fear of a defiled kind, not the knowledge of fear, which does not itself fear (Vism XXI §751); the knowledge of fear comes later in the series, after rise and fall and dissolution. Marking the story at comprehension, tender rise and fall and the imperfections follows the Sammohavinodanī and the Visuddhimagga together; neither says which knowledge Mahānāga himself had reached.

Dīghāvu on his sickbed

Disciples · SN 55.3

The lay disciple Dīghāvu lay gravely ill, his pain growing, and the Buddha came to his house. Finding that he already had the faith and virtue of a stream-enterer, the Buddha gave him more to do: see all formations as impermanent, the impermanent as suffering, suffering as not self, and keep in mind giving up, fading and ceasing. Dīghāvu died soon after, and the Buddha declared him a non-returner.

The three contemplations of “Ten knowledges, one by one” (CMA IX §24) at a deathbed, in a layman: impermanence in all formations, suffering in the impermanent, not-self in suffering. Linking his six perceptions to the three marks and the contemplations is the site's reading; the commentary calls them insight. The canon's words are “contemplating” impermanence and “perceiving” the other five (anupassī, saññī); in the Abhidhamma's terms such seeing is the work of wisdom, not of perception alone. His pain is painful bodily feeling, which arises only in one result citta, unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness (CMA III §3); whether his mind grieved or saw depended on the javanas that followed. Virtue, here the noble ones' virtue of the stream-enterer, is the ground the Buddha tells him to stand on.

Sāmāvatī and the burning palace

Disciples · Ud 7.10; Udāna commentary; Dhammapada commentary, vv. 21–23

While King Udena was away in his park, the women's quarters of his palace burned, and five hundred women died with Queen Sāmāvatī at their head. When the monks asked about them, the Buddha said that among them were stream-enterers, once-returners and non-returners, and that none had died without fruit. The commentaries say that as the fire came, Sāmāvatī told her women to be heedful, and they turned to watching feeling itself.

Feeling in “Feeling, roots and objects”: the pain of fire on the body is painful bodily feeling, which arises in one citta only, unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness, a result of past kamma (CMA III §3). Grief is another thing, displeasure in the two aversion-rooted cittas, and it comes only in the javanas. To discern feeling is to take the feeling itself as the object, not the fire. The commentary's word is pariggaha, “discerning”, the word of the stage of discerning mind and matter (CMA IX §30), not sammasana, comprehension, so the site marks it there; this is the site's reading. The Udāna's own word is the one for the end: none of them died without fruit.

The monk of the mirage and the foam

Disciples · Dhammapada v. 46, with its commentary; SN 22.95

A monk who could not reach arahantship was on his way to ask the Buddha for a sharper subject when he watched a mirage, solid from far off and nothing up close, and took his own body to be like it. Resting by the river, he watched great clots of foam rise and burst, and saw that the body too arises and breaks. The Buddha spoke the verse of foam and mirage to him, and he was freed.

Two readings of one image, and the site keeps both. The canon's foam and mirage picture the third mark as the Manual words it, non-self in the sense of corelessness (CMA IX §32); the commentary's foam is rise and fall, a nature image turned into the meditation object, as with Paṭācārā's water and Kisāgotamī's lamp. Linking the story to the knowledge of rise and fall is the site's reading; the commentary does not name the knowledge. As with those stories, the commentary speaks of “this body”, a whole individual; the Abhidhamma's rise and fall is of momentary formations, mind and matter arising and ceasing (“Ten knowledges, one by one”).

Vessantara's gifts

Jātaka · Ja 547

In the last human life before the final one, the Bodhisatta as Prince Vessantara gave away the kingdom's white elephant, his wealth, and in the end, in tears and for the sake of awakening, his children and his wife. After that life he was reborn in the Tusita heaven.

Sense-sphere wholesome kamma at its fullest (“Kamma, death and rebirth”). Ajātasattu's lost chance

King Ajātasattu, who had killed his father to take the throne, heard the Buddha's discourse on the fruits of the recluse's life, went for refuge and confessed his crime, and the Buddha accepted his confession. When he had left, the Buddha said that had he not killed his father, the eye of Dhamma would have arisen in him then and there.

Weighty kamma (“Kamma, death and rebirth”) can bar the path (“The tree of purification”). A heinous deed closes the door to path attainment in that life (AN 6.87). It does not close the door to refuge, confession and good kamma.

Sāriputta and the elephant's footprint

Disciples · MN 28

Sāriputta told the monks that as every creature's footprint fits inside an elephant's, every wholesome teaching fits inside the four noble truths. Then he took one moment of seeing and read it again and again: as elements, as five aggregates, as dependent origination, and as the truths.

Sāriputta does in a few lines what “One moment, every lens” does with the mango: one moment of seeing, read as matter, as five aggregates, as dependent origination and as the truths. Each reading is complete in its own terms, and none of them needs a self to finish the account.

The second dart

Disciples · SN 36.6

Both an ordinary person and a noble disciple feel pleasant, painful and neutral feeling. What is the difference? When pain touches the ordinary person, he grieves, and feels two pains, of body and of mind, like a man struck by one dart and then by a second. The noble disciple feels only the first.

The same pain, met twice. As in “Two moments, side by side”, the moments that receive the object are results and are the same in both people; the two processes part at the javanas, and the second dart is made there.
