Kisāgotamī and the mustard seed

Mad with grief, Kisāgotamī carried her dead child from house to house asking for medicine. The Buddha asked her for a mustard seed from a house where no one had died. She found none, laid the child down, and understood that death comes to all.

Where
Sāvatthī and the Jetavana monastery
Who
  • Kisāgotamī, “lean Gotamī”, a young mother from a poor family
  • The Buddha, at Jetavana
Source
Dhammapada commentary, v. 114

The story

Gotamī was called Kisā, “lean”, for her thin frame. She came from a poor family and married into a rich one. The Aṅguttara and Therīgāthā commentaries add that she was treated there with contempt until she bore a son, and then honoured. When the boy was old enough to run about, he died.

She could not accept it. Unable to believe he was gone, she carried his body on her hip from door to door in Sāvatthī, asking for medicine for her son. People laughed at her. A wise man, seeing her state, told her there was one who knew the medicine: the Buddha, at Jetavana.

The Buddha told her he did know the medicine. Bring a mustard seed, he said, from a house in which no one has ever died. She went from house to house. Everyone would gladly give a seed; but in every house someone had died: “the living are few, the dead are many.” By evening she understood. This was not her misfortune alone; it was the way of all who live. The commentary says that her heart, soft with love for her son, grew firm; she left his body in the forest and went back.

She told the Buddha she had found no such house. Death, he said, carries off the one who clings to children and herds as a flood carries off a sleeping village; hearing this, she became a stream-enterer, and asked to go forth. As a nun, the commentary says, she was one day watching the flames of a lamp flare up and die down, and saw in them the rising and falling of beings. The Buddha spoke the verse: better one day seeing the deathless than a hundred years without seeing it. She became an arahant, and he named her foremost among the nuns who wore rough robes.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Her grief is displeasure (domanassadomanassa · displeasure painful mental feeling; only in the two hate-rooted cittas), which in “A map of the eighty-nine” occurs only in the two hate-rooted cittas: grief is aversion toward loss. Its soil is attachment (“from the dear comes sorrow”, Dhp 212), and the commentary says her heart, soft with love for her son, had to grow firm before she could let him go. The search turned a private wound into a universal fact, and the verse on death turned that fact into the path.

Read with: A map of the eighty-nine · An ensemble of fifty-two

The dhammas it shows

  • hatred dosa: her grief is displeasure, a form of aversion
  • greed lobha: love that clings to the child: from the dear comes grief
  • feeling vedanā: displeasure, the feeling of the hate-rooted cittas
  • wisdom paññindriya: seeing at last that death comes to all

Where it is told

  • Dhammapada v. 114, with its commentary (story VIII.13)
  • Dhammapada v. 287, the verse at which she entered the stream
  • AN 1.246, foremost among nuns who wear coarse robes
  • Therīgāthā 10.1, her own verses (vv. 213–223)
  • SN 5.3, Kisāgotamī and Māra

The mustard seed belongs to the commentaries, which differ in small things: in the Dhammapada commentary she leaves the child in the forest, in the Aṅguttara commentary at the charnel ground. Her own verses in the Therīgāthā speak of a woman's sorrows in general and of her awakening, without the seed.
