Disciples · Level 6

Mahānāga and the conjured elephant

The arahant Dhammadinna saw that his old teacher Mahānāga, who thought himself an arahant, was still a worldling, and went to him. The old man answered a thousand questions without a pause. Then Dhammadinna asked him to conjure an elephant and make it charge; Mahānāga jumped up to run, and his pupil caught his robe: does an arahant feel fear?

Where
Monasteries of ancient Sri Lanka, Uccavālika and Talaṅgara
Who
  • Mahānāga of Uccavālika, an old elder who believed he had reached the goal sixty years before
  • Dhammadinna of Talaṅgara, his former pupil, an arahant with the analytical knowledges and a teacher of many monks
Source
Visuddhimagga XX §733; Sammohavinodanī §882; Majjhima and Aṅguttara commentaries

The story

The commentaries tell this story of elders of ancient Sri Lanka. Dhammadinna of Talaṅgara was a great arahant, master of the analytical knowledges, who advised a large community of monks. One day, sitting in his day's quarters, he turned his mind to his own teacher, the elder Mahānāga of Uccavālika, and wondered whether his work as a monk had reached its end. He saw that the old man was still a worldling, and that unless someone went to him he would die a worldling. He rose into the air and came down beside him, paid his respects and sat to one side.

Why have you come at this odd hour, friend? To ask questions, sir. Ask, then; I will say what I know. Dhammadinna asked a thousand questions, and the old man answered each one without hesitating. Your knowledge is very sharp, sir, said the pupil; when did you reach this state? Sixty years ago, friend. And do you also make use of your concentration, sir? That is no burden, friend.

Then make an elephant, sir. The elder made one, all white. Now, sir, make it come straight at you, ears spread, tail stretched out, trunk curled into its mouth, trumpeting terribly. The elder did, and as he saw the terrible thing bearing down on him he leapt up to run. Dhammadinna reached out, caught the edge of his robe, and asked: sir, does one whose taints are destroyed feel fear?

In that moment the old man knew what he was. He crouched at his pupil's feet and asked him to be his support. That is why I came, sir, said Dhammadinna; do not worry. He gave him a meditation subject, and the elder took it up on his walking path and became an arahant at the third step. The Visuddhimagga and the Sammohavinodanī both add that he was of hating temperament, and the Visuddhimagga that monks like him are shaken by the light that arises in insight.

The other commentaries know the test in other forms. In the Majjhima commentary Dhammadinna, on his way to a great monastery, meets an unnamed elder of more than sixty years who claims arahantship; the charging elephant gives him away too, and Dhammadinna comforts him: do not grieve, sir, overestimation comes only to those who practise. At Cittalapabbata another elder is asked to conjure a lotus pond with a woman singing and dancing among the flowers, and told to keep looking at her, and the defilements held down for sixty years stir. In the Aṅguttara commentary the deceived elder is Dhammadinna's meditation teacher Mahātissa, and the test is the lotus and a girl of sixteen.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

The imperfections of “Ten knowledges, one by one” as the Visuddhimagga illustrates them (CMA IX §32). Its placement is the Visuddhimagga's own; the Sammohavinodanī and the Majjhima commentary use the story for overestimation, which the Vibhaṅga defines as a kind of conceit, and the Aṅguttara commentary for defilements held down by attainment. The lesson is suppression against cutting off: jhāna holds defilements down and leaves their seeds, the path uproots them (Guide to CMA I §§30–31). Note what the test showed. His running was fear of a defiled kind, not the knowledge of fear, which does not itself fear (Vism XXI §751); the knowledge of fear comes later in the series, after rise and fall and dissolution. Marking the story at comprehension, tender rise and fall and the imperfections follows the Sammohavinodanī and the Visuddhimagga together; neither says which knowledge Mahānāga himself had reached.

The dhammas it shows

  • conceit māna: overestimation, the conceit of having reached what one has not
  • hatred dosa: of hating temperament, the Visuddhimagga and the Sammohavinodanī say

Where it is told

  • Visuddhimagga XX §§732–733 (CS): the imperfections of insight; the story at the first, the light
  • Sammohavinodanī (Vibhaṅga commentary), on Vibhaṅga XVII, CS §882: the same story, for overestimation (adhimāna)
  • Vibhaṅga XVII, the definition of overestimation (vb17:62.1–62.3)
  • MN 8 Sallekha Sutta, with the Majjhima commentary (Sallekhasuttavaṇṇanā, CS §82): the elders of Haṅkana and Cittalapabbata
  • AN 1.16, with the Aṅguttara commentary (CS §16): Mahātissa, the lotus pond and the girl
  • Sīlakkhandhavagga-abhinavaṭīkā (sub-commentary on DN 2), CS §235: the story cited for fear in one with direct knowledge

This is a commentarial story, with no canonical version, and the versions differ in names and tests. The Visuddhimagga tells it for the first imperfection of insight, the light, which, it says, mostly arises in one who has both calm and insight: the defilements his attainments hold down do not occur, so he thinks he is an arahant. It names two more elders who made the same mistake. The Sammohavinodanī tells it almost word for word, under overestimation, and says who is prone to it: one of pure virtue who has defined mind and matter, overcome doubt by discerning conditions, and is comprehending formations by the three marks. Those with calm alone or insight alone stop short, taking themselves for stream-enterers, once-returners or non-returners when no defilement has shown for ten, twenty or thirty years; one with both may see no defilement for sixty years or more, and take himself for an arahant. A later sub-commentary on DN 2 cites the elephant for the fear that can arise in one with the direct knowledges whose base was never fully understood, and says that comprehension by insight is what removes it.

Opening question

If sixty years without a defilement showing can still be a mistake, how could anyone tell suppression from uprooting in themselves?
