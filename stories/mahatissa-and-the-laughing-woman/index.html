Mahātissa and the laughing woman

On the road to Anurādhapura for alms, the elder Mahātissa met a young wife going home after a quarrel, who laughed out loud on seeing him. He looked up and saw only the bones of her teeth; the perception of foulness he had practised came back to him, and he became an arahant where he stood. Asked by her husband whether a woman had passed, he said he had seen only a heap of bones going along the high road.

Where
The road from Cetiyapabbata to Anurādhapura, in ancient Sri Lanka
Who
  • Mahātissa, an elder living at Cetiyapabbata, walking to the city for alms
  • A young wife, on her way back to her family after a quarrel with her husband
  • Her husband, following her down the road
Source
Visuddhimagga I §15

The story

The Visuddhimagga tells this story to explain a line that recurs in the suttas: on seeing a form with the eye, a monk does not grasp at its signs or its features. Not grasping the sign, it explains, means not taking up the mark of a woman or a man, or of beauty, that would feed the defilements, but stopping at what was seen. Not grasping the features means not taking up the details of hands and feet, of a smile, a laugh, a way of speaking or looking, but only what is really there. Just before, it gives the ancients' gloss on seeing with the eye: the eye does not see, having no mind, and the mind does not see, having no eye; one sees with the mind based on the eye's sensitivity, when door and object meet.

The elder Mahātissa lived at Cetiyapabbata, and one morning was walking to Anurādhapura for alms. A young wife had quarrelled with her husband and left the city early, dressed and adorned like a heavenly maiden, to go back to her family. Meeting the elder on the road, her mind in disorder, she laughed out loud.

The elder looked up to see what it was. In the bones of her teeth he found the perception of foulness, and he became an arahant. As a verse in the text puts it, seeing her teeth he recalled his former perception, and standing right there he reached arahantship.

Her husband came along the road after her and asked: sir, have you seen a woman go by? The elder answered in a verse: whether a woman or a man went past, I could not say; but a frame of bones is going along the high road.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Straight after the story the Visuddhimagga explains restraint by the cognitive process of “The mango and the moment”: when a visible form strikes the eye there arise adverting, eye-consciousness, receiving, investigating and determining, then the javanas. At none of the moments before javana is there restraint or its lack; they arise only at javana. Guarding the doors means guarding the javanas: the Visuddhimagga's city with its gates shut keeps its goods safe even if the inner doors stand open. The process itself is the Manual's eye-door process (CMA IV §6). Restraint of the senses is the second of the four kinds of purified virtue (CMA IX §28), and the Visuddhimagga's placement of the story there is its own; calling it the ground of insight is the site's framing.

Read with: The mango and the moment · The tree of purification

The dhammas it shows

  • visible form vaṇṇa: the flash of teeth, only what was there
  • perception saññā: the perception of foulness, recognised again
  • mindfulness sati: the door shut with mindfulness, the Visuddhimagga says

Where it is told

  • Visuddhimagga I §15 (CS): the restraint of the senses, the story, and the cognitive process
  • Visuddhimagga VI §122 (CS): the same elder, foulness seen in a living body
  • DN 2, the sense-restraint formula (dn2:64.2–64.8)
  • MN 27, the same formula (mn27:15.1)

This is a commentarial anecdote of the Sri Lankan tradition, with no canonical version. That Cetiyapabbata is Mihintale is a modern identification, not in the Visuddhimagga. The Visuddhimagga speaks only of the perception of foulness, a calm subject, and of arahantship; it does not mention insight, and reading his release as insight built on that perception is the site's inference. The Visuddhimagga cites the same elder again in its chapter on foulness, as one who saw foulness in a living body. Read carelessly, the story can sound like contempt for women. The site reads its point as the trained eye and the untrained one; the husband, searching the road for his wife, plays the untrained eye. The same chapter has a starker picture of virtue as ground: two elders bound by robbers with living creepers, who would not break them because of a training rule, and developed insight where they lay (Vism I §19).
