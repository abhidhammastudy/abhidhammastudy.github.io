Mālukyaputta's teaching in brief

In old age Mālukyaputta asked for a teaching in brief, to take into solitude. The Buddha asked: could desire arise for sights you have never seen and never will? No. Then let the seen be only the seen. Mālukyaputta answered in verses: lust grows not from seeing a form, but from attending to its pleasing sign with mindfulness lost.

Where
Late in the life of an aged monk who wanted to live alone
Who
  • Mālukyaputta, an old monk, the same who once demanded answers about the world and the self
  • The Buddha, who first teases him for asking so late
Source
SN 35.95

The story

Mālukyaputta came to the Buddha and asked for a short teaching, so that he could go off alone and practise. What shall I say to the young monks, the Buddha replied, when an old man like you, aged and far along in years, asks for a teaching in brief? Mālukyaputta asked again: even if he was old, perhaps he would understand, and become an heir of the teaching.

So the Buddha asked him. Are there forms known by the eye that you have not seen, have never seen, do not see now and do not expect to see; would you have desire or lust or affection for those? No, venerable sir. And so for sounds, smells, tastes, touches and ideas. Then, he said, let the seen be only the seen, the heard only the heard, the sensed only the sensed, the known only the known. When that is so, you will be neither here nor beyond nor in between, and that is the end of suffering.

Asked whether he had understood, Mālukyaputta answered in verses. Seeing a form with mindfulness lost, attending to its pleasing sign, one feels it with an infatuated mind and holds on; feelings born of the sight multiply, covetousness and harm unsettle the mind, and one is far from Nibbāna. But one who sees a form mindfully is not inflamed by it; he feels it with a mind detached, and as he sees and feels, suffering wears away instead of piling up. The Buddha approved every verse. Mālukyaputta went off alone, and before long he was one of the arahants.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Seeing is one of the fourteen jobs of “What each citta does”, done by a resultant citta that cannot be otherwise. The harm in Mālukyaputta's verses begins after it, in attending to the pleasing sign, at the one station where the javanas run and kamma is made.

Read with: What each citta does

The dhammas it shows

  • visible form vaṇṇa: forms seen, unseen, and never to be seen
  • attention manasikāra: attention is in every citta, the seeing too; the harm is unwise attention to the pleasing sign, which turns the javanas that follow toward lust
  • mindfulness sati: seeing a form mindfully, one is not inflamed

Where it is told

  • SN 35.95 Mālukyaputta Sutta
  • MN 63 Cūḷamālukya Sutta, the same monk and the poisoned arrow
  • Ud 1.10, the same instruction given to Bāhiya

Bāhiya was given the same instruction (Ud 1.10) and understood it on the spot. Here the student explains it back, and the verses place the trouble exactly: not in the seeing, but in what is done after it.
