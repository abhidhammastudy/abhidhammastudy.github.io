Maṭṭhakuṇḍalī's last thought

A miserly brahmin's son lay dying, left on the veranda so visitors would not see inside the house. The Buddha passed and let himself be seen. The boy's mind filled with faith, he died with that thought, and he was reborn in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven.

Where
Sāvatthī
Who
  • Maṭṭhakuṇḍalī, “Burnished Earrings”, a boy of sixteen
  • Adinnapubbaka, “Never-Gave”, his miserly brahmin father
  • The Buddha, on his alms round
Source
Dhammapada commentary, v. 2

The story

In Sāvatthī lived a brahmin so mean that he was called Adinnapubbaka, “one who never gave”. To save the goldsmith's fee he hammered out a pair of earrings for his only son himself, and the boy was called Maṭṭhakuṇḍalī, “Burnished Earrings”.

At sixteen the boy fell ill with jaundice. The father would not pay a physician; he asked doctors what they gave for such an illness and treated the boy himself with bark, and the boy grew worse. When it was clear he would die, the father carried him out to the veranda, so that people coming to mourn would not see the wealth inside the house.

That morning the Buddha, surveying the world, saw the boy. On his alms round he stopped at the door and sent out a ray of light. The boy turned and saw him. Because of his father, he thought, he had never served the Buddha, given to him or heard him teach, and now his hands would not obey him; all he could do was make his mind bright. He set his heart on him in faith, and died with that thought. He was reborn in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven in a golden mansion.

The father went to the charnel ground every day to weep. The young deva, seeing it, took his old form and wept beside him. Asked why, he said he wanted a pair of wheels for his golden chariot, and the only ones that would do were the sun and the moon. “You fool,” said the brahmin, “you weep for what cannot be had.” “The sun and moon can at least be seen,” said the boy. “You weep for one who is gone and cannot be seen. Which of us is the fool?” Then he revealed who he was. The brahmin invited the Buddha to a meal, and the Buddha spoke the second verse of the Dhammapada: mind goes before all things; if one speaks or acts with a pure mind, happiness follows like a shadow that never leaves.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Death-proximate kamma in “Kamma, death and rebirth”: the last javanas were full of faith, and the rebirth-linking that followed took up their object.

Read with: Kamma, death and rebirth · Thirty-one planes

The dhammas it shows

  • faith saddhā: a dying boy's faith in the Buddha
  • avarice macchariya: his father, Never-Gave, would not pay a doctor
  • object ārammaṇa: the Buddha is the object of his last thought
  • kamma kamma: those faithful last javanas give the rebirth

Where it is told

  • Dhammapada v. 2, with its commentary
  • Vimānavatthu 7.9, the Maṭṭhakuṇḍalī mansion story

The commentary has people at the meal doubt whether one can reach heaven by a thought of faith alone, without giving or keeping precepts; the young deva appears to settle the question.
