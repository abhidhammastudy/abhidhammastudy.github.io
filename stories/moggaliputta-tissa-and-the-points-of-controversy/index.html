Moggaliputta Tissa and the points of controversy

Under Asoka's patronage, the chronicles say, men of other sects put on the robe for the gifts and taught their own views as the Buddha's, and the monks' fortnightly observance lapsed for seven years. The elder Moggaliputta Tissa sifted the views, held a council, and set out the Kathāvatthu, the Abhidhamma's book of debates against other maps.

Where
The Asokārāma at Pāṭaliputta, in the reign of King Asoka
Who
  • Moggaliputta Tissa, the elder who, in the Theravāda account, presided over the third council
  • King Asoka, the Mauryan emperor, whose gifts drew crowds into the robe
  • Tissa, the king's younger brother, a monk
Source
Samantapāsādikā, introduction; Mahāvaṃsa V

The story

When the king's gifts to the Saṅgha grew lavish, the Theravāda chronicles tell, ascetics of other sects who had lost their support shaved their heads and put on robes, and went on teaching their own doctrines as the Dhamma. The true monks would not hold the observance with them, and at the Asokārāma it lapsed. A minister sent to restore it misunderstood his order and began to behead the monks who refused, until the king's own brother, himself a monk, sat down in front of the sword.

The king, afraid the deaths were on his head, sent upriver for Moggaliputta Tissa, who had withdrawn to a mountain retreat. The elder taught him that where there is no intention there is no kamma, and then taught him the Dhamma for seven days. Then, with the elder seated behind a curtain, the king called the monks sect by sect and asked each: what did the Fully Awakened One teach? Those who answered with eternalism and the other views were sent out of the Saṅgha in white clothes. Those who answered that he taught analysis, vibhajjavāda, the elder confirmed, and the observance was held in harmony again.

Then, the commentary says, the elder chose a thousand learned monks, recited the Dhamma with them in a third council, and set out the Kathāvatthu, the Points of Controversy, as the fifth book of the Abhidhamma. Its first debate asks whether a person is found as a real and ultimate thing. Its sixth asks whether everything exists, in past, future and present alike. The commentary names the other side in each: the schools that held to the person, and the Sabbatthivāda, the Sarvāstivāda of the north.

The tradition adds one more step. The Buddha himself, it says, had laid down the outline of the book in the heaven of the Thirty-three, foreseeing the disputes, so that the elder only filled in what was already there, and the book could be counted as the Buddha's word.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

The Kathāvatthu is where the Theravāda argued with the other maps of “Other maps of the same ground”: against a person who is more than the elements, and against the Sarvāstivāda's claim that past and future dhammas exist.

Read with: Other maps of the same ground

The dhammas it shows

  • wrong view diṭṭhi: views sorted one by one, sect by sect, before the curtain
  • volition cetanā: where there is no intention, the elder said, no kamma
  • kamma kamma: a past deed conditions now without existing now

Where it is told

  • Samantapāsādikā (the Vinaya commentary), introduction; the third council
  • Mahāvaṃsa V, translated by Wilhelm Geiger
  • Kathāvatthu I.1 and I.6, with the Kathāvatthu commentary; translated as Points of Controversy

The council is known only from Theravāda sources, and the account is commentarial. Asoka's own edicts speak of divisions in the Saṅgha and order that monks who split it be made to wear white, but they name no council and no Moggaliputta Tissa. The northern schools remember the period differently, and modern scholars see the Kathāvatthu as built up over time.
