Nakulapitā, sick in body, not in mind

An old, ailing householder asked the Buddha for a teaching. Train yourself, said the Buddha: though my body is afflicted, my mind will not be afflicted. Sāriputta explained how: the body's pain need not become the mind's grief, if one does not take the body, or feeling or anything else, as self.

Where
The Deer Park in Bhesakaḷā Grove, at Suṃsumāragira in the Bhagga country
Who
  • Nakulapitā, “Nakula's father”, an old householder of the Bhaggas
  • Nakulamātā, his wife, his equal in faith
  • The Buddha, staying in the Bhagga country
  • Sāriputta, who explained the Buddha's brief words
Source
SN 22.1

The story

Nakulapitā came to the Buddha and said: I am old, venerable sir, worn out, at the last stage of life, sick in body and often ill, and I seldom get to see the Blessed One and the monks. Let the Blessed One teach me something for my lasting good. The Buddha agreed: your body is afflicted, householder, and for anyone carrying such a body to claim even a moment's health would be foolish. So train yourself thus: though I am afflicted in body, my mind will not be afflicted.

Nakulapitā went to Sāriputta, who saw at once that his faculties were serene and his face bright, and asked if he had heard a talk on the Dhamma. He told him what the Buddha had said, and Sāriputta asked whether he had asked how far a body can be afflicted without the mind. Nakulapitā had not; he would come a long way to hear it. Sāriputta explained.

The untaught ordinary person regards form as self, or self as having form, or form in self, or self in form, and lives obsessed with the idea “I am form, form is mine”. When that form changes, sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief and despair arise: such a one is afflicted in body and in mind. So too with feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. The noble disciple does not regard them so; when the body changes, the mind does not follow it into grief. He is afflicted in body, but not in mind.

Elsewhere the couple are named together as the foremost among lay followers who were intimate with the Buddha, and in AN 4.55 they tell him that they have never been unfaithful to each other even in thought, and wish to meet again in future lives. The commentary says they had been his parents in many former births, and that on first seeing him Nakulapitā called him “son”.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

In “Feeling, roots and objects” bodily feeling alone is painful or pleasant, and mental pain comes only with the two hate-rooted cittas. Nakulapitā's training is the gap between the two.

Read with: Feeling, roots and objects

The dhammas it shows

  • feeling vedanā: bodily pain, without mental grief following it
  • body-sensitivity kāya: body-sensitivity, the only door where pain is felt as pain
  • hatred dosa: grief, which arises only with aversion, is what he trains to stop
  • wrong view diṭṭhi: taking form as self turns bodily change into grief

Where it is told

  • SN 22.1 Nakulapitu Sutta
  • AN 4.55 Samajīvī Sutta (the couple)
  • AN 6.16, Nakulamātā comforts him in a serious illness
  • AN 1.257 and 1.266, the list of foremost lay followers: Nakulapitā and Nakulamātā, foremost among those intimate with the Buddha
  • AN 1.266, Nakulamātā

SN 22.1 is the first sutta of the Saṃyutta on the aggregates; the tradition placed an old man's sickness at the gate of that whole collection. The past lives as the Buddha's parents come from the commentary.
