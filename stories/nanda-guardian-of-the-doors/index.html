Nanda, guardian of the doors

The Buddha's half-brother Nanda was ordained on his wedding day and pined for his bride. In time he became an arahant, and the Buddha named him foremost among those who guard the sense doors: whichever way Nanda looked, he looked with full awareness, so that longing and grief could not flow in.

Where
Kapilavatthu, Jetavana, and a journey to the Tāvatiṃsa heaven
Who
  • Nanda, the Buddha's half-brother, son of Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī
  • Janapadakalyāṇī, “the beauty of the land”, the Sakyan bride he left behind
  • The Buddha, his elder half-brother and teacher
Source
Ud 3.2; AN 8.9; AN 1.230

The story

The commentary tells how it began. On the Buddha's first visit home, on the day of Nanda's wedding and consecration, the Buddha handed him his bowl and walked away. Nanda, out of respect, followed him to the monastery holding it, and his bride called after him, hair half combed: “Come back soon, my lord.” At the monastery the Buddha asked if he would go forth, and Nanda could not bring himself to refuse.

In the Udāna, Nanda tells the monks he is not happy in the holy life: he keeps thinking of the Sakyan girl and her parting words, and means to give up the training. The Buddha takes him by the arm and, as quickly as one might bend an arm, takes him to the Tāvatiṃsa heaven, where five hundred nymphs with feet like doves are waiting on Sakka. Which is lovelier, he asks, your bride or these? Beside them, says Nanda, she is like a scorched she-monkey with her ears and nose cut off. The Buddha promises him the nymphs if he practises.

Back at Jetavana, the other monks hear of it and call Nanda a hireling, a monk bought for nymphs. Ashamed, he goes off alone and practises with all his strength, and soon becomes an arahant. He comes to release the Buddha from his promise; the Buddha already knows.

In AN 8.9 the Buddha praises him: Nanda is strong, handsome and passionate, and yet he lives the holy life in full, because he guards the doors of his senses, eats with moderation, stays wakeful, and is mindful. If he looks to the east, he looks having considered it fully, so that longing and grief do not flood in.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

The Buddha met Nanda's desire where it was and led it past itself: once free, Nanda wanted nothing of the promise. The doors he then learned to guard are the doors of “The mango and the moment”. Guarding them means the seeing is not followed by unwholesome javanas.

Read with: An ensemble of fifty-two · The mango and the moment

The dhammas it shows

  • eye-sensitivity cakkhu: the eye-door he learned to guard
  • greed lobha: pining for his bride, then for nymphs
  • mindfulness sati: looking in any direction with full awareness
  • shame hiri: shamed by being called a hireling, he strives

Where it is told

  • Ud 3.2 Nanda Sutta
  • AN 8.9 Nanda Sutta
  • AN 1.230, the list of foremost disciples: Nanda, foremost among those who guard the doors of the senses
  • Aśvaghoṣa's Saundarananda, a Sanskrit poem on the same story, 1st–2nd century

The she-monkey comparison is in the Udāna itself; the commentary adds that on the way the Buddha showed him a monkey burned in a forest fire, to make the point.
