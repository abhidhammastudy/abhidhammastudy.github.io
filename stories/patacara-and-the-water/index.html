Paṭācārā and the water

Having lost her whole family, Paṭācārā became a nun. Washing her feet, she watched the water trickle down, some a little way, some further, as beings die young, in middle age, or old. That night, as she drew down the wick of her lamp, her mind was freed.

Where
The road to Sāvatthī, the river Aciravatī, and the nuns' dwelling
Who
  • Paṭācārā, a merchant's daughter of Sāvatthī
  • The Buddha, at Jetavana
Source
Therīgāthā 112–116; Dhammapada commentary, v. 113

The story

The commentary tells her life before. She was the daughter of a rich merchant of Sāvatthī, and ran away with a servant of the house. Pregnant with her second child, she set out for her parents' home with her husband and her little son. A storm broke; her husband went to cut sticks for a shelter, was bitten by a snake, and died. She gave birth in the night, crouching over both children in the rain.

In the morning she found her husband dead. At the river Aciravatī, swollen with the rain, she could not carry both children across. She took the newborn over, laid it on the far bank, and turned back for the elder. Midstream, a hawk took the baby. She threw up her arms and screamed, and the elder boy, thinking she called him, stepped into the river and was swept away.

Near Sāvatthī she asked a traveller about her family. In the night's storm their house had fallen; her father, mother and brother were burning on one pyre, there, where the smoke rose. She went out of her mind. Her clothes fell from her, and she wandered, and came to Jetavana, where the Buddha said to her: “Sister, recover your mindfulness.” She came to herself. He told her that the tears she had shed in the round of births were more than the waters of the four oceans. She entered the stream, and went forth.

Her own verses tell the rest. (The Dhammapada commentary ends differently: as she watched the water the Buddha appeared to her in a ray of light and spoke verse 113, on seeing rise and fall, and she became an arahant there.) Men plough their fields and sow, and prosper; why do I, keeping the training, not reach Nibbāna? Washing her feet, she watched the water run down the slope, and her mind became still like a well-trained horse. She took her lamp into her cell, sat on the bed, and drew down the wick with a pin. As the lamp went out, her mind was freed. She became the foremost of the nuns in the discipline, and a teacher of many.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

The knowledge of rise and fall in “The tree of purification”, seen first in water and then in her own mind. In her verse the lamp going out is the image of the mind's release, Nibbāna, not of death. The water showed her death at the three stages of a life, young, middle-aged and old (Dhammapada commentary VIII.12): the rise and fall of a whole life. The Abhidhamma's rise and fall is of momentary formations, arising and ceasing by condition and by moment; seeing formations across a lifetime and its three stages is where the Manual's comprehension begins, “by way of duration” (CMA, Guide to IX §32).

Read with: Kamma, death and rebirth · The tree of purification

The dhammas it shows

  • hatred dosa: grief that drove her out of her mind
  • mindfulness sati: Sister, recover your mindfulness
  • wisdom paññindriya: rise and fall seen in water and a lamp

Where it is told

  • Therīgāthā 5.10, her verses (vv. 112–116)
  • Dhammapada v. 113, with its commentary (her life; story VIII.12)
  • Dhammapada vv. 288–289, the verses at which she entered the stream
  • Therīgāthā 5.11 and 6.1, the nuns she taught

Her name is usually explained as “one who walks with her cloak fallen”; some modern scholars read it as “one who walks in the right way”.
