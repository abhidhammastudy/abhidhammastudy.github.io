Pukkusāti and the elements

One night in a potter's shed, the Buddha taught a stranger, Pukkusāti, to see his body as earth, water, fire, air, space and consciousness, none of it “mine”. Pukkusāti recognised his teacher, but while seeking robes for ordination he was killed by a cow. The Buddha declared him a non-returner.

Where
The potter Bhaggava's workshop in Rājagaha, one night
Who
  • Pukkusāti, a monk who had gone forth out of faith in the Buddha, without ever having met him
  • The Buddha, wandering in Magadha, unrecognised
  • Bhaggava, the potter who lent his workshop
Source
MN 140

The story

The commentary says Pukkusāti was the king of Takkasilā, far to the north-west, and a friend by letter of King Bimbisāra. Bimbisāra once sent him, engraved on a gold plate, an account of the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Saṅgha. Pukkusāti read it, shaved his head, put on a robe, and walked alone the whole way to Rājagaha.

In the sutta, the Buddha arrives at Rājagaha and asks the potter Bhaggava for a night in his workshop. A monk is already there: Pukkusāti, who has gone forth under the Buddha's name without ever seeing him. Both sit in silence most of the night. Then the Buddha asks whom he follows, and offers to teach.

He teaches that a person is made of six elements: earth, water, fire, air, space and consciousness. Each is examined, inside the body and outside it: whatever is hard in the body, the hair, nails, teeth, bones, is earth, and it is to be seen as “this is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.” What remains is a pure, bright equanimity, and even that is seen as conditioned. Pukkusāti understands that this is the Teacher, and asks forgiveness for having called him “friend”.

He asks for full ordination. The Buddha does not ordain one without bowl and robes, so Pukkusāti goes out to find them, and a cow gores him to death. The monks ask what has become of him. He was wise, the Buddha says, and practised in line with the Dhamma: he has broken the five lower fetters, has been reborn spontaneously, and will reach final Nibbāna there without returning. The commentary names that world as the Pure Abodes.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

The four great elements are the core of the 28 kinds of matter in “How matter is made”. Analysing the body into elements is also one of the subjects in “Forty meditation subjects”.

Read with: How matter is made · Forty meditation subjects

The dhammas it shows

  • earth pathavī: whatever is hard in the body: hair, nails, bones
  • water āpo: whatever is liquid and cohesive in the body
  • fire tejo: whatever warms, digests and ages the body
  • air vāyo: whatever moves: breath and the winds of the body

Where it is told

  • MN 140 Dhātuvibhaṅga Sutta
  • Majjhima commentary on MN 140 (the king of Takkasilā)

Pukkusāti and Bāhiya both die by a cow. The Dhammapada commentary (on v. 66) says one yakkhinī, in the form of a cow, killed four men, these two among them: as four rich young men in a past life they had murdered a courtesan for her jewels, and she died vowing to kill them in turn.

Opening question

If hardness, cohesion, heat and motion are all there is in “my hand”, what does “my” point to?
