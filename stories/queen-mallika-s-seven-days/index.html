Queen Mallikā's seven days

Queen Mallikā was generous and devout, but as she died her mind went to one wrong deed she had hidden from the king. She was reborn in a woeful state for seven days, and then in the Tusita heaven.

Where
Sāvatthī, the court of King Pasenadi of Kosala
Who
  • Mallikā, a garland-maker's daughter who became Pasenadi's chief queen
  • King Pasenadi, of Kosala, a lay follower of the Buddha
  • The Buddha, at Jetavana
Source
Dhammapada commentary, v. 151

The story

Mallikā was the daughter of a garland-maker. As a girl she gave her own meal to the Buddha, and soon after King Pasenadi, meeting her by chance, made her his queen. She became one of the Buddha's most devoted followers. In one sutta she tells the king that no one is dearer to her than herself, and asks him; he has to say the same of himself; in another, when the king is disappointed that she has borne a daughter, the Buddha tells him a woman may prove better than a man.

The commentary tells of one fault. In the bath-house she let her dog behave improperly with her, and the king saw it through a window. When he spoke of it, she told him the room had a trick to it: whoever went in would look doubled to one outside. She sent him in, and claimed to see him with a she-goat. He believed her.

When she was dying, she did not think of her great gifts, but of this deed and the lie that covered it, and she was reborn in the Avīci hell for seven days. The grieving king went to the Buddha meaning to ask where she was reborn. Each day the Buddha turned the talk so that he forgot to ask: had he heard she was in hell, the commentary says, he would have decided that giving is useless and stopped his daily alms to five hundred monks. On the eighth day she was reborn in the Tusita heaven, and the Buddha told the king.

On the eighth day the Buddha came to the palace on alms round and sat in the chariot hall. Whose chariot is this, he asked, and this? The king's grandfather's, his father's, his own. Then the verse: even the splendid chariots of kings wear out, and so does the body; but the Dhamma of the good does not grow old.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

The other face of death-proximate kamma: one unwholesome last thought outweighed a good life, but only for a while. Her stored good kamma then took its turn (“Kamma, death and rebirth”).

Read with: Kamma, death and rebirth · Thirty-one planes

The dhammas it shows

  • worry kukkucca: remorse over a hidden deed at the hour of death
  • object ārammaṇa: the remembered deed becomes the dying mind's object
  • kamma kamma: stored good kamma takes its turn after seven days

Where it is told

  • Dhammapada v. 151, with its commentary (Buddhist Legends XI.6)
  • SN 3.8 Mallikā Sutta (dearer than oneself)
  • SN 3.16 (the daughter)

Translators and editors have noted that some references to Mallikā in later commentaries point to the wrong story or confuse her with another Mallikā, the wife of the general Bandhula. The version here follows the Dhammapada commentary on v. 151.
