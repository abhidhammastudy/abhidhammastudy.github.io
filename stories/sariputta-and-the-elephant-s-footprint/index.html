Sāriputta and the elephant's footprint

Sāriputta told the monks that as every creature's footprint fits inside an elephant's, every wholesome teaching fits inside the four noble truths. Then he took one moment of seeing and read it again and again: as elements, as five aggregates, as dependent origination, and as the truths.

Where
Jetavana, Anāthapiṇḍika's park at Sāvatthī
Who
  • Sāriputta, the chief disciple, foremost in wisdom
  • The monks, whom he addresses as friends
Source
MN 28

The story

He began with the picture. Of all the animals that walk the earth, the elephant leaves the largest print, and every other print fits inside it. In the same way every wholesome state is included in the four noble truths. He took the first truth, and in it the five aggregates affected by clinging, and began with matter: the four elements in the body and outside it. Even the great earth element outside, vast as it is, he said, is seen to be impermanent, liable to destruction and change; what then of this body, held together for a short while by craving? None of it is mine, none of it is I, none of it is my self.

He showed what follows for practice. If others abuse a monk, strike him or wound him, the monk understands: a painful feeling born of ear-contact has arisen in me, and it is dependent, not independent; dependent on contact. He sees that contact is impermanent, and feeling, and perception, and formations, and consciousness, and his mind settles in what it is attending to.

Then he turned to the eye. If the eye within is intact, but no forms come into its range and there is no matching engagement of the mind, no eye-consciousness appears. If forms come into range but there is no engagement, still none appears. Only when all three meet does the matching kind of consciousness come about. And in what has come to be thus, the form belongs to the aggregate of form, the feeling to the aggregate of feeling, and so with perception, formations and consciousness.

Last he laid the same moment on the widest lens. The Blessed One has said, Sāriputta reminded them, that one who sees dependent origination sees the Dhamma, and one who sees the Dhamma sees dependent origination. These five aggregates are dependently arisen. Desire and clinging for them is the origin of suffering; removing desire for them is its cessation. He went through every door the same way, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind, and the monks were delighted.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Sāriputta does in a few lines what “One moment, every lens” does with the mango: one moment of seeing, read as matter, as five aggregates, as dependent origination and as the truths. Each reading is complete in its own terms, and none of them needs a self to finish the account.

Read with: The four noble truths · One moment, every lens

The dhammas it shows

  • eye-sensitivity cakkhu: the eye within, intact: the first of three that must meet
  • visible form vaṇṇa: forms that come into the eye's range
  • attention manasikāra: the matching engagement, without which nothing is seen

Where it is told

  • MN 28 Mahāhatthipadopama Sutta
  • MN 27 Cūḷahatthipadopama Sutta, the elephant's footprint read another way

The saying Sāriputta quotes, that whoever sees dependent origination sees the Dhamma, is given as the Buddha's own; this sutta is its best-known source. The sutta is one of the few long teachings in the Majjhima given by a disciple rather than the Buddha.

Opening question

If every wholesome teaching fits inside the four truths, what is the use of reading one moment through the other lenses?
