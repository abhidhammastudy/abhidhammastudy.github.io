Subha and the dog

The commentary tells that the miser Todeyya was reborn as a dog in his own house, and showed his son where he had buried his treasure. Subha then came to the Buddha with a question: why are some people long-lived and others short-lived, some rich and others poor? Beings, the Buddha said, are the owners and heirs of their deeds.

Where
Tudigāma near Sāvatthī, and Jetavana
Who
  • Subha, a young brahmin, son of Todeyya
  • Todeyya, his father, a rich and miserly brahmin, reborn as a dog in his own house
  • The Buddha, on his alms round
Source
MN 135, with the Majjhima commentary

The story

Todeyya, the commentary says, was King Pasenadi's chaplain and lord of Tudigāma near Sāvatthī, very rich and very mean. He taught his son that wealth is kept the way ants build an anthill and bees gather honey, little by little, and lost the way a pot of eye-black is used up. He gave nothing, and when he died he was reborn as a dog in his own house, where Subha grew fond of it.

Seeing at dawn that this would bring Subha to the refuges, the Buddha came to the house on his alms round, and the dog ran out barking. The Buddha addressed it by its former name, and it crept away and lay down in the ashes by the hearth. Subha, who believed his father had been reborn in the Brahmā world, went angrily to the monastery to accuse the Buddha of lying. Ask the dog, said the Buddha, to show you the treasure your father hid. Subha did, and the dog went to the spot and scratched at it, and the treasure was found.

In the sutta Subha comes to the Buddha with fourteen questions in seven pairs. Why are some human beings short-lived and some long-lived, some sickly and some healthy, some ugly and some beautiful, some without influence and some influential, some poor and some rich, some low-born and some high-born, some unwise and some wise? Beings are owners of their kamma, the Buddha answers, heirs of their kamma; kamma is what divides them into inferior and superior.

Then he takes each pair. One who kills, if he comes back as a human, is short-lived; one who harms beings is sickly; one who is angry and irritable is ugly; one who is envious has little influence; one who does not give is poor; one who is obstinate and proud is low-born; one who never asks the wise what is good is unwise. The opposite deeds give the opposite lives. Subha went for refuge.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

“From kamma to its results” in seven pairs: one kind of deed gives the rebirth, and the same deed, ripening within a later human life, shapes its length, health, looks and wealth.

Read with: From kamma to its results · Thirty-one planes

The dhammas it shows

  • avarice macchariya: a miser's avarice, ripening as a dog's birth
  • envy issā: envy ripens as a life without influence
  • hatred dosa: anger and irritability ripen as ugliness
  • kamma kamma: beings are owners and heirs of their kamma

Where it is told

  • MN 135 Cūḷakammavibhaṅga Sutta
  • Majjhima commentary on MN 135 (Todeyya and the dog)
  • MN 99 Subha Sutta (another conversation with the same young brahmin)

The dog and the treasure come from the commentary; the sutta begins with Subha's questions. Each answer in the sutta first names the bad destination, and only then the human life that follows when the deed ripens more lightly.
