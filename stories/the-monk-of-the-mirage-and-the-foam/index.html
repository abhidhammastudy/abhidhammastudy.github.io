The monk of the mirage and the foam

A monk who could not reach arahantship was on his way to ask the Buddha for a sharper subject when he watched a mirage, solid from far off and nothing up close, and took his own body to be like it. Resting by the river, he watched great clots of foam rise and burst, and saw that the body too arises and breaks. The Buddha spoke the verse of foam and mirage to him, and he was freed.

Where
The road to Sāvatthī in the hot season, and the bank of the Aciravatī
Who
  • A monk, unnamed, who meditated on a mirage
  • The Buddha, in his Fragrant Cottage at Sāvatthī
Source
Dhammapada v. 46, with its commentary; SN 22.95

The story

The Dhammapada commentary tells it. A monk took a meditation subject from the Buddha and went into the forest to practise. He strove hard, but could not reach arahantship, and he set out to go back and ask for a subject more exactly fitted to him.

On the way, in the hot season, he saw a mirage. From a distance it looked like something with a shape; as he came closer there was nothing there. This body too, he thought, is like that, in its arising and vanishing, and he went on, taking the mirage as his subject.

Tired from the road, he bathed in the Aciravatī and sat in the shade of a tree on the bank where the current ran fast. The rushing water threw up great masses of foam, and he watched them break one after another. This body too, he thought, is just like that: it arises and it breaks. He took that as his object.

The Buddha, standing in his Fragrant Cottage, saw him and said: just so, monk; this body is like a lump of foam, like a mirage, of a nature to arise and break up. Then he spoke the verse: knowing this body to be like foam, awakening to its mirage nature, cut off Māra's flowers and go where the king of death cannot see you. As the verse ended, the commentary says, the monk became an arahant with the analytical knowledges, and came on praising the Teacher.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Two readings of one image, and the site keeps both. The canon's foam and mirage picture the third mark as the Manual words it, non-self in the sense of corelessness (CMA IX §32); the commentary's foam is rise and fall, a nature image turned into the meditation object, as with Paṭācārā's water and Kisāgotamī's lamp. Linking the story to the knowledge of rise and fall is the site's reading; the commentary does not name the knowledge. As with those stories, the commentary speaks of “this body”, a whole individual; the Abhidhamma's rise and fall is of momentary formations, mind and matter arising and ceasing (“Ten knowledges, one by one”).

The dhammas it shows

  • impermanence aniccatā: foam rising and breaking on the current
  • perception saññā: in SN 22.95 the mirage stands for perception
  • wisdom paññindriya: seeing the body as foam and mirage

Where it is told

  • Dhammapada v. 46 (dhp46:1–4)
  • Dhammapada commentary on v. 46 (story IV.2, Marīcikammaṭṭhānikattheravatthu)
  • SN 22.95, the lump of foam: form like foam, perception like a mirage (sn22.95:2.1, 4.1)
  • Dhammapada v. 170, with its commentary (story XIII.3): monks watching bubbles burst, a near twin

The narrative is the commentary's; the canon has the verse, and separately the foam and the mirage of SN 22.95. In the sutta both images stand for emptiness of core: looked at closely, a lump of foam on the Ganges is hollow and void, and so is any form; a mirage at noon is the same, and so is any perception. Seeing this, the disciple grows disenchanted with all five aggregates and is freed. The commentary reads the images by time instead: the mirage shows the body's arising and vanishing, the foam its arising and breaking up. In its gloss on the verse, the mirage is empty and ungraspable up close, present only for a brief moment.

Opening question

Foam is hollow, and foam bursts. Which of these two is the lesson, or are they one?
