The monkey king's bridge

To save his troop from archers, a monkey king stretched himself across a river as a living bridge. The troop crossed over his back, but a jealous monkey leapt down on him, and his heart burst. The king of Benares, moved, tended him as he died.

Where
A great mango tree on the bank of the Ganges, in the Himalaya
Who
  • The monkey king, the Bodhisatta, leader of eighty thousand monkeys
  • The king of Benares, Ānanda in a former life
  • The jealous monkey, Devadatta in a former life
Source
Ja 407

The story

The Buddha told this story when the monks praised him for working for his kinsmen's good. The monkey king lived with his troop in a mango tree whose branches spread over the Ganges. Knowing the danger, he had every fruit on the branches over the water eaten while still small. One mango, hidden by an ant's nest, ripened and fell into the river, and floated down to Benares, where it was caught in the king's bathing nets.

The king tasted it, and longed for more. He went upstream with his men, found the tree and the monkeys eating, and surrounded it with archers to shoot them at dawn. The monkeys, trapped, came trembling to their king.

He leapt to the far bank, cut a long cane, tied one end to a tree there and the other round his waist, and leapt back; but he had forgotten the length round his waist, and fell short by the length of his own body. So he caught a branch of the mango tree with both hands, made himself the last stretch of the bridge, and told the troop to cross. Eighty thousand monkeys crossed over his back. Last came one who had always hated him, and leapt down hard on his back, and his heart broke.

The king of Benares had watched it all. He had the monkey king lowered gently, bathed, robed and laid on a couch, and asked him why he had given his life for them. A king, the monkey answered, should be like this: he should seek the welfare of all who depend on him, even at the cost of his own life. Then he died, and the king honoured him as a king.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Compassion (karuṇākaruṇā · compassion Compassion is the wish that suffering be removed from others.) is one of the variable beautiful factors in “An ensemble of fifty-two”. It joins a wholesome citta only now and then, when the suffering of others is the object. The Jātaka's own lesson is for rulers: one who leads should seek the good of all who depend on him.

Read with: An ensemble of fifty-two · Forty meditation subjects

The dhammas it shows

  • compassion karuṇā: he gives his body for his suffering troop
  • envy issā: the jealous monkey who leapt on his back

Where it is told

  • Ja 407 Mahākapi Jātaka
  • Carved at the stūpa of Bhārhut, 2nd century BCE

The story is among the earliest Jātakas to be carved in stone: a medallion at Bhārhut shows the bridge, the archers and the king's men holding out a cloth.
