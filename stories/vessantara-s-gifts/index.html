Jātaka · Level 7

Vessantara's gifts

In the last human life before the final one, the Bodhisatta as Prince Vessantara gave away the kingdom's white elephant, his wealth, and in the end, in tears and for the sake of awakening, his children and his wife. After that life he was reborn in the Tusita heaven.

Where
Jetuttara, capital of the Sivi kingdom, and Mount Vaṅka
Who
  • Prince Vessantara, the Bodhisatta
  • Maddī, his wife; Yasodharā, Rāhula's mother, in a former life
  • Jāli and Kaṇhājinā, their children; Rāhula and Uppalavaṇṇā
  • Jūjaka, an old brahmin; Devadatta
Source
Ja 547

The story

The Buddha told this story on his first visit home to Kapilavatthu, when a rain fell that wet only those who wished to be wet: such a rain had fallen once before, he said. Vessantara, son of King Sañjaya of the Sivis, had loved giving since birth. When brahmins came from drought-stricken Kaliṅga to ask for the kingdom's white elephant, whose presence brought rain, he gave it.

The people were furious and demanded his banishment. Before leaving he gave the great gift of seven hundreds: elephants, horses, chariots, women, cows, slaves and wealth. Maddī his wife would not stay behind, and went with their two small children. On the road he gave away the horses and the chariot, and they walked to a hermitage on Mount Vaṅka.

An old brahmin, Jūjaka, came there. His young wife had been mocked by the village women for drawing water herself, and sent him to get her servants. While Maddī was out gathering fruit, held back by devas in the form of wild beasts, Jūjaka asked for the children, and Vessantara gave them. The children hid in a pond and had to be called out. They clung to his feet weeping, and his own tears fell on them. Before he gave them he set a price on each so that they could buy their freedom: a thousand pieces of gold for Jāli, and for Kaṇhājinā a hundred of everything, so that no low-born man could buy her. Then he poured the water of gift, wishing that it be a condition for a Buddha's omniscience, which he said was a hundred thousand times dearer to him than his children. When Maddī came home she searched all night and fell unconscious. Then Sakka came, disguised as a brahmin, and asked for Maddī, and Vessantara gave her too. Sakka revealed himself, gave her back, and granted him boons.

Led by the devas, Jūjaka took the children to the Sivi capital, where their grandfather ransomed them; Jūjaka ate so much at the feast that he died. The king and the people went to Mount Vaṅka, the family was reunited, and Vessantara was brought home as king. The earth shook, and a rain of jewels fell.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Sense-sphere wholesome kamma at its fullest (“Kamma, death and rebirth”). The tradition counts it as the completion of the perfection of giving, made for the sake of a Buddha's awakening; the Jātaka tells the gift of the children as the hardest of all, made in tears and with their ransom already set.

Read with: Kamma, death and rebirth · Thirty-one planes

The dhammas it shows

  • non-greed alobha: giving away elephant, wealth, children and wife
  • volition cetanā: the volition of giving, made with the wish that it lead to omniscience
  • greed lobha: Jūjaka eats at the feast until he dies

Where it is told

  • Ja 547 Vessantara Jātaka
  • Cariyāpiṭaka 1.9
  • Milindapañha, the question on Vessantara (Vessantarapañha)
  • Margaret Cone and Richard Gombrich, The Perfect Generosity of Prince Vessantara (1977)

It is the longest and best-loved of the Jātakas, recited over a day and a night at the Thet Mahachat festival in Thailand and Laos. Buddhists have argued about it for centuries, above all over the gift of the children and the wife, and the tradition itself puts those doubts into the mouths of its characters; the Milindapañha argues them out in its question on Vessantara.
