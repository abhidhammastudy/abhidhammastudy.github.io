Why Moggallāna was beaten

Late in life the great disciple Mahā Moggallāna was beaten to death by hired bandits. The commentary traces this to a deed many lives before, when he turned on his own blind parents; the Dhammapada commentary says he killed them, the Jātaka commentary that he stopped.

Where
Black Rock on the slope of Isigili, near Rājagaha
Who
  • Mahā Moggallāna, the chief disciple foremost in psychic power, now old
  • The Nigaṇṭhas, naked ascetics, rivals who hired his killers
Source
Dhammapada commentary, vv. 137–140

The story

Moggallāna used to visit the heavens and the hells and come back to tell people what he had seen: followers of the Buddha enjoying the fruit of their deeds, followers of other teachers suffering. The naked ascetics, the commentary says, saw their support draining away, and paid bandits a thousand coins to kill him.

The bandits surrounded his dwelling at Black Rock. He knew, and slipped out through the keyhole. They came back another time, and he rose through the roof into the sky. The third time he did not escape. He saw that an old deed was waiting for its chance, and let them take him. They beat him until his bones were broken like grains of rice, and left him for dead.

He held himself together by the power of jhāna, went to the Buddha to take his leave, and returned to Black Rock to attain final Nibbāna. When the monks asked why such a death had come to such a disciple, the Buddha told of a past life. Moggallāna had been a devoted son to blind parents; his wife resented caring for them and turned him against them. He took them into the forest by cart, pretended that bandits were attacking, and beat them himself.

Here the versions part. In the Dhammapada commentary the parents, thinking robbers were attacking, cried out to their son to save himself, and he beat them to death all the same; he burned in hell for many hundreds of thousands of years, and in a hundred lives since had been beaten to death in the same way. In the Jātaka commentary's telling their cry touched him; he stopped, comforted them and took them home, and the deed waited for its chance like embers under ash. In both, this life was its last. King Ajātasattu had the killers found and executed, and with them the naked ascetics who had hired them. The verses spoken were Dhammapada 137–140: whoever harms the harmless meets one of ten misfortunes.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Kamma that can ripen “in any later life” (“Kamma, death and rebirth”) waits for its chance, even for an arahant; so the Jātaka telling says. In the Dhammapada telling the deed was the killing of his parents, a heinous deed that ripened first in hell; what met him at the end was its remainder. Either way, what arahantship ends is new kamma and any further rebirth.

Read with: Kamma, death and rebirth

The dhammas it shows

  • kamma kamma: a deed from many lives ago finds its chance
  • hatred dosa: turning on his own blind parents
  • envy issā: rival ascetics, envious of his support, hire killers

Where it is told

  • Dhammapada vv. 137–140, with their commentary (Buddhist Legends X.7)
  • Jātaka 522 Sarabhaṅga, introduction (a parallel telling)
  • Therāpadāna, Mahāmoggallāna: the past deed and its fruit, in his own verses

The death scene comes from the commentaries, which give it in more than one form, with differences in the timing and in the past-life deed. The canon has only the bare bones: in his verses in the Apadāna he says that he once killed his mother and father, that in every birth since he has died with his head broken, and that it will be so in this last life too.
