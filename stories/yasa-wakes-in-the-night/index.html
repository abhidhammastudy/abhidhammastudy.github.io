Yasa wakes in the night

Yasa woke in the night in his pleasure palace and saw his musicians asleep around him, sprawled with their instruments, drooling and muttering, like a charnel ground. The danger struck him, his mind settled in disenchantment, and he walked out of the city in the dark crying that it was oppressive. At the Deer Park the Buddha told him that here nothing was oppressive, and taught him; he entered the stream, and soon after was freed.

Where
A rich house in Bārāṇasī, and the Deer Park at Isipatana before dawn
Who
  • Yasa, the delicately raised son of a rich merchant of Bārāṇasī
  • His father, the merchant, who came looking for him
  • His mother and his former wife, the first women to go for the three refuges
  • The Buddha, at the Deer Park, where the first five disciples had just become arahants
Source
Vinaya, Mahāvagga I.7–8

The story

Yasa was the son of a rich merchant of Bārāṇasī, raised in great comfort. He had three palaces, one for the cold season, one for the hot and one for the rains. Through the four months of the rains he stayed in the rains palace, entertained by women musicians, and never came downstairs.

One night he fell asleep early, and his attendants too, while an oil lamp burned all night. He woke first and saw them: one with a lute in her armpit, one with a drum at her neck, one with a tambourine, hair loose, saliva running, talking in their sleep. It seemed to him a charnel ground. The text says that danger appeared to him and his mind settled in disenchantment, and he cried out: how oppressive it is, how afflicting. He put on his golden slippers and went to the door of the house, and then to the city gate; non-human beings opened both, so that no one would stand in the way of his going forth. He walked on to the Deer Park at Isipatana.

The Buddha had risen before dawn and was walking up and down in the open. He saw Yasa coming, sat down, and heard him cry out again. Here, Yasa, he said, nothing is oppressive, nothing afflicting; come, sit, I will teach you. Glad, Yasa took off his slippers and sat. The Buddha gave him the graduated talk: on giving, on virtue, on the heavens, on the danger and the degradation of sense pleasures and the blessing of renunciation. When Yasa's mind was ready, soft, free of hindrances, uplifted and confident, he taught the four truths, and as a clean cloth takes the dye, the Dhamma-eye arose in him: whatever is subject to arising is all subject to ceasing.

His mother went up to the palace, could not find him, and went to the merchant: your son Yasa is nowhere to be seen. The father sent horsemen in four directions and went himself to the Deer Park, following the tracks of the golden slippers. The Buddha saw him coming from far off, as he had seen the son, and used his power so that the father, sitting there, could not see his son, and taught him in turn. The father entered the stream and went for refuge, the first lay follower to take the three refuges. While the Buddha was teaching his father, Yasa reviewed the ground he had seen and known, and his mind was freed from the taints without clinging. The Buddha withdrew his power. Your mother is overcome with grief, said the father; give her back her life. Yasa looked at the Buddha, and the Buddha explained that one freed in this way can no longer go back to the household life of pleasures. Yasa asked to go forth, and the Vinaya counts seven arahants in the world at that time. His mother and his former wife entered the stream when the Buddha came to the house for the meal that morning, and his friends, four and then fifty, went forth after him.

A reading · a study aid, not the tradition's own reading

Danger and disenchantment here are plain words, not yet the knowledges of “Ten knowledges, one by one”: Yasa was weary of a house of pleasure, not yet of all formations. The site links the story to danger, disenchantment and the wish to be free as an image of what those knowledges feel like, not as the knowledges themselves. The canonical sequence after it fits the Manual's: the Dhamma-eye at the four truths, which the commentaries take as the path of stream-entry (CMA IX §34), and release while “reviewing the ground he had seen and known”. The Visuddhimagga says a stream-enterer who has reviewed goes on to the next path “on that same seat or later” by turning insight on formations again (Vism XXII §813). A Vinaya sub-commentary takes Yasa's “reviewing” as seeing the same truths again with the three higher paths (Vimativinodanī on Mv I.7, CS §28), so the site does not mark it as reviewing knowledge.

The dhammas it shows

  • decisive support upanissaya: the commentary: foulness practised in a past life was the decisive support
  • path magga: the Dhamma-eye arose at the four truths

Where it is told

  • Vinaya, Mahāvagga I.7–8 (pli-tv-kd1:7.1.1–8.4.2): the night, the Deer Park, the father, the mother and wife
  • Vinaya, Mahāvagga I.9–10 (pli-tv-kd1:9.1.1–10.4.8): his four friends and his fifty friends
  • Vinaya commentary (Samantapāsādikā), Mahāvagga, Pabbajjākathā, CS §31: the past life at the charnel ground
  • Sāratthadīpanī (Vinaya sub-commentary), on Mahāvagga I.7, CS §§25–27 (the Buddha's “here” as Nibbāna; why the father was kept from seeing him)
  • Vajirabuddhi-ṭīkā (Vinaya sub-commentary), Mahāvagga, Pabbajjākathā, CS §26: he had seen only the human world as oppressive
  • Vimativinodanī (Vinaya sub-commentary), on Mahāvagga I.7, CS §28: the reviewing as the three higher paths
  • Mahāsi Sayadaw, Satipaṭṭhāna Vipassanā: Criticisms and Replies (modern; Yasa among those who reached the path while listening)

This is canonical prose, and the charnel-ground picture is the text's own. Its words for what Yasa felt, danger (ādīnava) and disenchantment (nibbidā), are the names of two insight knowledges in the Visuddhimagga and the Manual, but here they are used in the sense of the suttas: the danger in sense pleasures, and weariness of them. The Vinaya commentary explains the scene by foulness, not by insight. In a past life, it says, Yasa was one of fifty-five friends who cremated the bodies of the poor; burning the corpse of a pregnant woman, he gained the perception of foulness and taught it to his friends and family. That old practice is why the sleeping women looked like a charnel ground to him, and the support for all their attainments. A sub-commentary adds that “here” in the Buddha's greeting meant Nibbāna. Another, the Vajirabuddhi, says Yasa had seen the oppression of the human world only, not of the heavens, and that this is why the Buddha began with the graduated talk. In modern Burma, Mahāsi Sayadaw cites Yasa and his family, after Upāli and King Bimbisāra, among those who reached the path while listening, without jhāna. On his reading, the mind made ready and free of hindrances by the graduated talk was their purification of mind, and the knowledges in between can be inferred, since the start and the end are both in the text, as a deer's path is read from its tracks. That inference is a modern teacher's, argued first for Upāli.
